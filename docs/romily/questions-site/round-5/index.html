The start of the journey

You're right that asking for location straight away could put people off before they know what they're getting.

The postcode is also what brings up the map, the moment people see it's about their real place. One idea is to show something straight away, before asking anything personal.

It has to make someone curious without sounding like a campaign, and without promising more than the site can show. The name already asks a question, so the line can answer it or sharpen it.

Layers

Whichever way it goes, the whole page will still be there for anyone who wants it, and every step will have its own link so it can be shared.

Once someone has told us where they live, "What's coming up" would show only their own elections. Today, anyone without one gets a list of other places' by-elections, which is the wordy bit.

Candidates and positions

Whatever we choose, every candidate is treated identically.

A face tends to draw the eye more than a grey circle with initials, so candidates with a photo may get more attention just for having one.

Topic first means: pick "Housing", see what every candidate has published on it, side by side, in ballot-paper order. Candidate first means: pick a candidate, see everything they've published. The detail and sources stay one tap away either way.

For example, a renter sees Housing first; a household with children at school sees Education first. This orders topics, never candidates, and uses only the profile, never anyone's politics. The other side: two people see the site differently, and someone might say we're steering what they look at.

What's happening around you

This is doable. Councils publish their meeting agendas and decisions online (many through a common system called Modern.gov), and Democracy Club's ElectionLeaflets.org archives the leaflets candidates put through doors, which is often the only place a local candidate says anything about a local issue.

Whoever picks the issues shapes the election: an area where we highlight potholes feels different from one where we highlight housing. The options below go from "a fixed rule, no choosing by us" to "we choose, but publish how".

Proposed: "We haven't found anything [name] has published on this. That doesn't mean they have no view on it. If you're the candidate, send us a link and we'll add it."

Zooming in and out

The map already zooms, so it can become the control: street, then ward and council, then region, then the whole country, with the page changing at each level.

In parts of England it's a combined-authority mayor. In other parts of England there is nothing between the county council and Westminster. In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland it's their own parliament or assembly, which runs things like the NHS and schools there, so it matters far more than "regional" suggests.

The honest version is the UK's decisions abroad: what the parties have published on things like foreign affairs, trade, aid and climate, which are already topics on the site. It would be the one level with no election or representative of its own.

Track record

We agree it needs proper research before anything is built. The first pass is done: what the evidence allows (five minutes to read, every source linked). It also covers the local-issues sources for question 12. In short: for MPs the facts exist, every Commons vote is recorded by name; these questions are about where it gets harder.

Newcomers and many independents would have an empty section. Depending on how it looks, that could make incumbents seem more accountable, or more exposed, just because the section exists.

Council decisions are usually by show of hands, with names recorded only when a vote is formally requested. A councillor's record could be patchy through no choice of their own, which is hard to present fairly next to an MP's complete one.
