4. Councillors: named votes, and what else we can fairly show

Date: 24 September 2026

Question: When must councils record how each councillor voted, how often do they do it, is there any dataset of those votes, and what else could we fairly show for a councillor?

Summary 1. In England, the law has required councils to record every councillor's name on budget and council tax votes since 25 February 2014. Otherwise, a named vote usually only happens if councillors ask for one. We found no rule like the budget one in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland. 2. Practice varies a lot. In our sample of 20 councils (a sample, not a survey), some record names on most contested votes (Cardiff, Highland, Aberdeen, Fife, Kent). Many record names only on the budget or when asked. We found no dataset of council votes anywhere in the UK. 3. We can collect attendance and committee membership from the Modern.gov web service. The register of interests and allowances must be published by law, but mostly as web pages or PDFs. Each of these has fairness problems, and we will need to explain them on the page.

(a) What the law says

England: the budget rule (SI 2014/165)

Checked on legislation.gov.uk, 24 September 2026.

Point What the law says Source
What it is The Local Authorities (Standing Orders) (England) (Amendment) Regulations 2014. It changes the Local Authorities (Standing Orders) (England) Regulations 2001. legislation.gov.uk, SI 2014/165 as made
Since when Made 29 January 2014. In force from 25 February 2014. same
The rule (exact words) "Immediately after any vote is taken at a budget decision meeting of an authority there must be recorded in the minutes of the proceedings of that meeting the names of the persons who cast a vote for the decision or against the decision or who abstained from voting." same, reg. 2(3)
Which decisions A "budget decision meeting" is a meeting where the council makes the council tax calculations under the Local Government Finance Act 1992, or issues a precept (a county council asking district councils to collect its share of council tax). It includes any meeting where either item is on the agenda. "Vote" means a vote on any decision "related to" that calculation or precept. same
Amendments too? Yes. The government's note says the rule covers "the substantive budget motion ... and any amendments proposed at the budget meeting". Explanatory Memorandum to SI 2014/165, DCLG, 2014, para 8.2
Which councils The rule sits in standing orders that every "relevant authority" must adopt. A relevant authority means "a county council, a district council or a London borough council". Unitary and metropolitan councils are legally county or district councils, so they are covered. The rule is written for mayor-and-cabinet, leader-and-cabinet and committee-system councils alike. 2001 Regulations, reg. 2; SI 2014/165 reg. 2
What is recorded Names of councillors voting for, against, and abstaining. Absent councillors are not mentioned, although some councils list them anyway. SI 2014/165
Why "With recorded votes, electors and local tax payers will be able to see how those who represent them voted on the issue of the local tax." Explanatory Memorandum, para 7.1

In plain words: at the yearly budget and council tax meeting, every English council must write down in its minutes how each councillor voted, including on amendments.

England and Wales: any councillor can have their own vote recorded

This is an older rule, and many people don't know about it.

  • England: the Local Authorities (Standing Orders) Regulations 1993 make councils include this in their standing orders: "Where immediately after a vote is taken at a meeting of a relevant body any member of that body so requires, there shall be recorded in the minutes ... whether that person cast his vote for the question or against the question or whether he abstained from voting." It applies to the full council, committees, sub-committees and joint committees. legislation.gov.uk, SI 1993/202, Sch. 2 para 1. The 2001 Regulations removed other parts of the 1993 Regulations for England, but not this one (SI 2001/3384 reg. 8). legislation.gov.uk warns that some changes to the 1993 text have not yet been applied, so "still in force in England" is our reading, not something we could fully confirm.
  • This records one person's vote, not everyone's. A councillor can only have their own vote recorded.
  • Wales: the same rule appears, with almost the same words, in the Local Authorities (Standing Orders) (Wales) Regulations 2006 (in force 3 July 2006). It covers county and county borough councils. legislation.gov.uk, WSI 2006/1275, Sch. 2 para 1

"A set number of members can demand a recorded vote"

We found no national law that sets this number. Each council picks its own number in its constitution. Examples we read are in the table below: 1, 2, 6 and 10 councillors. A named vote of the whole meeting (a "recorded vote" or "roll call") depends entirely on these local rules. The one exception is England's budget rule.

The only national rule on recording names is about attendance, not votes: - England and Wales: "The names of the members present at a meeting of a local authority shall be recorded." Local Government Act 1972, Sch. 12 para 40 - Scotland: "The names of the members present at a meeting of a council shall be recorded." Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973, Sch. 7 para 6

Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland

Nation Budget votes named by law? Other national rules Source
Wales We found none. The 2006 Regulations contain nothing about budgets. Any member can have their own vote recorded. Principal councils must also broadcast their meetings, so video is a second source. WSI 2006/1275; Local Government and Elections (Wales) Act 2021, s.46
Scotland We found none. Decisions are made by a majority of members "present and voting". Names of those present must be recorded. Each council's standing orders decide how votes are taken. Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973, Sch. 7
Northern Ireland We found none. Qualified majority means 80% of the votes of members present and voting (s.40). Call-in: if 15% of the council's members ask, the council must reconsider a decision. The two grounds are that it was not properly considered, or that it would "disproportionately affect adversely any section of the inhabitants of the district" (s.41). Standing orders must list which decisions need a qualified majority. The draft Standing Orders Regulations require one for decisions on a call-in made on the second ("disproportionately affect") ground, and for suspending standing orders (with some exceptions). We found no national rule on recording named votes. Local Government Act (Northern Ireland) 2014, Part 7; draft Local Government (Standing Orders) Regulations (NI) 2015

Patterns (from the sample only): - England: named votes on the budget are guaranteed. Other votes depend on local rules, where the number of councillors needed to ask ranges from 1 (for your own vote only) to 10. Kent and Hartlepool record names more widely. - Scotland: roll-call and electronic voting are common, and three of the four readable councils publish names routinely. Glasgow is the exception: it keeps the names but did not publish them in the 2022 minutes. - Wales and Northern Ireland: names are mostly recorded on request, often by just one member. Cardiff published names on every vote in the minutes we checked. - Electronic voting systems usually capture each person's vote. Whether the council publishes that record is a choice. Oldham's system, for example, captures votes but does not keep them unless someone asks.

Data quality warning, with a real example. In Southwark's budget minutes (21 Feb 2024), the heading says "In favour of the substantive motion (48)", but the list under it has 50 names. Two of those 50 names also appear in another list: one under "Absent" and one under "Abstained". Southwark item, 21 Feb 2024. A program that reads minutes automatically would copy mistakes like this and could state something false about a real person.

(d) What else could fairly be shown for a councillor

Item Required by law? Machine-readable? Fairness problems
Attendance England, Wales and Scotland: names of those present must be recorded (LGA 1972 Sch. 12 para 40; LG(S)A 1973 Sch. 7 para 6). No law requires attendance statistics. Yes, per meeting. Modern.gov GetMeeting gives each attendee with party, ward and a status such as "Present, as expected", "Apologies", "Absent", "In attendance" or "Not required" (Brighton and Cardiff XML, 24 Sep 2026). There is also an HTML summary page, for example Kent mgAttendance.aspx, showing "Total expected attendances", "Present as expected", "Apologies received" and a percentage. There is no attendance method in the API. A low figure can come from illness, pregnancy, caring duties or bereavement. Apologies and unexplained absence look the same in a percentage. New councillors have few meetings, so one absence changes the figure a lot. Chairs and cabinet members sit on more committees. Kent's page counts only meetings "in their capacity as member of that committee", so going to extra meetings voluntarily does not count. Councils record this differently, so figures from different councils can't fairly be compared.
Committee membership Councils decide who sits on which committee. No publication law was checked. Yes. GetCommitteesByUser and GetMeeting attendees (role, e.g. "Chair", "Committee Member"). Low risk. The party group usually decides who goes where, so membership is not purely the councillor's choice.
Register of interests England: yes. The monitoring officer "must establish and maintain a register of interests" and make sure it "is published on the authority's website" (Localism Act 2011, s.29). New members must notify within 28 days (s.30). Details can be withheld where disclosure could lead to "violence or intimidation" (s.32). Home addresses are left off the public register unless the member asks for them to be included (s.32A). The rules for Wales, Scotland and NI were not checked. No. Modern.gov links to an HTML page per councillor (mgRofI.aspx), and some councils use PDFs or scans. Modern.gov also has a "Declarations at meetings" page (mgListDeclarationsOfInterest.aspx), seen on Brighton. Some entries are withheld lawfully, and that must not look like hiding something. Registers can be out of date. Summarising them means choosing what counts as important, which is our judgement, so it is safer to link than to summarise.
Allowances paid England: yes. Councils must keep a record of each payment with the recipient's name. After each year, they must publish "the total sum paid ... to each recipient" for basic, special responsibility, dependants' carers', and travel and subsistence allowances, plus co-optees' allowance and (since 11 May 2026) the employer pension contributions paid for each recipient (SI 2003/1021 reg. 15). Wales: a statement of payments to each named member must be published by 30 September, but care costs are published only as a total for all members (Welsh Government, IRPW remuneration guidance, 28 Feb 2024). The rules for Scotland and NI were not checked. Usually PDF or HTML tables. A few councils may publish CSV (unverified). In England, dependants' carers' allowance is published by name, which reveals caring duties. A higher total usually reflects a senior role, not greed. Some councillors turn down allowances.
Motions proposed and questions asked Not required nationally. Some constitutions require it. For example, East Sussex attaches "Questions which have been asked, the names of the questioners and the answers given" to the council minutes (SO 44.6) (East Sussex constitution, 25 Mar 2026). No. Only in minutes text (PDF or HTML). No API method. Counting motions or questions rewards quantity, not quality. Cabinet members answer questions rather than ask them. Opposition members have more reason to ask. Any count would compare people in different roles.
Named votes See (a) See (c) The councillor may simply have been absent. A missing record can mean the council never records names, not that the person didn't vote. Group whipping (party orders on how to vote) is common.

What this means for the site

  1. The fairest named vote everywhere in England is the budget vote. Since 25 Feb 2014, every English council must record names on the budget and council tax vote and its amendments. For a sitting English councillor we could show "How they voted on the [year] budget and council tax", with a link to the minutes. This works the same way for every English council, so it doesn't favour anyone. Wales, Scotland and NI have no such guarantee.
  2. Show other named votes only where the council records them, and say why they are missing. For example: "This council records names only when six councillors ask" or "This council records names on every vote". A missing record must never read as a bad mark against the person.
  3. For now, votes would have to be entered by hand, with a source link for every one. There is no dataset. The web service has no votes. Minutes vary between PDF tables, HTML text and separate documents, and they contain errors (the Southwark example). For the Holborn and St Pancras pilot, Camden blocks automated access, so any Camden record would need manual checking. Scottish councils such as Aberdeen, Highland, Fife and Cardiff in Wales publish names more often, so automation could be tried there later.
  4. Attendance is available by machine, but how we present it is a judgement for Romily and Barny. Options include the raw numbers ("expected at X meetings, present at Y, apologies Z") with the date range and a short note on why people miss meetings, a percentage, or not showing attendance at all. Each choice affects how a person comes across. Whichever is chosen should be the same for every candidate.
  5. Link to the register of interests instead of summarising it. Explain that some entries are withheld by law for safety.
  6. Allowances: show the published totals, and think carefully about the carers' allowance line. Publishing it by name is lawful in England, but it reveals family circumstances. Leaving it out is a judgement call for the team.
  7. Candidates who are not councillors will have none of this. The page needs a neutral "No council record, because this candidate has not been a councillor" message so newcomers don't look worse than people already in office.
