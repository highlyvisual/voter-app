The opening line: what similar sites say, and what the evidence shows

Date: 24 September 2026 Question: Which of Romily's four opening lines should go on whatsittome.org? (For Barny to decide. This paper does not pick one.)

Summary 1. The neutral UK and overseas election tools we checked mostly open with a plain task ("Find out…", "Understand…", "Compare…"), not a slogan. Slogan-style lines turn up more in campaigning groups. 2. Research says personal relevance makes people pay more attention and weigh their own interests more. But it also shows that small wording changes rarely change what people actually do. 3. Of the four lines, only "Politics is Personal" has a well-known history: it flips a 1969–70 feminist slogan. We found no party using it. Two lines echo words that quiz sites already use ("where you stand", "match").

Patterns we see (our reading of the table, not a study): - The neutral lookup and record tools (Democracy Club, mySociety, Full Fact, Vote Compass) open with a verb and a task: find out, understand, compare. They say what the site does. - "You/your" is almost everywhere. Personal address is normal, not unusual. - Slogan-style lines ("Vote For Your Life", "You Already Vote, So Vote", "Your Vote is Your Voice") come mostly from registration drives and campaigning groups, whose job is to get people to act. They are less common on tools whose job is to inform. - "Where you stand" and "match" are the language of quiz sites that place you on a scale or match you to a party (Political Compass, iSideWith, Vote Compass). - We found no public evaluation of which tagline wording worked best from the UK, Australian or Irish commissions. Their published evaluations measure reach and awareness. Example: the 2024 voter ID ads got over 863 million impressions, and awareness of the ID rule rose to 87% after the election (Electoral Commission blog, 2024). Elections Canada's pages were not accessible to us.

2. What research says about lines that draw people in

Personal relevance makes people pay attention. In the "elaboration likelihood" model of persuasion, people think harder about a message when it affects them personally. The classic test told students that an exam change would or would not apply to them (Petty, Cacioppo & Goldman, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 41:847–855, Nov 1981; summary in Wikipedia, "Elaboration likelihood model", accessed 24 Sep 2026). We checked the reference but could not read the paper itself.

Self-interest counts more when the stakes are made clear, and a frame can change what people weigh. A national survey experiment found "people are more likely to recognize their own self-interest, and to act upon it, when their stakes in the policy are clear or when they have been primed to think about the personal costs and benefits." People with less at stake were swayed more by values and by appeals to the good of society (Chong, Citrin & Conley, Political Psychology 22:541–570, Sep 2001). This supports the site's idea. It also means a "your life" frame is not a blank frame: it nudges people towards weighing their own interests over wider ones. Researchers disagree about how much self-interest drives opinion in general. One view says its effect is limited (Sears & Funk, Journal of Behavioral Economics 19:247–271, 1990; title only checked). The other says it "remains a potent factor" (Weeden & Kurzban, Political Psychology 38:67–90, 2017).

Civic duty alone moved turnout a little. Social pressure moved it more. In a Michigan field experiment with about 180,000 households, turnout was 29.7% in the control group and 31.5% with a "DO YOUR CIVIC DUTY—VOTE!" mailer. It was 37.8% when neighbours' voting records were shown (Gerber, Green & Larimer, American Political Science Review 102:33–48, 2008, via Yale ISPS PDF). The site will not use social pressure. The point is that a duty message on its own had a small effect.

Small wording changes: an early big result did not hold up. One study found that asking people about "being a voter" (a noun) rather than "voting" (a verb) raised turnout by 11–14 points in small samples (e.g. 95.5% vs 81.8%) (Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck, PNAS 108:12653–12656, 2011). A large field experiment then found the change "has no effect on turnout" (Gerber, Huber, Biggers & Hendry, PNAS 113:7112–7117, 2016). A second large replication again found "no evidence that this minor wording change increases turnout" (Gerber, Huber & Fang, Political Psychology 39:925–938, 2018). So the evidence does not suggest that any one of the four lines will change behaviour much. The choice is more about clarity, fit and signals.

Tools like this seem to help, but less in the strictest tests. A review of 22 studies across 9 countries found using a voting advice tool linked to higher reported turnout. Effects were "substantively weaker in causally more rigorous experimental studies" (Munzert & Ramirez-Ruiz, Political Communication 38:691–706, 2021; preprint abstract, OSF, Oct 2020).

What UK voters said they wanted. Government user research on the 2015 general election (30 phone interviews, 44 diaries) found people meeting candidates for the first time in the polling booth: "There were lots of parties I hadn't heard of on the actual card." They valued matching tools because they were independent: "The quiz is independent." (GDS user research, Sep 2015)

3. Trust and seeming neutral

  • People who care strongly see neutral content as biased against them. Pro-Palestinian and pro-Israeli students at Stanford watched the same news clips (Wikipedia's wording). "Each group reported more negative references to their side than positive ones" (Vallone, Ross & Lepper, JPSP 49:577–585, 1985; description from Wikipedia, "Hostile media effect", accessed 24 Sep 2026). So any line will be read by some people as leaning.
  • Neutral messages can still have uneven effects. Elections Canada's turnout ads raised turnout among low-turnout groups and "are associated with an overall decrease in the Conservative party's vote share" (McGregor & Anderson, Canadian Journal of Political Science 47(4), 2014).
  • Neutral organisations say trust is their main asset. Democracy Club: "The Electoral Commission and UK Parliament are happy to direct visitors to our site because they know we can be trusted to avoid political bias" (Democracy Club, 17 Feb 2023). Full Fact: "Full Fact doesn't take sides" and was launched by "a cross-party group of trustees" (Full Fact, accessed 24 Sep 2026).
  • Perceived tilt has had real costs. Rock the Vote's 2014 video was "criticized for having a disproportionate representation of left-wing political issues" (Wikipedia, "Rock the Vote", accessed 24 Sep 2026). In Germany a court stopped the Wahl-O-Mat days before the 2019 European election. It showed only eight parties at a time, which the court said breached parties' equal chances (Legal Tribune Online, 20 May 2019). That case was about layout, not a tagline, but it shows the standard that neutral tools get held to.

4. "The personal is political" and "Politics is personal"

  • Origin. Carol Hanisch wrote the essay as a memo in February 1969 in Gainesville, Florida. It was published in 1970 in Notes from the Second Year: Women's Liberation. She says: "I did not give the paper its title… that was done by… editors Shulie Firestone and Anne Koedt" (Carol Hanisch, carolhanisch.org, introduction dated January 2006; also Wikipedia, accessed 24 Sep 2026; Britannica, accessed 24 Sep 2026). Britannica links it to second-wave feminism in the 1960s and 70s.
  • UK students meet it at school. It appears in A-level Politics study notes on feminism for the AQA and Edexcel exam boards (tutor2u, updated 6 Nov 2018). Many sixth-formers and graduates will spot the flip.
  • Did any party or campaign use "Politics is personal" as a slogan? In our searches we found no UK or overseas party, and no electoral campaign, using it as a slogan. We did find it as:
  • a headline by a progressive Canadian think tank arguing that, "rather than looking for personal validation from politicians", voters should back "decisions that reflect what's best for everyone in the long-term" (Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives, Erika Shaker, 30 May 2011). This uses the phrase in a different sense to the site (as a warning, not an invitation);
  • a chapter title in a climate novel (Andrew Dana Hudson, Medium, 7 Feb 2022);
  • an almost unused Bluesky handle (1 follower, 3 posts, created Nov 2024) (Bluesky public API, read 24 Sep 2026);
  • several blogs and newsletters we could not open (see "Could not verify").
  • Other everyday meanings. Writers use the phrase in different senses. The 2011 think-tank piece above uses it for voting on feelings, not policy. A newsletter titled "All Politics is Personal" uses it to mean that your own actions matter more than polls (Need to Know, Substack, undated). Some readers may also hear "politics has turned into personal attacks". That is our reading; we did not find a source for it. It also sounds like "All politics is local", a phrase tied to US Speaker Tip O'Neill, with variations dating back to 1932 (Wikipedia, accessed 24 Sep 2026).
  • Could it read as partisan? No party owns it. The possible signal is its link to feminism, which some readers tie to the political left. That is our inference, not a finding from any study.

5. Other prior uses and associations for each line

  • "Politics, translated into your life." We found no organisation using "Politics, translated" as a tagline. The nearest use is an academic book, Translation Politicised and Politics Translated (eds. Almanna & House, 2023). It argues that translators bring their own "cultural background, beliefs and values" into a text (Peter Lang, 2023). Possible signals, in our reading: (a) it implies politics is a foreign language that needs a go-between, which fits plain-English goals; (b) translation involves choices, which some readers may take as the site interpreting and not only quoting; (c) some may expect a language-translation feature.
  • "Politics is Personal. See how." See section 4. The capital P on "Personal" makes it look like a title or brand.
  • "Every policy lands somewhere. Start with where you stand." We found no prior use of "Every policy lands somewhere". "Where you stand" is the Political Compass's call to action ("Find out where you stand") and means a place on an ideology scale (politicalcompass.org, accessed 24 Sep 2026). The site does not place users on a scale, so some readers may expect a quiz. In the site's own flow, "where you stand" may instead be read as "where you live". Romily has said framing step one as "where do you live" feels "a bit pushy" (project notes, round-five answers, 24 Sep 2026).
  • "Their policies. Your life. See where they meet." We found no exact prior use. The "Our/Your Life" pattern echoes a 2007 Conservative Party policy statement, "Our Society, Your Life", from David Cameron's time as Opposition leader (party source) (Wikipedia, accessed 24 Sep 2026). The echo is loose. "See where they meet" sits near quiz language about views that "match" or "align" (iSideWith, Vote Compass above). "Their… your" sets politicians apart from the reader. That can read as simple clarity or as "them and us". This is our reading, not a finding.

6. How each line sits with the name "What's It To Me?"

The name is a question. In everyday English "What's it to you?" is often defensive: "Mind your own business" (Wiktionary, accessed 24 Sep 2026). The site turns it into a sincere question. Romily's planned sequence runs "Housing. What's it to me? … What's it to my neighbourhood? My region? The country? Someone unlike me?" (project notes, 24 Sep 2026).

  • "Politics, translated into your life." Answers the question directly: this is how politics becomes "yours". Moves from "me" (name) to "your" (line).
  • "Politics is Personal. See how." Makes a claim, then invites. The name asks, the line asserts. Together that is three short parts, question then answer then action.
  • "Every policy lands somewhere. Start with where you stand." "Lands somewhere" leaves room for her neighbourhood → country → "someone unlike me" steps. It is the only line that does not centre on the reader alone. It is also the longest.
  • "Their policies. Your life. See where they meet." Repeats the name's rhythm of short pieces. Adds a third party ("their") that the name does not have.

What this means for the site

  • The evidence gives no strong reason to expect any of the four to change behaviour much. Big claims about small wording changes have not held up in large replications. The choice rests on clarity, fit with the name, and what each line signals.
  • Neutral peers mostly open with a plain description of the task. If Barny wants to follow that pattern, a line can sit next to one short plain sentence ("Enter your postcode to see every candidate on your ballot, the same page for each."). That sentence can carry the "what we do" job whichever line is chosen.
  • A "your life" frame is a real frame. Research shows it shifts people towards weighing their own interests. The "someone unlike me" step in Romily's sequence balances that. The line doesn't have to do it.
  • Two lines borrow words quiz sites already use ("where you stand"; "meet" near "match"). The site does not match or place users, so it may be worth checking how first-time visitors read these words. A quick test with 5–10 people would give direct evidence where the research gives none.
  • Whatever is chosen, a visible neutrality statement (like Democracy Club's and Full Fact's) is what neutral peers rely on for trust. A tagline cannot do that job.
  • We did not check trade marks or web domains for any line. Do this before print or merchandise.
