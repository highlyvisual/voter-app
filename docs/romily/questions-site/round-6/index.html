Candidates with no record

Other sites do one of three things. They give everyone the same section, with a neutral line when it's empty (TheyWorkForYou, Ballotpedia). They hide a section when it's empty (Democracy Club's WhoCanIVoteFor). Or they ask every party the same questions, so nobody needs a record (Germany's Wahl-O-Mat, the Netherlands' StemWijzer). StemWijzer says it doesn't use voting records because new parties have never voted. Research on decisions finds people give more weight to facts that every option has than to facts only some have. We found almost no research on the exact case, though: an empty "record" box on a candidate's page. Democracy Club lists 15 candidates for Holborn and St Pancras. Full paper

What each one still leaves for us to judge: with the first, whether an empty box makes a newcomer look worse. With the second, whether a link only some candidates have is itself a difference. With the third, which questions to ask, and what to do if few candidates answer (one US site's response rate reportedly fell to 20% by 2016). With the fourth, what to show for independents and parties with no manifesto, and there are several in Holborn and St Pancras.

Proposed: "Has not held this office before, so there is no voting record." The paper points out that "has not held this office" is a plain fact, while "no record" on its own can sound like a fault. TheyWorkForYou uses "This person has not voted on any of the key issues which we keep track of." The same pattern would be used for councillors: "No council record, because this candidate has not been a councillor."

Promises and votes

In round five you leaned towards pairing a promise with a vote only where the promise names the exact measure, with no "kept" or "broken". We wrote that as a rule with four tests and tried it on 50 promises from the 2024 manifestos, 10 from each of Labour, the Conservatives, the Liberal Democrats, the Greens and Reform UK, chosen the same way for all five. 5 paired cleanly, 7 were unclear, and 38 were never put to a Commons vote that names the measure. Every clean pair came from a vote the party set up itself. Smaller parties have fewer ways to get a vote held: of the 20 "opposition days" in each session of Parliament, when opposition parties can set up debates and votes, 17 go to the biggest opposition party and 3 to the second-biggest, which shares them with smaller parties. Full Fact, PolitiFact and others do give verdicts, and TheyWorkForYou sums up how MPs voted. The complaints about these sites that we found were mostly about which items were chosen, and about summaries that hide why someone voted the way they did. None we found were about a mechanical rule like this one. Full paper

From the first option to the third, each step adds more of our own judgement.

In the test we took the first ten qualifying promises in page order. That is mechanical, but it bunches topics together: all ten Green promises came from their health chapter, because it comes first. The alternative the paper gives is the first qualifying promise in each of the site's nine topics. Either way, the method would be published on the site.

Proposed: "No Commons vote on this exact measure, July 2024 – September 2026." It would be shown the same way for every party, and it must not read as a criticism. The paper also suggests a short explainer, the same for all parties, saying that how many votes a party can trigger depends on its number of MPs and the Commons rules.

Councillors

Since 25 February 2014, English councils have had to record each councillor's name on the budget and council tax vote. Otherwise a named vote usually only happens when councillors ask for one, and we found no rule like the budget one in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland. We looked at 20 councils as a sample (not a survey): a few (Cardiff, Highland, Aberdeen, Fife, Kent) record names on most contested votes; many only on the budget or when asked. We found no dataset of council votes anywhere in the UK, so for now any votes would have to be entered by hand, with a link for each. Attendance and committee membership can be collected automatically from the meetings system many councils use, though Camden's blocked us. Full paper

A low figure can come from illness, pregnancy, caring or bereavement, and "sent apologies" and "didn't turn up" look the same in a percentage. New councillors have had few meetings, so one absence moves the figure a lot. Councils record it differently, so figures from different councils can't fairly be compared.

English councils publish each councillor's dependants' carers' allowance by name. It's lawful, but it reveals family circumstances. A higher total usually reflects a senior role rather than anything else, and some councillors turn allowances down.

What "regional" means

There is no single regional level. What sits between the council and Westminster depends on where you live, and some of it is elected and some isn't. The site would need its own table for things the postcode tools don't give (which combined authority you're in, meaning the group of councils some areas have, often with a mayor; and who runs your police and fire), and its own calendar of when each level next votes, because Democracy Club only lists an election once it has been called. Local issues: the fixed rule

We checked the eight councils in the brief. Five (Brighton and Hove, Cotswold, Milton Keynes, Windsor and Maidenhead, Blackpool) have an open data feed of their meetings. South Staffordshire uses a different system, Stirling posts its agendas only as PDFs, and Camden blocked us. You asked for research first, so we turned round five's "fixed rule" option into a real draft, to show how it would work if nobody picked items by hand. It takes items from Full Council and Cabinet, the council's own "key decisions" (the ones the council itself labels as major), and anything naming your ward. It looks back six months and ahead three, removes routine, private and repeated items, and shows at most 8 per list, with a link to the council's full list. We ran it on Queen's Park ward in Brighton: 86 meetings and 639 agenda items. The only item tied to the ward was a pub licence. The first four "coming up" items were Full Council motions, titled in the proposers' own words. Full paper, with the rule written out in full

The paper sees this as the biggest neutrality risk. Its one firm point: show all or none, never pick some.

The opening line

You asked for research on your four lines and for Dad's view. He'll make the call, but here's what the paper found. The neutral election sites we checked mostly open with a plain task ("Find out…", "Compare…") rather than a slogan. Research finds that small wording changes rarely change what people actually do. It also finds a "your life" frame isn't blank: it nudges people to weigh their own interests more. "Politics is Personal" turns around "The personal is political", the 1969–70 feminist slogan, which turns up in A-level Politics study notes, though we found no party using it. "Their policies. Your life." loosely echoes "Our Society, Your Life", a 2007 Conservative policy paper. "Where you stand" is already the Political Compass quiz's call to action. The paper suggests a quick test with 5–10 people to see how first-time visitors read quiz-like words such as "where you stand", since the research can't answer that.
