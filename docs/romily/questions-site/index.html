What we ask people

Friday's decision was a small mandatory core with everything else optional. This pins it down.

Candidates: postcode, age band, who lives in the household, whether you rent or own, rough income band, whether you have children in school, whether you drive. Optional ones raised on Friday included disability and gender identity. List your mandatory set and your optional set.

For example "who did you vote for last time" or "which issues matter most to you". Asking makes the results feel more tailored; never asking makes the accusation "it favoured a party" impossible to sustain, and keeps the app out of the most sensitive category of personal data.

Option A: nothing is stored; the page works it out and forgets it. Option B: people can make an account to save their profile. Option C: we keep anonymous totals (how many people used it, which areas) but never individual answers. Friday's conversation also raised selling aggregated data one day, like polling. Where's your line?

Staying impartial

Agreed on Friday: no opinions generated by AI, black-and-white design, party colour only on that party's own panel, a verifiable source on every claim. These are the edge cases.

Same sections, same order, same space, listed in ballot-paper order (alphabetical by surname). Where a candidate has published nothing on a topic, the slot says "No published position found". Anything you'd change?

The winter fuel allowance example came up on Friday. For an MP standing again, their voting record is public and factual. Showing "pledged vs voted" is information; but it will look like an attack to that candidate's supporters. Include it, and if so, with what rules?

Proposal: every claim needs two people to sign it off, and there's a public log of every change and every complaint with how it was resolved. Who should those people be? Should there be an outside advisory group?

Beyond black-and-white and party colour only on that party's panel. Tone of voice, what the app never says, how it handles a topic where the parties' claims contradict each other.

How it's run

Structure decisions are hard to reverse, so they're worth deciding early.

Not-for-profit was floated on Friday. It makes impartiality easier to believe and partnerships easier to get; it makes fundraising and paying people harder.

The worry on Friday was being copied. The other view: the organisations already doing non-partisan election data in the UK (Democracy Club, mySociety, Full Fact) are natural allies, and their backing is the strongest defence against "who is behind this". Which instinct do you trust?

"Catchy" hooks were discussed and flagged as risky. What would actually make you and your friends open it the week before an election? Who would you trust to share it?
