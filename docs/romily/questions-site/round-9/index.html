  • Built“Right now” for far more councils. At first we could only read the meeting papers of about 14 councils and the consultations of 3, so most postcodes saw almost nothing. The site now finds each council’s own meetings and consultation websites by itself. In a test, that came to about 200 councils for meetings, 33 for consultations, and Gazette notices for 49, up from mostly Camden.
  • Built“Thinking of moving here?” Dad asked for this for people looking at an area they might move to, so they come for the area and stay for the politics. Put in a postcode and you see every school within 2 miles, nearest first, and GP practices within 1.5 miles. There’s a plain explanation of how school catchment areas and admissions work, then house prices, crime, the planning record, the next elections and who decides things there. There are no Ofsted grades or exam results, because they score schools. Questions 3 and 7.
  • FoundA deeper search for new data. On 7 October Dad had seven searches run at once for official, impartial data we don’t use yet, each one checked by loading real data. Among the finds: GOV.UK publishes how many times each page was viewed, so “What people are reading” can show a real number; DfE publishes how many families asked for each school; Scotland has a national map of school catchments; and councils’ meeting systems list their decisions and petitions. Questions 18 to 22.
  • Built“What people are reading”, impartial sources only: official statistics on GOV.UK and from the ONS, petitions to Parliament, the bills going through Parliament, and the House of Commons Library, Lords Library and Parliament’s science office. Each list stays in its publisher’s own order, and the page says what that order is. GOV.UK ranks by how often each page is read, and petitions by signatures. Where a publisher gives no count, the list is newest first. We never mix the lists or reorder them. Questions 4 to 7.

“Right now” and the news

Your ask of 6 October: “What matters to me right now”.

Your politics, after “What you’re voting for”.

Showing headlines from newspapers or broadcasters is a bigger decision. We only quote words exactly, so we’d need a news source we can quote word for word and link to, and we’d have to show every outlet the same way. None of them publish how often a story is read.

The two new pages

“Thinking of moving here?” and “What people are reading”. For the reading lists, Dad asked for an order that’s fair, such as how often something is read on its own website, and for the site to be open about how it does it.

Schools are listed by distance only. That’s the only fair order we have, because grades and results score them.

It’s never used for candidates or parties, and we don’t combine or adjust the counts. The page says plainly that being read a lot doesn’t make something important or true.

For example: the Electoral Commission, the Office for Budget Responsibility, or the research services of the Senedd, the Scottish Parliament and the Northern Ireland Assembly.

At the moment they’re only linked from the no-election area page and from “Right now”.
