01 · Why it exists

The question people actually ask

Not who is winning, but what would this mean for me, for my household, for my street. Most political coverage answers a different question.

What’s It To Me? began as an idea of Romily Johnson’s, a student: an informed electorate makes better decisions, and the way to inform it is to connect politics to a person’s own life without telling them how to vote.

Manifestos are written for everyone, and so for no one in particular. Campaigns talk in national averages. The gap between that and an ordinary life is where many people give up. The site closes the gap with a postcode and a few optional facts about a household.

It is a non-partisan, not-for-profit UK voter-information site. It takes no advertising and no money from any party, candidate or campaign, and neither founder is a member of, works for, or is paid by any party or campaign.

“So the name is the question, asked plainly.”whatsittome.org, “Who we are”
What it will never do

Rank, score, match or recommend. The site shows who is standing, what each has published, and where it touches the reader’s life. The judgement stays with the voter.

02 · The experience

What a voter sees

Four steps from a postcode to an informed choice, on a phone, in a few minutes.

Step 1
Your ballot

Enter a postcode. The site finds every election you can vote in. The full postcode is used once and discarded.

Step 2
Your household

Optional questions in broad bands (housing, work, children, income range), including answers that fit students. They stay on the device.

Step 3
Every candidate

Everyone standing, in ballot-paper order, on the same page layout, with what each has published on nine topics.

Step 4
What it means

Topics that touch your household come first, with the reason shown. Where the numbers allow, what a tax or benefit change would mean for a household like yours.

Nine topics

Money and cost of living · housing · health and social care · education · environment and energy · immigration and borders · crime, policing and justice · defence, foreign affairs and the EU · equality and rights.

Beyond the ballot

For every postcode, even without an election: when you next vote, who represents you at every level (council, mayor, devolved parliament, Westminster), and which unelected bodies also run things locally. For 29 councils, a page on what the council has published on housing, transport, council tax, the environment and schools, and what it is deciding at its meetings.

Practical help to vote

Registration, postal and proxy deadlines, the free Voter Authority Certificate, accepted photo ID, and where to vote. A printable notes sheet to take to the polling station.

Built for everyone

Light and dark themes, adjustable text size, high contrast, a plain-English mode, read-aloud, and a light version for slow connections. Pages are checked at phone width, with enlarged text, and with an automated accessibility checker.

03 · Safeguards

Neutral by design, not by promise

Romily’s founding requirements were that the site be completely impartial, attribute every fact to its source, leave no way for figures to be manipulated, and give no grounds to say it prefers any candidate. Each became a rule the software enforces.

SafeguardHow it works
One template for everyoneEvery candidate gets the same page, the same sections in the same place, in ballot-paper order. There is no other ordering anywhere on the site.
No record is not a mark againstA newcomer without a voting record gets the same neutral line as anyone else: “Has not held this office before, so there is no voting record to show.” Party positions are the layer everyone has.
Quotation, not interpretationEvery position is the source’s own words, with publisher, date and link. A short plain-English summary sits underneath as a reading aid, using neutral verbs (“says”, “pledged”).
Vague promises are labelledIf a pledge has no number, date or concrete object, it is shown as “Stated aim, no measurable commitment found”, the same way for every party.
Neutral colourThe interface is neutral. A party’s colour appears only on its own panel. Money figures never use green or red.
No AI at the point of useNo AI system runs when a visitor uses the site. AI helps draft summaries behind the scenes; every quotation is checked word for word against its source before publication, and the record shows what was drafted with AI help.
Nothing ever silently changesPositions are held in an append-only ledger. A correction is a new entry that points at the old one; nothing is edited or deleted, and the database refuses any attempt.

The never-build list

Features that would turn information into advice. They are refused however useful they might look.

Match percentages or “agreement” scores
Favourites, hearts or saved shortlists
Sorting by anything but the ballot paper
“Recommended for you” or “people like you chose”
Left/right axes or ideology quizzes
Weighting by incumbency, polls or past results
04 · Provenance

Where every fact comes from

The primary source first: the party’s or candidate’s own publication, the official record, the statute. Press reports are used only as a flagged stopgap until the primary is found.

Each kind carries a grey label of identical weight. Where candidate statements or leaflets appear, the page says: we check they are real, not that they are true.

The site’s own text, claims and ledger are published under CC BY-SA 4.0; the code is open source under the AGPL-3.0.

Anyone can check the record

  • The public ledger lists every live position and every change, with who made it, when and why.
  • A fingerprint before polling day. The full ledger is published with a SHA-256 checksum and archived, so anyone can confirm later that nothing was altered.
  • Every source is re-read every week. Each quotation is checked to still be there word for word, and an independent Internet Archive copy is linked beside it. The first pass led to 24 claims being re-linked to where the words actually are.
  • Complaints are logged publicly, with their outcome.
  • Coverage is shown, not hidden. A coverage page counts how much sourced material was found for each party on each topic, and says plainly that it measures what was found, not quality.

05 · The money question

What it could mean for a household

Where a party’s tax or benefit promise is specific enough to model, the site shows what it would change for a household like the reader’s, with every assumption stated.

8,100household types, from combinations of age, income, household, children, housing and work
40,500results computed in advance with PolicyEngine UK, an open-source model of the UK tax and benefit system
0calculations done live: every figure is a stored, reproducible result

Computing everything in advance means every figure can be checked and reproduced, and the model version is stored with each result. The assumptions (band midpoints, representative ages and rents, who claims which benefits) are listed next to every calculation.

Only promises with a concrete number are modelled. Three party policies are modelled so far, alongside current law.

Stated limits

The model currently uses London assumptions, because the first election covered is in London; it will be extended before money figures are shown elsewhere. Student finance sits outside the tax-and-benefit model and is not included.

Privacy, in one paragraph

The postcode is sent once, used to find the ballot and discarded; only the first half travels further, to place an approximate circle on a map. Household answers are broad bands and stay on the visitor’s device. The site keeps anonymous daily page counts per election and nothing else: no IP addresses, no tracking cookies, no per-visitor records.

07 · Foundations

Technology, governance and cost

Deliberately simple, open and cheap to run, so that the site can outlast any one election.

Technology

A server-rendered web application (Next.js) hosted on Netlify, one Postgres database (Supabase, hosted in London), and scheduled jobs in GitHub that refresh elections, schools and consultations daily, and council meetings, planning, party funding and every quoted source weekly. Each run is logged publicly on the site’s status page. Where a site blocks automated reading, it is checked by hand, so no party is watched less closely than another. Maps use OpenStreetMap. The code is open source under the AGPL-3.0.

Governance

Romily Johnson, Founder and Product Lead, owns the project and makes decisions on direction. Barny Trevelyan-Johnson, Technical Lead, builds and runs it. Decisions are recorded with a date and the person who made them. Corrections and withdrawals are made by a named maintainer and logged publicly with the reason.

Running costs

The site is self-funded. It currently runs on a shared hosting plan ($9 a month, shared with other sites) and a free database tier. The shared hosting allowance has run out every month since June, and briefly took the site offline on 24 September. Dedicated hosting before polling days is the most immediate practical need.

Independence

The site takes no advertising and no money from any party, candidate or campaign. Its rules on what is shown are written into the software and published, so they do not depend on who pays for it.

Where partners could help

Hosting and running costs

Dedicated hosting with headroom for polling-day traffic, so the site cannot be suspended by other sites’ usage.

Data partners

Democracy Club, which already supplies candidate data: a candidates-API token, higher request limits for polling day, and clarity on candidate photo licences.

Welsh translation

A translator for a full Welsh-language version before Welsh elections.

Testing and feedback

Schools, colleges, libraries and community groups to try the site with first-time voters and tell us where it falls short.
