01 · Verdict

The verdict on one page

The site's foundations are unusually sound. Its problems are almost all at the surface, and most can be fixed in days. The bigger question the rest of the world raises is not whether the design is right, but whether it can be kept running.

Strong

The rules are real and visible: identical pages in ballot order, a verbatim quotation beside every summary, a source and date on every claim, an append-only ledger, and an honest list of what the site cannot tell you. Accessibility is clean (100 out of 100 on every page tested, no horizontal scroll on any phone width). No cookies, no trackers. Zero broken internal links across 400 pages. Voting mechanics match gov.uk on every point checked. The freshness reporting on /status is better than any comparable site we found.

Weak

Several things the site says about itself are not yet true: that answers are never sent to us (they travel in the page address), that every candidate is invited (none has been), that seats held and votes are shown (they are not), that figures are computed from pledges (two pledges, for one election). After the postcode, a voter lands on a page of crime and house-price statistics rather than the ballot. Big per-topic numbers read as scores, and an independent's page is a column of zeros. Every page is hidden from search engines. Nothing is cached, so every visit renders from scratch.

The lesson from everyone else

Tools that match or rank voters to parties get attacked on the matching step, every time, in every country, and there is no neutral version of it. Tools that only publish what is on the record (Policy.nz in New Zealand, MyNeta in India, NRK's Partiguiden in Norway) reach a sixth of voters and survive lawsuits. The ones that die, die of hand-built content and five-yearly funding, not of bad design. Our design is in the second family; our risk is the third.

Five things before 8 October

  1. Make every claim the site makes about itself true, or change the wording. The site asks to be judged on accuracy; a journalist will start with the About page. Section 4 lists eleven such claims.
  2. Land people on the ballot. Postcode in, candidates out, with the election named and dated at the top. Everything else becomes a card beneath it.
  3. Stop the numbers reading as scores. Replace the large per-topic counts with a plain list of the topics a candidate has published on, and put counts inside the open card in small type.
  4. Tell the unregistered voter what has happened. Registration closed on 22 September. The only signal is a line through the date.
  5. Flip the launch switches together. Indexing, caching, security headers and the internal pages that are currently public (one is headed "For Romily … tell Dad").

03 · Strengths

What the site does well

It is worth being precise about the strengths, because the improvements list should not accidentally undo any of them. Most of what follows is rare among the sixty tools examined; some of it is unique.

The promise, kept on every page

The first screen of the home page says what the site is, what it will never do, and how it handles a postcode, all before the fold on a phone. The footer of every page repeats that the site does not recommend, rank or score. All fifteen Holborn and St Pancras candidates have structurally identical pages: the same sections in the same order, the same "Nothing published that we could source on … That means nothing found, not nothing to say" wording wherever a topic is empty, and every summary sits beside its exact quotation with a toggle between the two. The "Hide names and parties while I read" control on the comparison page is a feature Policy.nz also offers; we already have it.

Even-handed language

Of 260 unique claims on the Holborn pages, every one uses the same label set in the same order (position, meaning, who it applies to, source). Where a party's own words carry a loaded term ("wasteful", "liberal laws", "anti-British", "real marriage", "unfair targeting"), the summary distances it with "what it calls" or "it describes as", and it does so for parties of the left and the right alike. No summary on any page ranks, scores or recommends in words.

Honesty about limits

The About page carries an eight-point "What this site can't tell you" list, a public change log with reasons, an accuracy report that says "skipped" when a check was skipped, and an impact page that reports zeros. Nothing of that kind exists on Democracy Club, Ballotpedia (whose corrections log is private) or any voting-advice tool we found. Full Fact's public corrections page is the nearest UK precedent, and ours is append-only where theirs is edited.

Accurate mechanics

Polling hours, the photo-ID list and the proxy and postal deadlines all match gov.uk; the registration and Voter Authority Certificate deadlines were verified by calculation because the Electoral Commission's pages refuse automated requests. The eligibility checker runs in the browser. The no-election page names the next date, says why it might move, shows who represents the reader now and nudges them to register. The office explainer ("What this office decides / What it does not") includes a line most sites would not dare: that candidates often campaign on things their office cannot change, and that this is not dishonest in itself.

2. Where the postcode lands weak

Typing a Holborn postcode and pressing "Find my election" lands on step 1 of a four-step trail: a map, who represents the area now, the planning record, five Parliament petitions, the claimant count, seven deprivation deciles for "Camden 029C", house prices and recorded crime within a mile. The words "by-election", "8 October" and "candidates" do not appear until the links at the bottom, where "Skip to the candidates" is a small grey footnote. A voter who typed a postcode to find out who is on the ballot has to scroll past crime statistics to discover there is a ballot. The ballot page itself is good; it is two clicks too far away, and the page in front of it promises schools and air quality it does not show.

3. Numbers that read as scores weak

Each candidate page shows nine collapsed topic cards, each with a large numeral. Tom Darwood, the independent, has nine large zeros; Reform UK's candidate has 4, 8, 2, 5, 3 and so on; the ballot-paper cards say "36 in total" against "0 in total". The caption explains that a count measures how much has been published, not how good it is, but the numeral is the most prominent element on the page and a reader who never opens a card sees only that. It is a league table by another name, and it rewards parties with long policy websites and punishes independents and small parties. The evidence in section 9 says volume becomes a proxy for seriousness the moment candidates are seen side by side, whether or not the site intends it.

4. The deadline that has passed weak

Registration for 8 October closed on 22 September and postal-vote applications on 23 September. On the ballot page both dates carry a strikethrough and nothing else: no "closed", no "what now". A nineteen-year-old who finds the site this week needs one sentence: registration for this election has closed; if you were not registered by 22 September you cannot vote on 8 October, but register now and you will be on the roll for May 2027; you can still apply for a proxy vote until 5pm on 30 September. The /how-to-vote page's deadline section is 26 identical lines saying "the key dates are at the top of that page", which tells the reader nothing.

5. Council by-elections weak

The Lambeth Myatt's Fields ballot page says, correctly, that a councillor's win does not change national tax, benefits or NHS policy and that only positions published for this council election are shown. It then offers the same nine national topics, including "Immigration and borders" and "Defence, foreign affairs and the EU", and six of the seven candidate pages are nine empty national-topic cards each saying "Nothing published that we could source". For most council by-elections the site currently delivers names and parties wrapped in 54 empty boxes. Twenty-five of the 26 elections listed this autumn are council elections, and May 2027 will be almost entirely council elections.

6. The money layer's promise weak

The home page promises "what it could mean for a home like yours" and the About page describes a computed layer built "from a party's published pledges". What exists is a baseline under current law for the reader's household (well caveated, with assumptions listed) and, on the ballot page only, when the profile is complete, modelled bars for two pledges: Reform UK's £15,000 personal allowance (worth £392 a year to the test household) and the Green Party's removal of the National Insurance upper earnings limit (no change for that household). The Liberal Democrats pledge the same £15,000 allowance in a live claim on the same ballot and it is not modelled, which is precisely the asymmetry a rival would point at. Separately, the baseline's five lines do not add up to its total: £32,500 less £1,472 tax less £589 National Insurance plus £9,620 Universal Credit plus £1,407 Child Benefit is £41,466, and the page shows £44,838. PolicyEngine's net income adds every benefit it models and subtracts every tax and pension contribution; the page lists four of those lines and does not say what else is in the total.

7. Words that lean without meaning to adequate

The attribution verb follows the source type: parties whose source is a document called a manifesto or contract are "pledged"; Reform UK, whose source is a policy web page, is never "pledged" and always "says it would" (0 of 35 claims against, for example, Labour's 18 and the Liberal Democrats' 25). A reader can take "pledged" as firmer. The hedge "without giving figures" appears only on the two smallest parties' claims (6 of their 12) and on no large party's figure-free pledge. Both patterns are defensible on the facts and neither rule is stated anywhere. Only Labour carries a "Public record" layer of enacted policy, written in the past tense ("froze", "removed"), which the About page explains but the card does not.

8. Small inconsistencies an activist would count adequate

Every "own words" claim appears twice on a candidate page, the second time under a "Your area" heading that for one candidate contains a line about Ukraine. Ben Walker's ballot description ("UKIP - NO to Illegal Immigration") is entered as a claim and counted under Immigration; no other candidate's description is. Two Liberal Democrat claims are in the ledger twice (rows 493 and 496, 494 and 495, word for word, superseding originals that were themselves duplicates), so that candidate's count is two too high. Party names appear as the registered name at the top of the ballot page and as the ballot description on the cards, with no label saying which is which. "Has stood N times before" is present for thirteen candidates and simply absent for two; there is no "Standing for the first time".

9. One list of topics, seven spellings adequate

The nine topics appear as "Healthcare and social care", "Health", "YOUR HEALTH AND CARE" and "healthcare and social care" on different pages; "Environment, climate and energy", "Environment", "Climate" and "CLIMATE, ENERGY AND THE LOCAL ENVIRONMENT"; and in the ledger as "environment climate & energy" without its comma. The site name appears as "What's It To Me?", "What's It To Me" and "What's it to me?". Dates appear as "Thursday, 8 October 2026", "Thursday 8 October", "8 October 2026", "2026-10-08", "Tue 22 Sept" and "Sep". Most pages' browser titles are just "What's It To Me?" with no page name, including every candidate's page.

10. Internal material on public pages weak

/journey is a public page headed "For Romily · not linked from the site" that ends "or tell Dad". The Camden council page contains "Read via WebFetch; camden.gov.uk returns 403 to direct requests" inside a sourced section. The public ledger names the actor of 35 entries as "maintainer (Barny's build session)" and shows raw JSON in its "Applies if" column. /positions lists archived elections by internal identifier. The maintainer sign-in is on the public origin with a single passcode field.

11. Trust plumbing adequate

Who runs the site, how it is funded, how to complain and the data licence are all present and one tap from every ballot page, which is better than most. Gaps: there is no page called Privacy (the address returns 404); no accessibility statement; /feedback returns 404 though robots.txt mentions it; the status page says 46 of 233 sources could not be re-read automatically, but the ballot pages do not mark which claims those are.

12. Smaller things seen on the way adequate

The profile's introductory paragraph repeats in full on all nine steps, pushing each question below the fold on a phone. On a 390px phone the ballot number "12" wraps and reads as "1 / 2". The floating "?" help button covers the "Or build your profile first" link on the home page, the "Add your profile" link on candidate pages and cells in the ledger. On the comparison page at phone width a summary and its quotation run together with no space ("…2031.Asking everyone…"). The comparison page reaches about 57,000 pixels tall with only an h1 to navigate by. A topic page and a candidate page returned a 502 or "upstream request failed" on first load and worked on retry. The Monster Raving Loony Party's own spellings ("Manicfesto", "footware") are correctly preserved but a reader may take them for ours.

05 · Weaknesses

Weaknesses: under the bonnet

The technical picture is the reverse of the content picture: the hard things are done well and a handful of switches were never flipped. All of these are Barny's, and most are an afternoon each.

1. Every page is hidden from search engines weak

The site's root layout sets robots: index false, follow false, so every page, the home page included, carries "noindex, nofollow", while robots.txt allows everything and publishes a 91-address sitemap. Lighthouse's SEO score is 66 on every page for that single reason. If this is a deliberate soft-launch setting, fine; but the sitemap and the robots file then contradict it, and the switch needs a date. If it is accidental, the site is invisible for its first live election. A candidate's page also has the browser title "What's It To Me?" and the ballot's share image rather than its own, and no page has a canonical address or any structured data.

2. Nothing is cached weak

Every HTML response is private, no-store, so no page is ever served from Netlify's edge or the browser's back-forward cache, and every visit to the 1.2 MB Holborn ballot page is a fresh origin render (59% of that page is the React payload repeating the rendered content as JSON). Warm time to first byte on the ballot was 1.3 to 1.5 seconds and 2.0 seconds cold; the home page's largest paint on simulated mobile was 4.7 seconds. The nav and footer links also fire eight to fourteen uncacheable prefetches on every page load. Content changes at most daily; a five-minute public cache with stale-while-revalidate, purged by the refresh jobs, would remove almost every origin render and keep the footer's "last refreshed" honest.

3. No security headers beyond HSTS weak

No Content-Security-Policy, no X-Frame-Options, no Referrer-Policy, no Permissions-Policy on any route; HSTS without includeSubDomains. Any site can frame the main ballot page, which asks people to type postcodes and household details. Fonts come from Google (every visitor's IP goes to Google before the page renders, on a privacy-led site) and Leaflet comes from cdnjs with no integrity hash. The response header advertises the framework version. The embed route needs to be frameable; nothing else does.

4. Weight on the critical path adequate

Both the light and dark brand lockups (244 and 240 KiB) and both marks (47 and 49 KiB) are preloaded on every page although only one theme shows; the home page's largest paint is a 633×514 PNG shown at 330×268. A 92 KiB 512-pixel favicon loads on every page. The Google Fonts stylesheet blocks rendering for about 0.8 seconds on mobile. Brand images are revalidated on every visit rather than cached.

5. Accessibility defects, few but real adequate

The two ledger tables scroll sideways on phones and cannot be reached or scrolled from the keyboard (axe rates this serious, and the ledger is the accountability page). The fifteen candidate summaries on the ballot page carry an aria-label that does not begin with the visible name, which breaks voice control. The postcode error is announced but not tied to the field and focus is not returned to it. The home page runs a 38-second marquee with no pause control. The comparison and positions pages have only an h1. The embed page has no heading at all.

6. Dead and unverifiable source links adequate

Thirteen external source links are confirmed dead (Blackpool three, Stroud, East Hertfordshire, Windsor and Maidenhead, Bassetlaw, South Kesteven, Stirling, Bournemouth Christchurch and Poole, Newcastle-under-Lyme, a candidate's own site returning 503, a local news site whose origin is down); two are plain http. None of them has an archived copy to fall back on. A further 184 links on 29 hosts returned 403 to the test machine, almost certainly bot protection rather than dead pages, and need re-checking from a UK connection. Thirteen dead out of roughly 950 verifiable is 1.4%, respectable for a site quoting council PDFs, but each one undermines "every claim links to its source".

7. Wrong error for a real-looking postcode adequate

A well-formed postcode that does not exist ("EH1 1AA") produces "We couldn't look up that postcode just now. Please try again in a minute", which blames a temporary fault and invites a pointless retry. Every other bad input degrades gracefully. The 404 for an unknown ballot is rendered client-side and is blank without JavaScript, unlike every real page.

8. Odds and ends strong

The RSS feed is valid but lacks a language, a build date, a self link and item descriptions, and every item shares one date. There is no favicon.ico (legacy clients request it) and no web manifest, so no "add to home screen" name or colour. The print stylesheet exists but prints the ballot with one of 120 collapsed cards open. Dark mode works but the browser chrome does not follow because there is no color-scheme meta. The Camden council page shows a layout shift of 0.165 when its consultations load. The /api/place-layers route returns empty layers for every input and appears to be a stub. The home page's timeline initials render at 10.4px, the smallest text on the site.

In one paragraph

Search indexing, edge caching, four security headers, self-hosted fonts, single-theme image loading and a smaller favicon are six configuration changes that would take one working day together and would improve the site's speed, privacy and discoverability more than any feature. They are items 33 to 38 in section 10.

06 · The field

The world's voter tools, in seven families

Sixty tools were examined. They sort into seven kinds, and each kind fails in its own characteristic way. We belong to the third family and borrow from the first, fourth and sixth. Figures were re-read from their sources on 25 September 2026; the full tool-by-tool notes, with addresses, are in the five research reports.

1 · List-makers: who is standing, where to vote

Who does it best. Democracy Club's elections database, behind WhoCanIVoteFor and its partners, handled 20 million postcode searches between April and July 2024 and 10.6 million in spring 2026; half arrive through the Electoral Commission, the BBC and other partners, and 78 councils embedded its widget in 2026. Its best design detail is a one-line provenance sentence under every candidate statement saying who added it and when. Several countries make the list-maker the state: Japan's election gazette, Taiwan's household bulletin (with audio for blind voters), Korea's mailed booklet of five years of tax, assets and criminal records, Mexico's INE portal, and the California and Oregon voter pamphlets, which guarantee every candidate the same typeface, the same length and ballot order (Oregon: 325 words, four required fields certified as true, a felony for lying in them).

What goes wrong. Depth. Democracy Club's own feedback names empty candidate pages as its top complaint ("If candidates can't be bothered to advertise themselves then I can't be bothered to vote"), statements were held for 53.6% of candidates at the 2024 general election but only 25% at the 2025 locals, and in 2023 it admitted it could not tell when a stored statement had gone stale. The official pamphlets are fair in format but not in access: California statewide candidates must pay and accept spending limits; Oregon charges $600 to $3,500 or 200 to 500 signatures.

To borrow. The provenance line. The "every election this person has stood in" table. Equal format as a written rule (Elections ACT publishes ours almost verbatim: same font, same size, order by lot, every candidate listed whether or not they sent a statement). The Electoral Commission's polling-station lookup, which every list-maker offers and we do not.

2 · Matchers: answer questions, get a percentage

Who does it best. Germany's Wahl-O-Mat was used 26 million times at the 2025 federal election; its 38 statements are chosen by a workshop of 20 to 25 first-time voters and answered by the parties themselves, and its "Begründungen" view shows each party's own justification side by side. The Netherlands' StemWijzer passed 8 million completions in 2025; Switzerland's smartvote had profiles for 83% of 5,925 National Council candidates in 2023; Sweden's SVT ran 312 local and national compasses in 2026 with 3.7 million completions; Finland's Yle had answers from about 90% of candidates in 2019 and over 14,000 in 2025.

What goes wrong. The matching step, always. A Belgian study simulated 500,000 random subsets of statements and found the advised party swung by tens of points; a Dutch study found most StemWijzer users would have received different advice under another matching model. Every fairness complaint found against Vote Compass (Canada 2011: critics said answering "neutral" throughout produced a Liberal result), iSideWith (one-sided question blurbs, unexplained score drift), the Japanese newspaper matchers (the same voter gets different "closest parties" from different papers because five-point scales are treated as distances) and Kieskompas (its second axis is nearly redundant) attacks that step. Courts have intervened three times over who was in and who was out: Cologne in May 2019 shut the Wahl-O-Mat down for capping comparisons at eight parties (settled in three days with "all parties, one button"), and Brussels in May 2024 ordered VRT to tell every user that the party Voor U was missing from its Stemtest, on pain of €2,500 a day. Finland's press council reprimanded Yle in February 2026 for launching on a provisional candidate list without saying so. France's Elyze collected birth date, postcode and voting intention from a million people in a fortnight, had a hidden tie-break favouring one candidate and a writable database, and deleted its users' personal data under regulator scrutiny.

To borrow. Nothing from the matching. The youth editorial workshop as a way to choose what to ask. The Begründungen page (each party's own words, side by side, no score). 3 · Encyclopaedias: what each side has published, no quiz, no score

Who does it best. New Zealand's Policy.nz: over 500,000 people, roughly one in six voters, used it before each of the 2020 and 2023 general elections. It collects party announcements, rewrites each as one plain, consistent sentence, links to the source, lets readers hide or show party names, and says on its About page, in those words, "Are you telling me how to vote?" and answers no. Norway's NRK Partiguiden is "a kind of encyclopaedia": one issue, one question, two to four answer options with the parties sorted across them, sources at the foot, parties allowed to read and correct before publication, and a written note wherever NRK overruled a party. Ballotpedia covers every federal and state race and many local ones, orders candidates by a fixed published rule, and settles disputes by stacking a dated candidate note above the original, never deleting.

What goes wrong. Sustainability and paraphrase. Policy.nz depends on a paid 13-person editorial team and re-fundraising every cycle (it crowdfunded NZ$10,444 to finish 2023); its summaries are paraphrases with the source a click away, so a party can dispute the wording; and its small "favourites" feature carries its own caveat. The UK's Vote for Policies, a blind manifesto matcher with 4 million uses over five elections and the cheapest impartiality mechanism found anywhere (summaries sent to the parties for verification before publication), closed in May 2025 because "we still don't have the technology needed to automate policy tracking so it can be run sustainably" and "current funding of non-partisan services is a fraction of what's needed". Guides.vote in the US adds a one-word verdict ("Mixed", "Mostly yes") above each sourced answer and covers only candidates polling 15% or more.

To borrow. The "Are you telling me how to vote?" section. The plain one-line headline as a scannable label above the verbatim quote, visibly ours. Partiguiden's one-issue-one-question layout for the topic pages. Ballotpedia's dispute rules and [bracket] convention for editorial changes. The parity rule: if any candidate in a race gets only a quotation, every staff-written entry in that race is reduced to a quotation too. What to avoid: the verdict word, the polling threshold, and the hand-built content that killed Vote for Policies.

4 · Record sites: what they did, not what they say

Who does it best. India's MyNeta has published every candidate's sworn affidavit (criminal cases, assets, liabilities, education, tax returns) for over twenty years, labels every field "self-declared", says in writing that the original beats the copy, and adds a "Data Readability Report" when the scan was poor. What goes wrong. Summaries. TheyWorkForYou is twenty years old and still adding caveats: 30 Conservative MPs complained in 2019 and 50 in 2021 that its one-line voting summaries misrepresented them; a vote against a bill thought too weak reads as a vote against its aim; screenshots travel without context. Kenya's Mzalendo scorecard changed MPs' behaviour and is read elsewhere as "sensationalism". MyNeta's sortable "most criminal cases" table is, in practice, a ranking. VoteWatch Europe, fourteen years of MEP records, closed in 2022 and now lives in two people's inboxes.

To borrow. "Self-declared" and "who is responsible for this claim" labels on every quote. The readability flag. The re-contest comparison (what did they say last time?). Abgeordnetenwatch's visible non-response count, which is accountability for responsiveness without any policy score. What the pattern means

Three things decide take-up, and none of them is the size of the site. First, whether taking part is expected: where a profile is the normal thing for a serious candidate to have (Switzerland, Finland, Sweden), most do it; where it is a novelty, one in five does. Second, how much is asked: a short, identical form that asks only for what the candidate has already said in public gets three times the response of a long survey that asks them to take new positions, and asking candidates to commit to yes/no answers that can become attack-ad material is what collapsed Vote Smart. Third, whether a person chases: the League's 61% against Ballotpedia's 19% is the chasing.

Romily's worry is well founded and the data show which way it cuts. Response is not random by party: the big parties control their candidates' messages centrally (Vote Smart's founder said both US parties train their candidates not to answer), while small parties and independents welcome any free, equal platform. So the likely pattern is not "one big party replies and the rest don't" but "small parties and independents reply and the big parties don't", which makes the big parties' cards thinner. That is still a visible imbalance.

The rules every mature guide shares

The same questions for every candidate in a race. A hard length cap (VOTE411 750 characters; California 250 words; Oregon 325). Verbatim publication with no editing for meaning; site edits marked in [brackets]. One content rule, taken from California statute: the statement is about the candidate's own background and intentions and "shall not in any way make reference to other candidates". Identity verified, truth not, and a line saying so. A neutral present-tense label for silence ("The candidate has not yet responded"), never "declined" or "failed". A fixed deadline, then late answers accepted with a visible date. Disputes settled by stacking a dated note above the original, never by deleting. And, in the German and Swedish tools, a public count of who has responded, which is accountability for responsiveness without any policy score.

What transfers to a two-person site with no broadcaster

  1. Profile by default. Build every candidate's page from Democracy Club, leaflets and public records before asking for anything (we already do). The candidate's choice is then between a page made of their published words and the same page with their confirmation on it. This is Abgeordnetenwatch's lever and it costs nothing.
  2. Ask only for what is already public. Corrections and confirmation of quotes, not new positions. This avoids the Vote Smart trap entirely.
  3. Send everything automatically and on the same day. Democracy Club holds an email address for 9 of the 15 Holborn candidates. The morning update can create each link and send each email the day a candidate appears, and one reminder three days later. Sending needs no person; reading replies does.
  4. Make silence a fact, identically for all. "Invited 28 Sep. No reply yet." on every card, or on none. Never a blank that looks like the site's failure.
  5. Keep any reply out of the comparison. A confirmation or correction changes the quoted record; a statement, if allowed, sits in a labelled box under the card and never counts anywhere candidates are compared.
  6. Bulk routes for May 2027. The election agent named on each Statement of Persons Nominated and the local party officers are how SVT reached 97% of 2,167 local parties and smartvote registers whole slates.
  7. Publish take-up by party after each election. It is the only pressure a small site can apply, and it is a fact, not a score.
Or do nothing, honestly

If Romily chooses option A or B on the round-seven page, the site must stop saying it invites candidates, and Democracy Club's own statement route becomes the single path. Both are clean outcomes. What is not clean is the present state: a promise on the submissions page and a "not yet invited" line on every card, with nothing behind either.

08 · Money

The money layer: what the costing bodies teach

"What would this mean for a household like mine" is the idea the site is named for, and no UK institution does it for candidates. The bodies that do it for parties abroad have written down, in their own evaluations, exactly how it goes wrong.

Where ours stands

The grid holds a baseline under 2026 law and under 2024 law for every household band, computed with PolicyEngine UK 2.98.0, with assumptions listed under each figure (band midpoint, 60/40 split for couples, one representative age per band, a representative rent, benefits claimed where entitled). That is the right foundation. Three things are wrong with the layer as shown today. It models two pledges (Reform UK's £15,000 personal allowance; the Green Party's National Insurance upper-limit removal) in one election, and shows them only on the ballot page and only when the profile is complete. It models one party's £15,000 allowance and not another's identical pledge on the same ballot. And the baseline's five lines do not sum to the total shown, because PolicyEngine's net income includes every benefit and every tax it models and the page lists four of them.

Five rules from the Dutch CPB

The Centraal Planbureau has costed manifestos since 1986. Ten parties took part in 2025; the PVV and the SP refused. The SP has called the models "neoliberal", and Pieter Omtzigt, whose party did take part in 2025, once called them a "pseudo-reality". The CPB's own evaluation of that edition, published in April 2026, and its 2018 note on presentation give five rules that transfer directly:

  1. Say what the number is. A median, over a stated period, under a static definition, against a stated baseline. The CPB warned in 2018 "against steering on precise 'median' outcomes, given the uncertainty".
  2. Direction before precision. Its 2026 evaluation records the view that "it is about the direction, not precise figures". Show "about £30 a month more" before £392.
  3. Show the spread, not one figure. Box plots by income group; an explicit note that a positive median does not mean every household gains. Dutch economists add that standard household types hide renters against owners, which our tenure band already captures.
  4. Say what usually matters more. The CPB's explainer states that changes in a person's own life (a new job, a move, a birth) usually outweigh policy. Nibud, the household budget institute, puts it as "Perhaps you get no pay rise at all, or much less, or only next year".
  5. Print "not a voting guide" on the page itself. Page 2 of every edition. Not a footnote.

What the budget offices and calculators add

Australia's Parliamentary Budget Office attaches "a statement about the factors affecting the reliability of the costing estimates" to every costing and rounds to signal precision; Canada's requires "a qualitative statement identifying the sources of uncertainty" and refuses to cost whole platforms or economy-wide effects. The IFS's 2024 calculator states what is in ("three more years of threshold freezes"), what is out (take-up, behaviour, fuel duty), the price year and the funder. The US KFF calculator asks and answers its own question: "Does the calculator provide definitive results for what I will pay? No." The Tax Foundation writes "To be determined" where a plan is unspecified and "should not be used for tax preparation". GOV.UK's benefits page lists who its calculators will be wrong for ("a prisoner, a student, not a British or Irish citizen…"). PolicyEngine itself says "simulations default to static behavior" and publishes the list of measures its UK model cannot express (inheritance tax, ISAs, vehicle duty, prescription charges, fire precepts, all business measures). HM Treasury's transparency record on PolicyEngine found its income-tax results "less promising" in evaluation.

The non-money layer, when it comes

Nobody anywhere converts a pledge into "your waiting time" or "your child's class size" per household, and the CPB dropped its education and housing effects in 2021 because the error risk was too high, with about 125 staff (housing came back in 2025 only as a qualitative arrow). HM Treasury's "benefits in kind" method (allocate public spending by measured use, exclude public goods) is the only established way to put a figure on services. A future layer should start from things already measured locally (school funding per pupil, trust waiting times, council-tax band bills) and describe direction and mechanism, never invent a number.

09 · Evidence and law

The evidence, and the law

What is known about tools like ours, with the confidence each finding deserves, and the statutes that touch the site, read directly rather than summarised from guidance.

What these tools actually do

The best evidence is a meta-analysis of 22 studies with 73,673 participants in nine countries: across all studies, using a voting-advice tool went with higher reported turnout (odds ratio 1.87) and more vote switching (1.44), but when only randomised experiments are counted both effects shrink to something not distinguishable from zero. The people who use such tools were already going to vote. The individual experiments agree: a German field experiment with web tracking (979 people) found no causal effect on turnout or switching but a three-point gain in correctly knowing where parties stand; a 2025 study of 6,501 people across three countries found no turnout effect; a 2025 chatbot study of about 2,900 young unaffiliated Americans found a 13-point knowledge gain on the issues the user cared about and weak or no change in party preference; six coordinated field experiments in five countries (24,007 people) giving voters factual performance information found effects on vote choice of well under one point. One Italian experiment (77,386 voters) found that information about an incumbent's competence, delivered by phone, moved his vote share by roughly three to four points, mailed information alone did nothing, and information about ideology did nothing.

What follows. Measure and claim knowledge, not turnout. Expect the largest effect among the young, the less engaged and weak partisans, which is who the site is for. Give what candidates have done the same weight as what they say, because records move people and words mostly do not. Do not expect, or try to engineer, conversion: a voter who leaves better informed and unchanged is the success case.

Where bias gets in

Not through an algorithm, since we have none, but through the same doors: which topics get a page (the Belgian statement-selection study), how a topic is headed (headers shifted the least sophisticated users' answers by up to half a standard deviation in a live Dutch tool of 27,404 people), and order (being listed first is worth 1.3 to 1.5 points of vote share across 29,076 randomised Californian local contests, and more where information is thin). The Cologne court's 2019 ruling made equal treatment of small parties a legal expectation once a tool is popular. The hostile-media effect means partisans on both sides will judge balanced material as biased against them; what the trust research supports is not neutrality of method but neutrality made visible: sources beside every claim, a "how we check" page, and a one-line statement of who runs and funds the site, which people look at for about four seconds and then trust. Money is the one dimension of British politics the public rates as opaque: 14% think party finance is transparent.

Empty records

Readers do not notice what is missing; they judge confidently on whatever is present (omission neglect). Side by side, the amount of material becomes a proxy for seriousness (evaluability). If a site says plainly that a record is empty, readers discount that candidate. And where records are empty, ballot order and party label do the work. Together these predict that thin records silently disadvantage independents, and that our large numerals make it worse. The design answers that stay inside the rules: state absence explicitly and identically, with the date checked and the sources searched; show the party's position as a labelled fallback, because party is the cue voters actually use; never show counts; keep every card the same size; and offer every candidate the same route to fill the gap.

Personalisation

Registry-matched Swedish data show that personal finances move votes about as much as the national economy once measurement error is removed; British panel data show people respond far more to changes in their own finances that are clearly the government's doing than to changes that are not; Swedish parents of young children shifted their votes on promised child-benefit changes, not delivered ones. The household grid is on exactly that ground. Whether foregrounding self-interest makes people reason more selfishly rests on one 66-person study from 1991 and is contested; the wider literature finds that information changes beliefs far more than preferences and that fairness dominates self-interest in tax attitudes. Phrase it as "a household like yours", show the area and national figures beside it, and expect it to inform rather than convert.

Demand

After the May 2025 English local elections, 49% of voters in electing areas agreed they had enough information about candidates to make an informed choice (YouGov for the Electoral Commission, 2,781 adults). UK trust in news is 30% and falling (Reuters Institute 2026); 52% of Britons disagree that online political information is trustworthy (Electoral Commission 2026); across the 48 countries in the 2026 Digital News Report, 52% of 18 to 24-year-olds get their news mainly from social and video networks. Votes at 16 is not yet law: the Representation of the People Bill passed the Commons on 2 September 2026 and awaits its Lords committee stage, with no commencement date, so 8 October and May 2027 run on the current franchise.

11 · Position

What nobody else does, and what everybody else does

Measured against sixty tools, this is where the site stands. The first list is the reason to keep going; the second is the cheapest way to look like a grown-up site.

Nobody else found does this

  • Shows every ballot candidate on an identical page with every position quoted word for word from a named, dated, linked source. Democracy Club shows statements the candidates wrote; TheyWorkForYou shows votes for sitting MPs only; Policy.nz paraphrases; the matchers show positions someone asserted.
  • Links a per-candidate ballot to a household-level tax and benefit model. The IFS works at party level for three parties; PolicyEngine is a calculator with no ballot; the CPB costs parties, not candidates, and only those that opt in.
  • Keeps an append-only public ledger of every claim, with a hash. Full Fact's corrections log is edited and covers corrections only; Ballotpedia's is private.
  • Puts council meeting agendas, planning applications, school changes and consultations on the same page as the ballot.
  • Refuses on principle to rank, score, match or predict, and says why. Democracy Club does not rank but does not say why; every quiz ranks; Electoral Calculus predicts.
  • Reports its own freshness job by job, and its own accuracy checks including the ones it skipped.

Everybody else does this, and we do not

  • Polling-station lookup (Electoral Commission, Democracy Club, VOTE411, the official pamphlets).
  • Candidate contact details on the page and a write-to-them route (WhoCanIVoteFor, WriteToThem, Abgeordnetenwatch).
  • Named funders with amounts, or a plain statement that there are none (Full Fact, Policy.nz, PolicyEngine).
  • Published usage and satisfaction figures after every election (Democracy Club, SVT, Full Fact).
  • A partner widget actually adopted by councils and newspapers (Democracy Club: 78 councils). Ours exists; take-up is what is missing.
  • Leaflets beside the candidate (WhoCanIVoteFor to ElectionLeaflets).
  • Being indexed by search engines.
The one sentence

The field splits into list-makers that are trusted but shallow, matchers that are engaging but opaque and mostly dead or hobbyist in the UK, and analysts that are rigorous but stop at the party or the sitting MP. What's It To Me? sits in the empty space between them. The risks are the ones that killed Vote for Policies (hand curation that does not scale) and that dog TheyWorkForYou (context stripped by sharers). The defences are already in the design: automation, quotes with sources, the ledger, and the refusal to rank. What remains is to make the surface as true as the foundations.
