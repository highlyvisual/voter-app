# 4. Councillors: named votes, and what else we can fairly show

**Date:** 24 September 2026

**Question:** When must councils record how each councillor voted, how often do they do it, is there any dataset of those votes, and what else could we fairly show for a councillor?

**Summary**
1. In England, the law has required councils to record every councillor's name on budget and council tax votes since 25 February 2014. Otherwise, a named vote usually only happens if councillors ask for one. We found no rule like the budget one in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland.
2. Practice varies a lot. In our sample of 20 councils (a sample, not a survey), some record names on most contested votes (Cardiff, Highland, Aberdeen, Fife, Kent). Many record names only on the budget or when asked. We found no dataset of council votes anywhere in the UK.
3. We can collect attendance and committee membership from the Modern.gov web service. The register of interests and allowances must be published by law, but mostly as web pages or PDFs. Each of these has fairness problems, and we will need to explain them on the page.

## (a) What the law says

### England: the budget rule (SI 2014/165)

Checked on legislation.gov.uk, 24 September 2026.

| Point | What the law says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The Local Authorities (Standing Orders) (England) (Amendment) Regulations 2014. It changes the Local Authorities (Standing Orders) (England) Regulations 2001. | [legislation.gov.uk, SI 2014/165 as made](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/made) |
| Since when | Made 29 January 2014. In force from **25 February 2014**. | [same](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/made) |
| The rule (exact words) | "Immediately after any vote is taken at a budget decision meeting of an authority there must be recorded in the minutes of the proceedings of that meeting the names of the persons who cast a vote for the decision or against the decision or who abstained from voting." | [same, reg. 2(3)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/made) |
| Which decisions | A "budget decision meeting" is a meeting where the council makes the council tax calculations under the Local Government Finance Act 1992, or issues a precept (a county council asking district councils to collect its share of council tax). It includes any meeting where either item is on the agenda. "Vote" means a vote on any decision "related to" that calculation or precept. | [same](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/made) |
| Amendments too? | Yes. The government's note says the rule covers "the substantive budget motion ... and any amendments proposed at the budget meeting". | [Explanatory Memorandum to SI 2014/165, DCLG, 2014, para 8.2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/pdfs/uksiem_20140165_en.pdf) |
| Which councils | The rule sits in standing orders that every "relevant authority" must adopt. A relevant authority means "a county council, a district council or a London borough council". Unitary and metropolitan councils are legally county or district councils, so they are covered. The rule is written for mayor-and-cabinet, leader-and-cabinet and committee-system councils alike. | [2001 Regulations, reg. 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2001/3384/regulation/2); [SI 2014/165 reg. 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/made) |
| What is recorded | Names of councillors voting **for**, **against**, and **abstaining**. Absent councillors are not mentioned, although some councils list them anyway. | [SI 2014/165](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/made) |
| Why | "With recorded votes, electors and local tax payers will be able to see how those who represent them voted on the issue of the local tax." | [Explanatory Memorandum, para 7.1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/165/pdfs/uksiem_20140165_en.pdf) |

In plain words: at the yearly budget and council tax meeting, every English council must write down in its minutes how each councillor voted, including on amendments.

### England and Wales: any councillor can have their own vote recorded

This is an older rule, and many people don't know about it.

- **England:** the Local Authorities (Standing Orders) Regulations 1993 make councils include this in their standing orders: "Where immediately after a vote is taken at a meeting of a relevant body any member of that body so requires, there shall be recorded in the minutes ... whether that person cast his vote for the question or against the question or whether he abstained from voting." It applies to the full council, committees, sub-committees and joint committees. [legislation.gov.uk, SI 1993/202, Sch. 2 para 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1993/202/made). The 2001 Regulations removed other parts of the 1993 Regulations for England, but not this one ([SI 2001/3384 reg. 8](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2001/3384/regulation/8/made)). legislation.gov.uk warns that some changes to the 1993 text have not yet been applied, so "still in force in England" is our reading, not something we could fully confirm.
- **This records one person's vote, not everyone's.** A councillor can only have their *own* vote recorded.
- **Wales:** the same rule appears, with almost the same words, in the Local Authorities (Standing Orders) (Wales) Regulations 2006 (in force 3 July 2006). It covers county and county borough councils. [legislation.gov.uk, WSI 2006/1275, Sch. 2 para 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/wsi/2006/1275)

### "A set number of members can demand a recorded vote"

We found **no national law that sets this number**. Each council picks its own number in its constitution. Examples we read are in the table below: 1, 2, 6 and 10 councillors. A named vote of the whole meeting (a "recorded vote" or "roll call") depends entirely on these local rules. The one exception is England's budget rule.

The only national rule on recording names is about *attendance*, not votes:
- England and Wales: "The names of the members present at a meeting of a local authority shall be recorded." [Local Government Act 1972, Sch. 12 para 40](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1972/70/schedule/12)
- Scotland: "The names of the members present at a meeting of a council shall be recorded." [Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973, Sch. 7 para 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/65/schedule/7)

| Scotland | We found none. | Decisions are made by a majority of members "present and voting". Names of those present must be recorded. Each council's standing orders decide how votes are taken. | [Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973, Sch. 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/65/schedule/7) |

**Patterns (from the sample only):**
- **England:** named votes on the budget are guaranteed. Other votes depend on local rules, where the number of councillors needed to ask ranges from 1 (for your own vote only) to 10. Kent and Hartlepool record names more widely.
- **Scotland:** roll-call and electronic voting are common, and three of the four readable councils publish names routinely. Glasgow is the exception: it keeps the names but did not publish them in the 2022 minutes.
- **Wales and Northern Ireland:** names are mostly recorded on request, often by just one member. Cardiff published names on every vote in the minutes we checked.
- **Electronic voting systems usually capture each person's vote.** Whether the council *publishes* that record is a choice. Oldham's system, for example, captures votes but does not keep them unless someone asks.

## What this means for the site

1. **The fairest named vote everywhere in England is the budget vote.** Since 25 Feb 2014, every English council must record names on the budget and council tax vote and its amendments. For a sitting English councillor we could show "How they voted on the [year] budget and council tax", with a link to the minutes. This works the same way for every English council, so it doesn't favour anyone. Wales, Scotland and NI have no such guarantee.
2. **Show other named votes only where the council records them, and say why they are missing.** For example: "This council records names only when six councillors ask" or "This council records names on every vote". A missing record must never read as a bad mark against the person.
3. **For now, votes would have to be entered by hand, with a source link for every one.** There is no dataset. The web service has no votes. Minutes vary between PDF tables, HTML text and separate documents, and they contain errors (the Southwark example). For the Holborn and St Pancras pilot, Camden blocks automated access, so any Camden record would need manual checking. Scottish councils such as Aberdeen, Highland, Fife and Cardiff in Wales publish names more often, so automation could be tried there later.
4. **Attendance is available by machine, but how we present it is a judgement for Romily and Barny.** Options include the raw numbers ("expected at X meetings, present at Y, apologies Z") with the date range and a short note on why people miss meetings, a percentage, or not showing attendance at all. Each choice affects how a person comes across. Whichever is chosen should be the same for every candidate.
5. **Link to the register of interests instead of summarising it.** Explain that some entries are withheld by law for safety.
6. **Allowances: show the published totals, and think carefully about the carers' allowance line.** Publishing it by name is lawful in England, but it reveals family circumstances. Leaving it out is a judgement call for the team.
7. **Candidates who are not councillors will have none of this.** The page needs a neutral "No council record, because this candidate has not been a councillor" message so newcomers don't look worse than people already in office.
