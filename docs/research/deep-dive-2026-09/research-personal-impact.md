# "What does this mean for my household?" — tools, official costings and the evidence on personalised impact

Deep dive for What's It To Me? (whatsittome.org). Written 25 September 2026. All URLs accessed 25 September 2026 unless stated.

## 0. How to read this

Each section separates **OBSERVED** (what a source says, with the source) from **JUDGE** (my opinion for the site). Where I could not confirm a detail against a source, it is marked **unverified**. The session's web-search allowance ran out part-way through, so the second half relies on direct fetches of known pages; a few pages (Sky News, This is Money, JSTOR, gov.ie 2024 costings, Scottish Government distributional analysis, Institut Montaigne) were blocked or returned errors and are noted as such. Nothing below was invented to fill a gap.

The site's money layer is a precomputed PolicyEngine UK grid, with assumptions listed, applied to household facts entered in bands and kept in the browser. That is the lens for every lesson here.

## 1. Official and semi-official manifesto costing

### 1.1 Netherlands — CPB "Keuzes in Kaart" (Charted Choices)

**OBSERVED.**

*What it is.* The Centraal Planbureau (CPB, the government's independent economic analysis bureau) invites every party with seats to submit its manifesto for costing before a general election. The 2027–2030 edition was published 10 October 2025 for the 29 October 2025 election; ten parties took part (GroenLinks–PvdA, VVD, NSC, D66, BBB, CDA, SGP, CU, Volt, JA21). The CPB states on the front page: "Keuzes in Kaart is geen kieswijzer, maar maakt een onderlinge vergelijking tussen partijen mogelijk" ("Keuzes in Kaart is not a voting guide, but makes a comparison between parties possible"), p. 2. Source: CPB, "Keuzes in Kaart 2027-2030" (PDF), https://www.cpb.nl/system/files/cpbmedia/CPB-Keuzes-in-Kaart-2027-2030.pdf and the English landing page https://www.cpb.nl/en/publication/charted-choices-2027-2030.

*How parties opt in.* The rules are set out in advance in a "startnotitie" (starting note). For 2027–2030: parties confirm participation by 18 July 2025 and submit measures on a standard sheet by 23 July 2025, 12:00; the CPB applies a budget floor of €0.2 billion per measure (so small measures are ignored); it will not cost proposals that depend on local or European authorities or private parties, nor measures that plainly conflict with EU law. "Wij behandelen de informatie die wij krijgen van politieke partijen strikt vertrouwelijk" ("We treat the information we receive from political parties strictly confidentially"). Purchasing power is shown as a box plot of the median and spread across five income quintiles, as the annual average effect over 2027–2030, not year by year. "Eerste-orde gedragseffecten" (first-order behavioural effects) are included using standard elasticities per measure type, but macroeconomic feedback is not. "De doorrekening beoogt dan ook geen stemwijzer te zijn" ("The costing is therefore not intended to be a voting guide"), p. 3. Source: CPB, "Startnotitie Keuzes in Kaart 2027-2030" (PDF), https://www.cpb.nl/system/files/cpbmedia/CPB-publicatie-startnotitie-keuzes-in-kaart-2027-2030.pdf. The 2025–2028 edition (published 8 November 2023, eight parties) used the same €200m floor, up from €100m in 2021, and said: "the calculations are not intended to provide voting advice." Source: CPB, "Charted Choices 2025-2028", https://www.cpb.nl/en/charted-choices-2025-2028.

*Koopkrachtplaatjes (purchasing-power pictures).* These are the tables of income change by household type or income group that Dutch media lift from the CPB report. In 2018 the CPB changed how it presents them, saying "De presentatie van koopkrachteffecten kan veel invloed hebben op politieke discussies" ("The presentation of purchasing-power effects can strongly influence political discussion") and warning "tegen het sturen op precieze 'mediane' uitkomsten, gelet op de onzekerheid rond het ramen van de koopkracht" ("against steering on precise 'median' outcomes, given the uncertainty in forecasting purchasing power"). Source: CPB, "Toelichting aanpassingen presentatie koopkrachtramingen CPB", 16 August 2018, https://www.cpb.nl/publicatie/toelichting-aanpassingen-presentatie-koopkrachtramingen-cpb. The Nibud (national household budget institute) publishes its own plaatjes for 117 example households and tells readers plainly why theirs will differ: "Misschien krijgt u wel helemaal geen loonsverhoging, of veel minder, of pas volgend jaar" ("Perhaps you get no pay rise at all, or much less, or only next year"); the models are static and exclude life events such as job loss or moving house; price forecasts are the least reliable input. Source: Nibud, "Over koopkrachtplaatjes", https://www.nibud.nl/consumenten/over-koopkrachtplaatjes/.

*The 2023 refusals.* Ahead of the November 2023 election NSC (Omtzigt), BBB, SP, PVV, Partij voor de Dieren, Denk and Forum voor Democratie did not submit. SP leader Lilian Marijnissen called the CPB's models "neoliberale modellen" in which her party's ideas "automatically come out badly" and said "We want an ideas debate, not an accounting debate." Omtzigt said the CPB works with a "pseudo-reality" that parties can manipulate. The article also reports that parties have learned to pick measures that score well in the model. Source: NOS, "Steeds minder partijen laten hun plannen doorrekenen door het CPB", 7 November 2023, https://nos.nl/l/2497028. In 2025 five parties (PVV, SP, Denk, PvdD, FVD) again stayed out; a viewer-reaction piece on the 2025 tables shows the public reading the tables as "who pays? healthcare!" and doubting the numbers when they differed from the parties' own programmes. Source: MAX Meldpunt, 20 October 2025, https://www.maxmeldpunt.nl/politiek/de-doorgerekende-programmas-bij-de-verkiezingen-en-bij-wie-halen-ze-het-weg-bij-de-zorg-waarom-niet-bij-de-rijken/.

*What the CPB's own evaluations say.* The evaluation of the 2018–2021 edition found the process had become "too risky" through scope creep, with high error potential; the CPB narrowed scope (dropped education, innovation and housing effects), raised the floor to €100m, and moved climate and nature effects to the PBL's separate publication. Source: CPB, "Evaluatie Keuzes in Kaart 2018-2021", https://www.cpb.nl/publicatie/evaluatie-keuzes-in-kaart-2018-2021. The evaluation of the 2027–2030 edition (published 9 April 2026, interviews with nine parties, five ministries, NOS and FD journalists, and two outside experts) reports: media worry that "niet alle partijen nemen deel" makes an "imperfecte vergelijking" (p. 4) and that quantified effects get disproportionate attention over qualitative ones; parties "voelden analyses soms als een black box" (p. 5) yet accepted that full transparency would invite gaming; arrow-based summary symbols are "niet onomstotelijk, maar wel bepalend voor hoe de uitkomsten worden gelezen" (not beyond dispute, but decisive for how results are read, p. 5); "het gaat om de richting en niet om precieze cijfers" ("it is about the direction, not precise figures", p. 5); some interviewees "misten de koopkrachteffecten uitgesplitst naar huishoudgroepen" (missed purchasing-power effects split by household group, p. 5); the CPB assesses whether measures are possible "in beginsel", not on what timescale, and gives the example of a party "freezing" the health basic package, which experts called "onrealistisch"; tax rises after 2030 are invisible, so parties can hide costs beyond the horizon (p. 7). The main recommendation: "Communiceer nog actiever over de waarde en beperkingen van KiK" (p. 7). Source: CPB, "Evaluatie Keuzes in Kaart 2027-2030" (PDF), https://www.cpb.nl/system/files/cpbmedia/CPB_publicatie-Evaluatie-Keuzes-in-Kaart-2027-2030.pdf.

*Academic view.* Wilbert Bolhuis's Leiden thesis (2018) traces nine editions (1986–2017) and finds the exercise disciplines parties into concrete, costable proposals (83% of the Rutte II coalition measures appeared in both governing parties' costed manifestos), but "it remains questionable whether many voters actually read, understand and base their votes on published results"; he notes budget numbers are more reliable than macro projections yet both get equal weight in public debate, and that the models may crowd out radical alternatives. Source: W. Bolhuis, "Van woord tot akkoord", Leiden University, 2018, https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2971958/view.

**JUDGE.** The Dutch system is the closest thing in the world to a national "what's it to me" for manifestos, and its own evaluations are the best free consultancy the site will get. Three findings matter most: presentation choices (arrows, medians) drive how numbers are read; parties that are missing make the comparison lopsided and the media notice; and the CPB's answer to false precision is not to stop publishing numbers but to say "direction, not precise figures" and to communicate limits "even more actively".

### 1.2 Belgium — Federal Planning Bureau "DC2024"

**OBSERVED.** The Bureau fédéral du Plan / Federaal Planbureau costs the priority measures submitted by the twelve parties in the Chamber, publishing results one month before the election because "de wet voorschrijft" (the law prescribes) it. Indicators: growth, public finances, employment, purchasing power, mobility, electricity. Source: https://www.dc2024.be/index_nl.html. The legal basis is a 2014 law and the exercise is compulsory for the Bureau but the parties choose what to submit — **unverified** (the summary page at plan.be returned 403).

**JUDGE.** Belgium shows a costing duty can be written into law without forcing parties to submit everything; the same gap ("what the party chose to submit") will exist for any candidate-level tool.

### 1.3 Ireland — Department of Finance costing facility

**OBSERVED.** The Department of Finance and the Department of Public Expenditure offer confidential costings to political parties before Budgets and general elections and publish the costings they supplied "after the enactment of the Finance Bill or formation of Government". Source: gov.ie, "Political Party Costings 2026", https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-finance/publications/political-party-costings-2026/. The Institute for Government's case study explains: in practice only opposition parties use it; it costs individual proposals (mainly tax and welfare), never whole manifestos; requests and responses are confidential and exempt from freedom-of-information law; officials cannot handle "transformational" or cross-departmental policies and may apply conservative assumptions; each policy is costed "in isolation" with no interaction effects; parties can release selectively and present results under favourable assumptions. It quotes an observer that "some of the more extreme possibilities would tend to disappear because [parties] know that they wouldn't get any sense of endorsement." Source: Institute for Government, "The Irish Facility for Party Policy Costings", https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/publications/final%20-%20Ireland_0.pdf. In the 2024 campaign the Irish Times compared totals (Fine Gael €34.3bn, Fianna Fáil €38.7bn, Sinn Féin €49.3bn) and quoted economist Barra Roantree asking how extra capital spending could be delivered "without (further) pushing up prices in the construction sector", and noted roughly €15bn a year of corporation tax is "windfall". Source: Irish Times, 23 November 2024, https://www.irishtimes.com/politics/2024/11/23/costing-the-manifestos-making-sense-of-billions-being-thrown-out-by-the-parties-to-win-your-vote/.

**JUDGE.** Ireland demonstrates the weakness of policy-by-policy costing with no household view and no interactions. Its one strong lesson is the disciplining effect: knowing something will be costed makes parties drop the wildest ideas.

### 1.4 Australia — Parliamentary Budget Office

**OBSERVED.** The PBO must publish an Election Commitments Report "within 30 days of the end of the caretaker period or seven days prior to the first sitting day of the new parliament"; the Coalition, Labor and the Greens (parties with five or more members when the election is called) are included by law, others may opt in; costings cover the forward estimates (four years) and a medium term (budget year plus ten); during the caretaker period costing requests and responses "must be publicly released". It warns "The total cost of a party's election platform could differ from the sum of all individual components... as policies can interact with each other." Source: PBO, "About election commitments reports", https://www.pbo.gov.au/elections/about-election-commitments-reports. The 2025 methodology says costings "take into account the expected direct behavioural responses... wherever those responses are likely to have a significant impact", that "Broader economic impacts are generally not included in costings because their magnitude and timing are highly uncertain", that "Some individual costings are only slightly affected by changes in economic parameters, while others are very sensitive", and that an infrastructure costing "is simply an estimate of the cost of the project" and "not: A cost-benefit analysis". Source: PBO, "Costing election commitments" (2025), https://www.pbo.gov.au/elections/2025-general-election/2025-election-commitments-report/report-requirements-and-methodology/costing-election-commitments. Its explainer says every costing minute carries "A statement about the factors affecting the reliability of the costing estimates", uses rounding to reflect precision, and for one tax costing "assumed that individuals with incomes in excess of $180,000 have a taxable income elasticity of 0.2" while excluding labour-supply change "due to uncertainty". Source: PBO, "What is a Parliamentary Budget Office costing?", https://www.pbo.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-01/What%20is%20a%20Parliamentary%20Budget%20Office%20Costing%20PDF.pdf. The 2025 report foreword (Sam Reinhardt, 20 June 2025) says it "includes full costings of all publicly announced commitments with a material impact on the budget". Source: https://www.pbo.gov.au/elections/2025-general-election/2025-election-commitments-report/foreword.

**JUDGE.** Two habits worth copying: a plain reliability statement attached to every number, and rounding as a signal of precision. Note the Australian report lands after the election, so it informs accountability more than the vote.

### 1.5 Canada — Parliamentary Budget Officer

**OBSERVED.** Section 79.21 of the Parliament of Canada Act lets recognised parties and independent MPs ask the PBO to "estimate the financial cost of any campaign proposal" during the 120 days before a fixed election date or once Parliament is dissolved. The PBO "Will not cost full campaign platforms – only proposals". Source: PBO, "Legislative Mandate", https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/epc-legislative-mandate--mandat-legislatif-du-cpe. Its 2025 guide: requests are confidential; "When PBO receives written notification that the campaign proposal has been made public, the PBO will publish the costing"; each note "will contain a qualitative statement identifying the sources of uncertainty in the estimate"; horizon is the current year plus five; "PBO will not incorporate impacts from the policy to the economy in individual cost estimates"; and "The PBO has a legislative mandate to cost proposals, rather than platforms." Source: PBO, "Costing Election Proposals for the 45th Canadian Federal Election", https://distribution-a617274656661637473.pbo-dpb.ca/b8441f9fba4284af9b1e8ab7bf4c11fde44dff291fd6f42e9214915b86576f37. In the April 2025 campaign the Liberals, Conservatives and NDP used the service but none committed to releasing full platforms before the leaders' debates; former PBO Kevin Page said "sooner rather than later is better, and before the debate... is best." Source: Globe and Mail, https://www.theglobeandmail.com/politics/federal-election/article-parties-using-pbo-to-cost-promises-but-have-not-pledged-to-release/.

**JUDGE.** Canada's "qualitative uncertainty statement per estimate" is the lightest-weight honest way to flag confidence, and fits a per-policy page better than numeric ranges the model cannot really support.

### 1.6 United Kingdom — IFS, Resolution Foundation, HM Treasury and the OBR debate

**OBSERVED.**

*IFS.* The IFS has analysed manifestos at every election since 1997 and ran a "Be the Chancellor" tool in 2024 ("Change spending plans, set tax policies and see how your choices impact borrowing and debt"). Sources: https://ifs.org.uk/microsite/election-2024 and https://ifs.org.uk/be-the-chancellor. Paul Johnson's 2024 opening remarks: "The 'conspiracy of silence' about all of this has been maintained" and "You can't pledge to end all waits of more than 18 weeks, allocate no money to that pledge, and then claim to have a fully costed manifesto." Source: IFS, Paul Johnson's Opening Remarks, June 2024, https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/Paul%20Johnson's%20Opening%20Remarks.pdf; reported by ITV News, 24 June 2024, https://www.itv.com/news/2024-06-24/ifs-accuses-labour-and-tories-of-conspiracy-of-silence-over-manifestoes. Its distributional charts (2015 example) show average gain or loss by income decile and by household type (working-age with children, without, pensioners), with stated caveats: the analysis "assumes full take-up of means-tested benefits and tax credits" and shows the "direct impact of tax and benefit changes on household incomes, ignoring the fact that the economy might have evolved differently". Source: IFS, "Distributional Analysis", Election 2015, https://ifs.org.uk/election-2015/distributional-analysis. On council tax (the one local tax anyone models at household level), the IFS 2020 report modelled gains and losses by region, income, age, tenure and disability but used regional rather than local-authority data "due to small sample sizes", and said of capitalisation into house prices "The scale of these impacts is highly uncertain." Source: IFS, "Revaluation and reform: bringing council tax in England into the 21st century", 18 March 2020, https://ifs.org.uk/publications/revaluation-and-reform-bringing-council-tax-england-21st-century.

*Resolution Foundation.* Its Budget 2025 commentary (28 November 2025) phrases impacts as "For a typical earner, £220", "Families with three or more children: over half a million will gain £5,310 on average in 2029-30", and warns that "family type matters for whether the poorest gain or lose". Source: https://www.resolutionfoundation.org/comment/budget-2025-special/. Its reports carry data caveats, e.g. Labour Force Survey data "shown to have significant issues with data quality and weighting". Source: RF, "Hard Lessons", 9 November 2024, https://www.resolutionfoundation.org/publications/hard-lessons/. I did not find a stand-alone RF 2024 manifesto household analysis in the pages reachable today — **unverified** whether one exists.

*HM Treasury "Impact on households".* The Treasury's distributional analysis allocates public-service spending ("benefits in kind" such as health and education) to income deciles using survey data on use (e.g. GP visits) or regression-estimated probabilities of use; it excludes administration, most capital spending, and public goods "because it is not possible to identify the direct benefits from these areas of spending for specific households", and assumes "full compliance with the rules of the welfare system". Source: HM Treasury, "Impact on households: distributional analysis to accompany Budget 2018", https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/752045/impact_on_households_distributional_analysis_to_accompany_budget_2018_web.pdf.

*The OBR.* The OBR's FAQ: asked whether it can cost opposition policies, "No, that would require a change in the law. Subsection 5(iii)(b) of The Budget Responsibility and National Audit Act 2011 states that the OBR must not consider alternative policies". It shows forecast uncertainty with fan charts of "20 per cent probability bands... based on the size and distribution of past official forecast errors". Source: OBR FAQs, https://obr.uk/faqs/. The Institute for Government argued in 2015 that "With more accurate costings voters could make more informed decisions" and that parties could submit policies "using their own assumptions – and then have those costings certified". Source: R. Munro, IfG, 23 April 2015, https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/article/comment/know-costs-manifesto-promises-obr-should-be-first-place-we-look. Gemma Tetlow (IfG, 19 November 2019) set out the case for and against: equal credibility for all parties versus the need for far more staff (OBR about 30 then; CPB about 125), data access, earlier manifestos and a risk to perceived independence. Source: https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/article/comment/asking-obr-cost-manifestos-could-make-sense-would-be-complicated. The IEA's Booth and Bourne (26 June 2014) opposed it: "Presenting costings based on contestable assumptions as if they were an objective truth would stifle debate", and called it a "managerialist, technocratic approach". Source: https://iea.org.uk/blog/why-the-obr-shouldn%E2%80%99t-audit-the-manifestos-of-political-parties.

**JUDGE.** The UK has no official household-level manifesto costing and is unlikely to get one before May 2027. That leaves the site doing, for one constituency at a time, something no UK institution does. The IFS's caveat wording and the HMT benefits-in-kind method are the two most re-usable UK assets: the first for how to write assumptions, the second as the only established way to put a pound figure on public services per household.

## 2. Household calculators and "how it affects you" tools

### 2.1 PolicyEngine UK and US

**OBSERVED.**

*Legal status and money.* "PolicyEngine is a nonprofit building free, open-source tools", donating "through our fiscal sponsor, the PSL Foundation" (Columbia, South Carolina); donations are tax-deductible in the US. Source: https://policyengine.org/uk/donate. Listed supporters include Arnold Ventures ($273,525 May 2025; $94,800 July 2024; $85,200 December 2023), the Nuffield Foundation (£251,296, September 2024, "Enhancing, localising and democratising tax-benefit policy analysis"), MyFriendBen ($300,000 October 2024; $25,000 January 2025), the US National Science Foundation ($300,000 August 2025), NEO Philanthropy ($200,000 October 2024), Pritzker Children's Initiative ($150,000 December 2025) and the Gerald Huff Fund for Humanity ($50,000 August 2024, a fund promoting universal basic income). Source: https://policyengine.org/uk/supporters. The Nuffield grant (September 2024–September 2025, led by Max Ghenis) funds "creating micro-datasets representative of each local authority and constituency". Source: https://www.nuffieldfoundation.org/project/enhancing-localising-and-democratising-tax-benefit-policy-analysis.

*Method.* The model combines encoded policy rules ("Over 50 tax and benefit programs across HMRC, DWP, DfE, Treasury, and devolved administrations"), a representative microdata sample, and optional behavioural elasticities; steps are "Start with households… Calculate baseline taxes… Calculate reform taxes… Model behavioural responses… Weight and aggregate". Sources: https://policyengine.org/uk/model and https://policyengine.org/uk/model/rules/coverage. "PolicyEngine simulations default to static behavior. These CBO elasticity estimates are optional presets for sensitivity analysis, and all parameters are fully adjustable" (income elasticity central −0.05; substitution elasticities 0.22–0.31 by decile; capital gains −0.79 persistent). Source: https://policyengine.org/uk/model/behavioral. The November 2024 methodology post adds that no UK-specific capital-gains elasticity study exists, so US evidence was adapted, and that "By default, the PolicyEngine web app assumes no behavioural responses." Source: V. Ahmadi, "How PolicyEngine UK models behavioural responses", 12 November 2024, https://www.policyengine.org/uk/research/behavioural-responses. Validation is documented against NBER's TAXSIM (a US model); UK calibration targets are "currently available for the US model only". Sources: https://policyengine.org/uk/model/data/validation and https://policyengine.org/uk/model/data/calibration. The Python package documentation calls it "a microsimulation model of the UK tax and benefit system" using the Enhanced FRS 2022–23 and says the docs are "a work in progress". Source: https://policyengine.github.io/policyengine-uk/. The software paper is Ahmadi, Ghenis, Woodruff, Makarchuk and Volk, "policyengine: A Microsimulation Tool for Tax-Benefit Policy Analysis", Journal of Open Source Software 11(125), 14 September 2026, https://joss.theoj.org/papers/34b9f72df98e586d7e6a2448922407e7.

*Government use.* HM Treasury's algorithmic transparency record (17 December 2024) describes PolicyEngine UK as under exploration, not in operational use; it lists data sources (FRS enhanced with SPI, LCF, WAS and ETB, with machine-learning imputation and reweighting) and risks: income tax results were "less promising" in evaluation, plus "erroneous operation of the code" and "incorrect parameters or input data". Source: https://gov.uk/algorithmic-transparency-records/hmt-modelling-policy-engine. No 10's data science team built "10ds-microsim" on PolicyEngine to "rapidly estimate the impacts of policy reforms on living standards, local area incomes, and distributional outcomes"; co-founder Nikhil Woodruff spent six months there as an Innovation Fellow. Source: M. Ghenis, 20 January 2026, https://www.policyengine.org/us/research/policyengine-10-downing-street.

*What it cannot model.* PolicyEngine's own "scorecard" repository records "pe_gap rows" for measures "PolicyEngine's UK model cannot currently express". For Autumn Budget 2025 (26 November 2025) that list includes electric-vehicle duty, cash ISA limits, gambling duties, Motability VAT, inheritance-tax reforms (no estates module), homeworking relief, prescription-charge freeze, fire precepts, and "All business-side measures"; among options others scored: CGT uplift at death, pension lump sums, NI on investment income, non-dom reforms, sugar and salt levies. Source: GitHub issue #136, opened 24 September 2026, https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-scorecard/issues/136. A separate issue notes vehicle excise duty is not modelled. Source: https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-uk/issues/1874.

*Privacy.* "PolicyEngine does not store personally identifiable information. We do use Google Analytics… When you create a household or policy reform, PolicyEngine stores an anonymous ID for it in the page URL". Source: https://policyengine.org/uk/privacy.

*Uncertainty.* I found no confidence intervals or ranges on PolicyEngine's household or population outputs on the pages reachable today; the only uncertainty handling is the optional elasticity "lower/central/upper" presets. **Unverified** whether any product page shows ranges.

**JUDGE.** PolicyEngine is the right engine for the site — open, documented, used inside No 10 — but three things must be said on the site: it is static unless told otherwise; it cannot model a long, specific list of policies (and that list is public, so quote it); and the Treasury itself found some parts "less promising". The funder list is public and includes a UBI-promoting fund; a hostile reader will find it, so the site should link it first.

### 2.2 Nibud (Netherlands)

**OBSERVED.** See 1.1: 117 example households, static, explicit reasons your result differs, honest ranking of which inputs are least reliable (prices). Source: https://www.nibud.nl/consumenten/over-koopkrachtplaatjes/.

**JUDGE.** The Nibud page is the best model I found of a plain-language "why your number will differ" note.

### 2.3 KFF Health Insurance Marketplace Calculator (US)

**OBSERVED.** Inputs: state, zip code, yearly household income (dollars or % of poverty level), whether employer coverage is available, family size, adults and children enrolling. Wording: "The calculator is intended to show you an estimate of how much you may pay and the amount of financial help you may be eligible for"; "there are several reasons why your calculator results may not match your actual tax credit amount"; "Not all plans are available in all parts of the county, so actual premiums may vary"; and a self-posed FAQ: "Does the calculator provide definitive results for what I will pay? No." No calculator-specific privacy note; links to the general privacy policy. Source: https://www.kff.org/interactive/subsidy-calculator/.

**JUDGE.** The question-and-answer caveat ("Does it give definitive results? No.") is the clearest single device seen in any tool.

### 2.4 Tax Foundation and Penn Wharton Budget Model (US)

**OBSERVED.** Tax Foundation's 2024 election tracker listed Trump and Harris proposals by tax type and marked unknowns "To be determined"; it did not offer a household calculator for the two plans and said "We will continue to update the tracker as candidates issue more detailed tax plans". Source: https://taxfoundation.org/research/federal-tax/2024-tax-plans/. Its separate "2026 Tax Calculator" exists (link on that page) — details **unverified**. PWBM's home page lists interactive tools (Social Security provisions, corporate tax line items, a real-time budget tracker, a tariff simulator, DataView); no household-level "your taxes under candidate X" tool appears there; its debt work uses probability language ("a 25% chance of reaching it in 14 years"). Source: https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/.

**JUDGE.** The US think-tanks mostly publish by income percentile, not per household, and say "to be determined" rather than guess. That is the honest default for any candidate position without enough detail.

### 2.5 UK media "budget calculators" and explainers

**OBSERVED.** The BBC's 26 November 2025 Budget piece is an explainer ("Isas, cars and pensions: How the Budget affects you"), not a calculator; it walks through measures with hedged phrasing such as a milkshake sugar levy that "may push up prices, or lead to ingredient changes". Source: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5y2g2qn0eyo. Sky News ("Budget 2025 calculator: see how your finances have changed") and This is Money ("How the Budget will cost YOU: detailed calculator shows you the EXACT impact") both published calculators on Budget day 2025; their pages were blocked to this session, so their inputs and caveats are **unverified**. The This is Money headline's word "EXACT" is observable in its URL. Which?'s pre-Budget 2026 piece (21 September 2026) says: "While nothing is officially confirmed until Budget day itself, here's a breakdown of the key rumours… Remember not to make major financial decisions based on speculation alone." Source: https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/autumn-budget-2026-when-is-it-and-what-will-it-contain-aQqC47O24jnl. MoneySavingExpert maintains a generic income-tax calculator (https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/tax-calculator/); its Budget-day explainers could not be located in this session — **unverified**. Guardian and Telegraph calculators — **unverified** (pages not reachable).

**JUDGE.** Media calculators sell precision ("EXACT") because it drives clicks; the site should do the opposite and say so. The one media habit worth copying is Which?'s explicit "don't act on speculation" line, adapted to "a manifesto pledge is not a law".

### 2.6 Benefit calculators as input UX (entitledto, Turn2us, Policy in Practice)

**OBSERVED.** GOV.UK says: "You can use an independent, free and anonymous benefits calculator to check what you could be entitled to", lists what you need ("savings, income, including your partner's (from payslips, for example), existing benefits and pensions… outgoings (such as rent, mortgage, childcare payments), Council Tax bill"), and states plainly who the calculators fail: "They will not give accurate results if you're a prisoner, a student, not a British or Irish citizen, on strike, living outside the UK, living permanently in residential care or a nursing home". Source: https://www.gov.uk/benefits-calculators. Turn2us describes its tool as "free" and for checking "which means-tested benefits you may be entitled to"; it is run by Elizabeth Finn Care, registered charity 207812 / SC040987. Source: https://www.turn2us.org.uk/get-support/benefits-calculator. entitledto's and Policy in Practice's own pages sit behind bot protection; their exact input flows are **unverified** from this session. PolicyEngine lists "EntitledTo Ltd" as a peer model. Source: https://policyengine.org/uk/model/rules/coverage.

**JUDGE.** The benefit calculators ask for exact figures because they need them; the site asks bands because it does not. GOV.UK's "will not give accurate results if you are…" list is the pattern to copy for the site's exclusions (students, non-UK citizens, care-home residents, and so on).

### 2.7 Local-level and non-money tools (the almost-empty categories)

**OBSERVED.**

*Council tax and local services.* Fairer Share Campaign CIC (company 12334999) runs a postcode calculator (postcode, property type, owner or renter) showing what a proportional property tax would mean for that address, with the claim "75% of UK households will save!" and no methodology on the page. Source: https://fairershare.org.uk/ (calculator at /counciltaxcalculator/). The IFS 2020 council tax work is the rigorous version (see 1.6) and could only go to regional level for household analysis. HM Treasury's benefits-in-kind method (1.6) is the only established way of allocating public-service spending to households, and it excludes public goods. Scottish Government distributional analysis — page not reachable, **unverified**.

*Schools.* School Cuts (National Education Union with ASCL, NAHT, Parentkind, NGA; built by Outlandish) lets you look up your school's real-terms funding change since 2010, using DfE allocations 2011-12 to 2024-25, a school-costs deflator (teacher pay 52–54%, other staff 28–30%, non-staff 18–20%) and covering 19,068 schools (94% of schools and pupils), with the caveat "The impact on individual schools may slightly vary based on staffing, resources required and other unique sources of additional costs." Sources: https://schoolcuts.org.uk/ and https://schoolcuts.org.uk/methodology/. It is retrospective and campaign-run, not a candidate-policy tool.

*Health.* The BBC NHS Tracker (postcode to local trust performance on A&E, cancer and planned care targets) was not reachable — **unverified** in this session. No tool was found anywhere that converts a candidate's or party's pledge into "your waiting time" or "your child's class size". The nearest official practice is the Dutch CPB's qualitative treatment of care and education effects (1.1) and HMT's benefits-in-kind allocation (1.6).

**JUDGE.** Nobody does per-household local or non-money effects for manifesto choices. That is both an opportunity and a warning: the CPB, with 125 staff, deliberately dropped education and housing effects in 2021 because the error risk was too high. A future non-money layer should start with things that are already measured locally (school funding per pupil, trust waiting times, council tax band bills) and describe direction and mechanism, not invent a number.

## 4. What the evidence says about personalised policy-impact information

**OBSERVED.**

*Do voters respond to personal gains?* In Sweden the Social Democrats promised to cut family transfers in 1994 and to raise them in 1998, and did both; comparing parents of young children (affected) with parents of slightly older children (not affected), "voters with young children responded markedly to economic promises rather [than] to implemented policies." Source: M. Elinder, H. Jordahl, P. Poutvaara, "Promises, policies and pocketbook voting", European Economic Review 75 (2015) 177–194, abstract at https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eecrev/v75y2015icp177-194.html. Using Swedish tax-registry income matched to the 2010 election survey (n = 2,736), Healy, Persson and Snowberg found that once measurement error is removed, "The portion of pocketbook evaluations containing real economic information proved at least as consequential as sociotropic evaluations", that "partisan bias was stronger in national than personal assessments", and that income changes in the government's first year (when tax cuts happened) predicted incumbent voting more than final-year changes. Source: A. Healy, M. Persson, E. Snowberg, "Digging into the Pocketbook", American Political Science Review 111(4), 2017, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/digging-into-the-pocketbook-evidence-on-economic-voting-from-income-registry-data-matched-to-a-voter-survey/7FF7E7FFFDCA634B79D2289C6880CF3D. The classic counter-finding is Kinder and Kiewiet, "Sociotropic Politics: The American Case", British Journal of Political Science 11(2), 1981 (voters weigh the national economy more than their own wallet) and Sears and Funk, "The Role of Self-Interest in Social and Political Attitudes", Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 24, 1991 (self-interest effects are usually small); Chong, Citrin and Conley, "When Self-Interest Matters", Political Psychology 22(3), 2001, argue self-interest does bite when personal stakes are large, clear and made salient. Titles, authors and years verified via Crossref/Semantic Scholar metadata; abstracts not retrievable in this session, so the one-line summaries are from the papers' established reception and should be treated as **unverified paraphrase**.

*Does personalised information change policy views?* Kuziemko, Norton, Saez and Stantcheva ran randomised online experiments giving Americans customised information including their own place in the income distribution: "The treatment has large effects on views about inequality but only slightly moves tax and transfer policy preferences", except that learning how few estates pay estate tax roughly doubled support for it; they attribute the small effect to low trust in government. Source: AER 105(4), 2015, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20130360. In Argentina, households told their true income rank who had "overestimated their relative position… tend to demand higher levels of redistribution when informed". Source: Cruces, Perez-Truglia, Tetaz, Journal of Public Economics 98 (2013), https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v98y2013icp100-112.html. Stantcheva's large US surveys find "support for income and estate taxes is most strongly correlated with social preferences, that is, the perceived benefits of redistribution and concerns around the fairness of inequality and taxation… Efficiency concerns play a more minor role." Source: S. Stantcheva, "Understanding Tax Policy: How Do People Reason?", Quarterly Journal of Economics 136(4), 2021, abstract via Crossref, https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab033. Related: Bartels, "Homer Gets a Tax Cut", Perspectives on Politics 3(1), 2005 (title and venue verified; findings on "unenlightened self-interest" **unverified** here); Fernández-Albertos and Kuo, "Income Perception, Information, and Progressive Taxation", Political Science Research and Methods (2015/2018), and Karadja, Mollerstrom, Seim, "Richer (and Holier) Than Thou?", Review of Economics and Statistics 99(2), 2017 (titles verified; both find telling people their true income rank shifts redistribution preferences in the self-interested direction — **unverified paraphrase**).

*Ethics.* Jason Brennan argues citizens who vote should "vote well—or not vote at all": vote on evidence for the common good rather than narrow interest, and abstain if they cannot. Source: J. Brennan, The Ethics of Voting, Princeton University Press, https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691154442/the-ethics-of-voting. The CPB repeatedly insists its costing "is not a voting guide" and that "elections revolve around much more than economic effects alone" (1.1). The IEA's objection that costings on "contestable assumptions" presented "as if they were an objective truth would stifle debate" (1.6) is the strongest general critique of any pound figure on a ballot page.

**JUDGE.** The evidence cuts both ways and the site should say so on /about/impact. Personal stakes do move votes when they are clear and large (Elinder et al.; Healy et al.), so a "£312 better off" line is not neutral. But personalised information mostly changes what people believe about the world, not what they want (Kuziemko et al.; Stantcheva), and Dutch experience is that voters largely do not read the tables (Bolhuis). The defensible position is: show the household figure alongside the whole-country figure and the direction for other household types, never alone; never rank; and quote the CPB line that it is not a voting guide.

## 6. Deliverable 2 — the 15 most useful lessons

Each lesson names its source. M = money layer now; N = future non-money layer.

1. **Say "not a voting guide" on the page itself, not in a footnote (M, N).** The CPB prints "Keuzes in Kaart is geen kieswijzer" on page 2 of every edition. https://www.cpb.nl/system/files/cpbmedia/CPB-Keuzes-in-Kaart-2027-2030.pdf
2. **Lead with direction and size class, not the pound figure (M).** The CPB's own evaluation: "het gaat om de richting en niet om precieze cijfers". Show "£200–£400 a year more" or "about £25 a month less" before any exact number. https://www.cpb.nl/system/files/cpbmedia/CPB_publicatie-Evaluatie-Keuzes-in-Kaart-2027-2030.pdf
3. **Never show one median alone; show the spread for "households like yours" (M).** CPB 2018 warned against "sturen op precieze 'mediane' uitkomsten"; Resolution Foundation: "family type matters for whether the poorest gain or lose". https://www.cpb.nl/publicatie/toelichting-aanpassingen-presentatie-koopkrachtramingen-cpb ; https://www.resolutionfoundation.org/comment/budget-2025-special/
4. **Publish the "cannot model" list per candidate and count it (M).** PolicyEngine's own pe_gap list (IHT, ISAs, VED, business measures, prescription charges, fire precepts…) is public; a candidate page should say "3 of this candidate's 9 money pledges cannot be modelled". https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-scorecard/issues/136
5. **Attach a one-line reliability note to every figure (M).** Australia's PBO puts "A statement about the factors affecting the reliability of the costing estimates" on every costing; Canada requires "a qualitative statement identifying the sources of uncertainty". https://www.pbo.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-01/What%20is%20a%20Parliamentary%20Budget%20Office%20Costing%20PDF.pdf ; https://distribution-a617274656661637473.pbo-dpb.ca/b8441f9fba4284af9b1e8ab7bf4c11fde44dff291fd6f42e9214915b86576f37
6. **Round to signal precision (M).** The Australian PBO uses "Rounding methodologies that reflect estimation precision". Nearest £10 a month, never £312. Same source as 5.
7. **State "static, no behaviour change" and what that means in one sentence (M).** PolicyEngine: "PolicyEngine simulations default to static behavior." Canada's PBO: it "will not incorporate impacts from the policy to the economy". https://policyengine.org/uk/model/behavioral
8. **Ask the KFF question and answer it (M).** "Does the calculator provide definitive results for what I will pay? No." https://www.kff.org/interactive/subsidy-calculator/
9. **List who the numbers will be wrong for (M).** GOV.UK: "They will not give accurate results if you're a prisoner, a student, not a British or Irish citizen…". The site's bands cannot capture students, care-home residents, mixed-immigration-status households; say so. https://www.gov.uk/benefits-calculators
10. **Explain why your own result will differ, in Nibud's plain style (M).** "Misschien krijgt u wel helemaal geen loonsverhoging" — your pay rise, rent change or job change are not in the model. https://www.nibud.nl/consumenten/over-koopkrachtplaatjes/
11. **Name the year and say what is hidden beyond it (M).** CPB evaluation: post-2030 tax rises "aren't visible, making them unaccountable"; Australia shows four years plus ten. Show "in 2027-28" and "by 2031-32" side by side and note pledges with costs after that. Sources as in 2 and 5.
12. **Treat implementation as a separate, unmodelled question (M, N).** The CPB's "bevriezen basispakket" example: a pledge can be costed and still be "onrealistisch". Add a "depends on delivery" flag rather than a number. https://www.cpb.nl/system/files/cpbmedia/CPB_publicatie-Evaluatie-Keuzes-in-Kaart-2027-2030.pdf
13. **For public services, borrow HMT's benefits-in-kind method and its exclusions (N).** Allocate spending by measured use (GP visits, pupils) and say public goods cannot be allocated "for specific households". https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/752045/impact_on_households_distributional_analysis_to_accompany_budget_2018_web.pdf
14. **Start the non-money layer from things already measured locally (N).** School Cuts (per-school funding, 94% coverage, stated caveat) and the IFS council tax work (regional, "highly uncertain" capitalisation) show what is feasible and what is not; the CPB dropped education and housing effects in 2021 as too error-prone. https://schoolcuts.org.uk/methodology/ ; https://ifs.org.uk/publications/revaluation-and-reform-bringing-council-tax-england-21st-century
15. **Put the whole-country and other-household view next to "yours", because personal stakes do move votes (M, N).** Elinder et al.: parents "responded markedly to economic promises"; Kuziemko et al.: information "only slightly moves tax and transfer policy preferences". Showing both is the honest hedge against the pocketbook effect. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eecrev/v75y2015icp177-194.html ; https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20130360

## 7. Deliverable 3 — the strongest criticisms the site must be ready to answer

1. **"You are presenting contestable assumptions as objective truth."** Source: Booth and Bourne, IEA, 26 June 2014, https://iea.org.uk/blog/why-the-obr-shouldn%E2%80%99t-audit-the-manifestos-of-political-parties. Answer with a visible assumptions list, the static-model statement, and the cannot-model count on every page.
2. **"The model is ideological; our ideas always come out badly."** Source: SP leader Marijnissen ("neoliberale modellen") and Omtzigt ("pseudo-reality"), NOS, 7 November 2023, https://nos.nl/l/2497028. Answer: the engine is open source; behavioural responses are off; the site models what candidates said, and the ledger records every choice.
3. **"Parties (and candidates) will game a public model."** Sources: NOS 2023 (parties pick measures that score well); Bolhuis 2018 (strategic positioning); IfG on Ireland (selective release). Answer: everything is quoted from a dated public source; nothing is submitted privately; the ledger is append-only.
4. **"Comparison is unfair when some candidates have no costable pledges."** Source: CPB Evaluatie 2027-2030, p. 4 ("imperfecte vergelijking"). Answer: identical page for every candidate, with "no modellable money pledge found" shown as a state, not a zero.
5. **"A precise pound figure is false precision — nobody is the median."** Sources: CPB 2018 presentation note; CPB Evaluatie 2027-2030 p. 5; Nibud. Answer: rounded bands, spread shown, direction first.
6. **"Static analysis ignores behaviour, growth and interactions."** Sources: IEA 2014; Australian PBO ("policies can interact with each other"); Ireland "in isolation". Answer: say static, say per-pledge, and say the sum may not equal the whole.
7. **"Telling voters they'd be £312 better off encourages selfish voting and corrodes civic reasoning."** Sources: Elinder et al. 2015 (promises move affected parents' votes); Chong et al. 2001 (self-interest bites when stakes are salient); Brennan, The Ethics of Voting (vote for the common good); CPB "elections revolve around much more than economic effects alone". Answer: never rank; always show the national and other-household effects beside "yours"; state the research on /about/impact and invite challenge.
8. **"Voters don't read these tables anyway."** Source: Bolhuis 2018 ("questionable whether many voters actually read, understand and base their votes on published results"). Answer: measure and publish usage; keep the direction-first summary short.
9. **"It's a black box."** Source: CPB Evaluatie 2027-2030, p. 5. Answer: link the exact PolicyEngine version, parameters and the grid file for each figure.
10. **"The underlying data are weak."** Sources: HM Treasury algorithmic transparency record (income tax results "less promising"; risks of "incorrect parameters or input data"), https://gov.uk/algorithmic-transparency-records/hmt-modelling-policy-engine; RF on LFS quality. Answer: cite the record openly; show validation status per benefit or tax.
11. **"Costs are hidden beyond your horizon."** Source: CPB Evaluatie 2027-2030, p. 7. Answer: show two years and flag later-dated costs.
12. **"Local and service effects, which matter most to people, are missing."** Sources: IFS 2020 (household analysis only to region "due to small sample sizes"); HMT (public goods excluded). Answer: say what is not covered; build the non-money layer from measured local data only.
13. **"You collect household data."** Source contrast: GOV.UK "free and anonymous"; PolicyEngine "does not store personally identifiable information" but keeps an anonymous ID in the URL. Answer: bands only, browser only, no server round trip; say it next to the input, not in a policy page.
14. **"A pledge is not a law."** Source: Which?, 21 September 2026 ("Remember not to make major financial decisions based on speculation alone"); CPB implementation caveat. Answer: label every figure "if enacted as described".
15. **"Who funds your engine?"** Source: PolicyEngine supporters page (Arnold Ventures, Nuffield, NSF, a UBI-promoting fund, others), https://policyengine.org/uk/supporters. Answer: link it from /about/data-use before anyone else does.
