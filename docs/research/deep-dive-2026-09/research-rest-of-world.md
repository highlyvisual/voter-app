# Voter-information tools outside the UK, Europe and North America

Deep-dive research for What's It To Me? (whatsittome.org). Date of all access: 25 September 2026. Research method: web search (search budget exhausted at 200 calls, shared across the session) plus roughly 75 direct page fetches. Where a page could not be fetched or a number could not be confirmed, the text says "unverified". Every paragraph is labelled OBSERVED (what the source says) or JUDGED (my opinion).

Reading note. Our site's model is: every candidate on the ballot, identical pages, every position quoted word for word from a named, dated, linked source, no ranking, scoring, matching or recommending. Two tools abroad come closest to that model and are described at most length: Policy.nz (New Zealand) and MyNeta (India). Taiwan's g0v voter guides and the official "election bulletin" tradition in Taiwan, Japan and South Korea are the other close relatives.

## Part A. Country by country

### 1. Australia

#### 1a. ABC Vote Compass (Vox Pop Labs)

OBSERVED. Vote Compass is built by Vox Pop Labs, a Canadian company of data and social scientists founded by Clifton van der Linden, and run in Australia with the ABC. The 2025 federal edition had 30 questions "developed to reflect policy areas where parties differ". It shows "your alignment on a social and economic scale" and "the extent of your agreement and disagreement with the parties and candidates", and lets users weight issues by importance. Its own disclaimer: "Vote Compass doesn't tell you how to vote or who to vote for, nor does it try to predict how people will vote." In 2022, 1.5 million people completed it. It collects optional demographic data (gender, age, education, region, past vote) so results can be weighted and published as a running "poll" during the campaign. Incumbent independents "have been invited to respond to the survey". Source: ABC News, "Vote Compass is back for the 2025 federal election. Here's how it works", https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-04-02/vote-compass-2025-federal-election-how-it-works/104967326 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. By 2 May 2025, 1.2 million people had used the 2025 edition. The ABC describes it as "not a conventional public opinion poll" and "an educational tool designed to promote electoral literacy and civic engagement", but then weights the responses "by gender, age, education, language, religion, place of residence and past vote" and reports population-level findings (for example "62.7% support increased government intervention in housing"). Source: ABC News, "The key issues identified by Vote Compass as 1.2m use election tool", https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-05-02/vote-compass-what-the-data-shows-ahead-of-federal-election-2025/105234112 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). An earlier article in the same series reports 254,376 responses by 20 April 2025: https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-04-20/federal-election-2025-most-important-issue-vote-compass/105178788 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. How party positions are set. The Vox Pop Labs method (same across countries) is expert coding calibrated to "party manifestos, policy proposals, official websites, speeches, media releases, statements made in the legislature", with questions drafted with local political scientists. Source: Wikipedia, "Vote Compass", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_Compass (accessed 25 Sep 2026). A CBC explainer for the Canadian edition states: "The party positions were found through a comprehensive review of the public statements made by party officials", and "All the parties were consulted and invited to review the findings and provide feedback", with the disclaimer "Results are not intended and should not be interpreted as voting advice". Source: CBC News, "Vote Compass: Find out how your views compare with the major parties", https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/newfoundland-labrador/vote-compass-explainer-1.5891260 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The ABC edition's own methodology page (votecompass.au/faq and /methodology) is a JavaScript app and returned no text to my fetches; the ABC-specific calibration process is therefore unverified beyond the generic description above.

OBSERVED. Criticisms. Wikipedia records that Vote Compass surveys "have the potential to become push polling, if the survey questions posed are poorly designed", and that van der Linden "explicitly rejects" the label "voting advice application". The 2011 rabble.ca critique of the Canadian edition found compound questions ("Questions with more than one question in them, but that you have to answer with only one answer"), mismatched party placements, and many NDP supporters being matched to the Greens. Source: rabble.ca, "CBC's Vote Compass is miscalibrated. Have you noticed?", https://rabble.ca/politics/cbcs-vote-compass-miscalibrated-have-you-noticed-babblers-have/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). In New Zealand a conservative outlet complained that the 2023 TVNZ edition omitted abortion and euthanasia while including cannabis, worded a transgender-sport question confusingly, and offered only one minor party (TOP) as a result option. Source: Centrist, "Electoral Commission Backs TVNZ's Biased Vote Compass Tool", https://centrist.nz/electoral-commission-tvnz-vote-compass/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). I could not find a documented 2013 Australian controversy; unverified.

JUDGED. Vote Compass is a research instrument dressed as a voter tool. Its real product is the weighted opinion dataset the broadcaster publishes. That is legitimate but it means the question set is designed for measurement, not for the voter's ballot. Two lessons for us: (1) the "disclaimer plus a percentage match" pattern does not stop people reading a match as advice; (2) the party-review step ("parties were invited to review the findings") is a good idea we can copy without the matching: let each candidate see and dispute the quotes attributed to them before publication.

#### 1b. They Vote For You (OpenAustralia Foundation)

OBSERVED. They Vote For You (theyvoteforyou.org.au) turns every recorded division in the House and Senate into plain-English "policies" and gives each MP a stance on each. Launched 2014 with Google seed funding; four founders (Henare Degan, Matthew Landauer, Luke Bacon, Katherine Szuminska). Source: Designing Open Democracy, "They Vote For You", https://www.designingopendemocracy.com/organisations/they-vote-for-you/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). It is now "Funded entirely by donations from people who use the services", and policies are community-editable "much like Wikipedia". Source: OpenAustralia Foundation, "They Vote For You", https://www.oaf.org.au/projects/they-vote-for-you/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Staffing is tiny: "We don't have teams of researchers to add in the missing data. Just one. Part time." Source: OpenAustralia Foundation on X, https://x.com/openaustralia/status/1507097520169971715 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. Method. "A person's stance on a Policy is determined by how they voted in parliament in Divisions that relate to that Policy." Editors choose which divisions count and "work out how someone who supported it would have voted"; divisions can be marked "most important" and are then weighted "much more strongly". Policies are chosen where "politicians have rebelled" or where parties allowed a free vote, and "Every policy is a work in progress." On absences: "We have no way of telling the difference between someone that was not in parliament on the day, was busy with other duties in parliament and someone who actively abstained." No warranty: "We give no warranty for the data so let us know if you find any factual inaccuracies." Source: They Vote For You, "FAQ", https://theyvoteforyou.org.au/help/faq (accessed 25 Sep 2026). A policy page (example: "Same-sex marriage equality") lists MPs in six bands: voted consistently for/against, generally for/against, a mixture, insufficient data. Source: https://theyvoteforyou.org.au/policies/1 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The exact percentage thresholds behind the bands are not in the FAQ (unverified).

OBSERVED. Criticisms. A detailed critique by Pat Leslie gives the Jane Hume example: the site said she "Consistently voted against creating a federal Anti-Corruption Commission" when the bill passed unanimously and her "against" votes were on crossbench amendments. It also found that Parliament recorded 185 House divisions in 2023 while the site showed 154, and argued that presenting whipped party votes as personal choices "misleads". Source: Pat Leslie, "How They Vote For You can mislead the voting public", https://patleslie.net/blogs/blog_tvfy/tvfy_post (accessed 25 Sep 2026). In 2021–22 "Liberal MPs Andrew Bragg and Dave Sharma complained to regulators and then threatened legal action over They Vote For You's representation of their voting records"; the director "acknowledged it was constrained to recording formal votes only — not the public positions MPs take outside the chamber". Source: Designing Open Democracy, "OpenAustralia Foundation", https://www.designingopendemocracy.com/organisations/open-australia-foundation/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). An open GitHub issue proposes reframing the site's copy as "a library ... of Australian federal voting records" and reserving the word "our" for "the people of Australia, to whom the democracy belongs". Source: https://github.com/openaustralia/theyvoteforyou/issues/1768 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

JUDGED. This is the clearest cautionary tale in the whole survey for a site like ours. The moment an editor writes a one-line policy title and decides which divisions "count", a plain vote record becomes an interpretation, and MPs will (and did) treat it as an accusation. Their own defence, "we only record formal votes", is an admission that the title promised more than the data. For us: if we ever show Parliament voting data on a candidate page, show the division name, date, the question actually put, and the vote, and let the reader do the summarising. Do not write "voted against X".

#### 1c. AEC (official)

OBSERVED. The Australian Electoral Commission does not publish candidate statements or policies. Its voter-facing tools are procedural: a "practise voting" simulator that uses fictional candidates ("SUN PARTY", "fog PARTY") and asks the user to "number every box", with a "Check my ballot paper" button; it states it "does not seek to fully detail the complexity of informal and formal votes". Source: AEC, "Practise voting – House of Representatives", https://www.aec.gov.au/Voting/How_to_vote/practice/practice-house-of-reps.htm (accessed 25 Sep 2026). By contrast the ACT's territorial commission does publish voluntary candidate statements: up to 500 words plus photo and links, rejected only if "obscene, defamatory or otherwise unlawful", not edited for spelling, all published "in the same single font and at the same font size", ordered "by lot", and every candidate listed "regardless of whether that candidate has submitted a candidate information statement". Source: Elections ACT, "Fact sheet – Candidate information statements", https://www.elections.act.gov.au/elections/education/fact-sheets/fact-sheet-candidate-statements (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

JUDGED. The Elections ACT rules are a near-perfect statement of the equal-treatment design we already use (same font, same size, order by lot, everyone listed). Worth citing on our /about page as an official precedent. The AEC's practise ballot with a "check my ballot" button is a small feature we could adapt for our /how-to-vote page once the May 2027 local elections (first-past-the-post, multiple seats in some wards) approach.

#### 1d. Build a Ballot (Project Planet)

OBSERVED. Run by Project Planet, a "climate-focused charity" co-founded by Lizzie Hedding and Tegan Lerm; launched 15 April 2025. Source: Pedestrian.TV, "Build A Ballot Aims To Help You Create A Personal Voting Plan", https://www.pedestrian.tv/news/election-tool-build-a-ballot-young-voter/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Its candidate-information page says 600,000 Australians used it at the 2025 federal election. Incumbents are rated from "official policy documents, parliamentary voting records, media appearances and interviews, public statements and social media posts"; non-incumbents fill in a questionnaire and "must provide evidence to support their stated agreement rating". Non-respondents: minor-party and independent candidates with no response get no rating "until verified positions are provided"; a non-answered policy is "N/A" and "excluded from match calculations". Matches are colour-coded: 70%+ green, 40–69% yellow, below 40% red, "Insufficient information — Grey". Source: Build a Ballot, "Methodology", https://www.buildaballot.org.au/transparency/methodology and "Candidate information", https://www.buildaballot.org.au/candidate-information (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Funding: "hundreds of Australians"; "we don't take money from any political parties, candidates or associated campaign groups". The final "voting plan" is drag-and-drop and "doesn't have to reflect your 'match levels'". Source: Build a Ballot, "FAQs", https://www.buildaballot.org.au/faqs (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Candidate response rates are not published (unverified).

JUDGED. Two things worth borrowing: the published timetable (policy cut-off, questionnaire sent, assessments sent to incumbents, deadline, go-live) and the rule that a candidate's self-reported position must come with evidence. Two things to avoid: a climate charity running a "non-partisan" matcher invites the obvious attack, and the traffic-light colours turn a percentage into a verdict.

#### 1e. smartvote Australia (ANU)

OBSERVED. Run by the ANU School of Politics and International Relations, led by Professor Patrick Dumont, funded by ANU and donations; ran for the 2019 federal election (media partner Nine) and the 2020 ACT election. It is "a candidate-oriented tool, as opposed to other Voting Advice Applications that code the positions of the main parties". Source: ANU, "smartvote", https://politicsir.cass.anu.edu.au/research/projects/electoral-surveys/smartvote (accessed 25 Sep 2026). For the 2020 ACT election, "101 of 137 candidates (74%)" had answered the 32-question survey by the reported deadline and more than 16,000 voters had used it. Source: Canberra Times, "Smartvote suits the ACT's electoral system especially well", https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/6957405/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The 2019 federal response rate is not published on the ANU launch page (unverified). No edition after 2020 was found (unverified).

JUDGED. 74% candidate participation in a small, well-resourced territory with a university behind it is the realistic ceiling for candidate self-report. Note that the tool then went quiet: candidate-questionnaire tools are expensive to re-run each election.

#### 1f. Australia Institute and preference-flow explainers

OBSERVED. The Australia Institute publishes text explainers on preferential voting ("Full preferential voting means you can't waste your vote") with historical examples, but no interactive tool was found. Source: https://australiainstitute.org.au/post/full-preferential-voting-means-you-cant-waste-your-vote/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). A round-up of 2025 tools lists only Build a Ballot, Vote Compass and They Vote For You. Source: RUSSH, "Not sure how to vote in the election? These online tools can help", https://www.russh.com/which-party-to-vote-for-australian-2025-election-tools/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Any Australia Institute interactive tool is unverified.

### 2. New Zealand

#### 2a. Policy.nz (Policy Ltd, with The Spinoff) — closest relative to our model

OBSERVED. Who and funding. "Policy.nz is a product of Policy Ltd, an organisation that makes tools to improve access to election information." Founders: Asher Emanuel, Chris McIntyre, Oliver (Ollie) Neas, Racheal Reeves. Current team (2025 local edition): editor Shanti Mathias, product engineer Luka Didham, assistant editor Rayne Aldridge. The 2025 local-election edition was funded by Local Government New Zealand and 19 named councils, with the line "Our sponsors have no influence on editorial decisions." Source: Policy.nz, "About Policy.nz", https://policy.nz/about (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Since 2019 it "has received funding from Google News Initiative"; partners have included The Spinoff, LGNZ, and overseas media (Le Vif and Knack in Belgium, Newslaundry in India, Taiwan News). Source: Wikipedia, "Policy.nz", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy.nz (accessed 25 Sep 2026). For 2023 it crowdfunded NZ$10,444 from 200 pledgers against a NZ$10,000 target to "cover the last costs"; the campaign page describes a 13-person Wellington team (editorial director, two senior editors, six editors, support staff) who "research and summarise every single policy released by the eight largest parties". Source: PledgeMe, "Bring back Policy.nz – the complete guide to election 2023", https://www.pledgeme.co.nz/projects/7627-bring-back-policy-nz-the-complete-guide-to-election-2023 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The 2026 edition, announced 25 September 2026, is supported by Foggy Valley Aotearoa Trust, Mike Frumkin and Christina Falco, and law firm MinterEllisonRuddWatts. Source: Scoop, "Policy.nz Returns For The 2026 Election", https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PO2609/S00223/policynz-returns-for-the-2026-election.htm (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. Usage. "Around one in six New Zealand voters – 500,000 users" in 2020, covering 2,000 policies and 500 candidates; "around one in 20 voters" used PolicyLocal.nz in 2019, which profiled 3,500 candidates across 574 elections. Source: Wikipedia, "Policy.nz" (as above). The 2026 announcement says "over 500,000 voters — approximately one in six New Zealand electors" used it before both the 2020 and 2023 general elections. Source: Scoop (as above). The Spinoff says "hundreds of thousands of voters have used the tool" across "two general and two local body elections" since 2017. Source: The Spinoff, "Huge news: The best voting tool in NZ is back", https://thespinoff.co.nz/politics/06-09-2023/huge-news-the-best-voting-tool-in-nz-is-back (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. How party policies are sourced (general elections). Wikipedia's description, drawn from the site's own material: policies are captured "from official websites, documents, press releases, social media, and direct candidate surveys"; "An editorial process is applied to create simplified policy statements and descriptions that use consistent language"; "Citations link to original source material"; and it "contrasts to other voting advice applications by not using a quiz-based format or algorithms to recommend who a user should vote for". Source: Wikipedia, "Policy.nz" (as above). The 2026 announcement repeats: the platform "collects announcements made by political parties and presents comparable proposals using consistent, plain language with direct links to original sources" and "does not tell voters who they should vote for". Source: Scoop (as above). On the live site, each policy is a one-line headline (example: "Ban synthetic nitrogen fertilisers over time") with a "Read more" expansion, grouped by topic and sub-area, with a toggle to hide or show party names, and a "favourite" heart. The list view shows no date or source link; source links sit inside "Read more" (per Wikipedia; I could not render the expanded panel, so the exact position of the link is unverified). Source: Policy.nz, https://policy.nz/2023/party-vote/policies/economy/policy/POL53765 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. How candidate policies are sourced (local elections and electorate candidates). "We sent questionnaires to every candidate whose email address we could find. We asked candidates for up to three policies on key topics." Editing is minimal: "Candidates' responses have been edited for spelling, typos and to comply with our style guide, but have otherwise been left in their own words. We do not publish responses that are off-topic, defamatory or contain hate speech." All confirmed candidates are listed, but biography and policies appear only "if the candidate completes the Policy.nz questionnaire"; a candidate with no policies "received a questionnaire but did not complete it" or could not be reached. Geographic data comes from Statistics NZ, the Local Government Commission and LINZ. Source: Policy.nz, "About" (as above). The 2019 launch article says candidates who gave an email on their nomination form were emailed the questionnaire, and adds "If you can't find it, or mistook it for a personality quiz, fear not". Source: The Spinoff, "Announcing Policy Local: 573 New Zealand elections in one place", https://thespinoff.co.nz/local-elections/29-08-2019/announcing-policy-local-573-new-zealand-elections-in-one-place (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Candidate response rates are not published (unverified).

OBSERVED. The one soft "matching" feature and its caveat. Users can "Like the policies you support as you go through each topic" and "Find out which parties match your views based on your choices" (Source: https://policy.nz/2023, accessed 25 Sep 2026). The About page has a section headed "Are you telling me how to vote?" which answers: "Your vote is yours, use it how you like. Policy.nz exists to make it easier to understand what the candidates want to do with your vote, but there's a lot more to voting than this." It then warns that "Some candidates have more policies than others on any given issue. Each candidate was allowed to state up to three policies per topic, but not all candidates did," and that the favourites graph does not account for how much a user prioritises different issues. Source: Policy.nz, "About" (as above).

OBSERVED. Design. 2026 redesign by the agency Daylight; new features are "entering your address to explore party and electorate vote options, browsing policies with party names hidden or visible, and favoriting policies to identify common themes". Source: Scoop (as above). The New Zealand Herald called it "the stand-out success of the 2017 election". Source: Wikipedia (as above).

JUDGED. Policy.nz is the strongest proof that a no-quiz, document-sourced, plain-language comparison can reach one in six voters. Three design decisions matter most: (1) a ruthless editorial standard, "one policy = one plain sentence, same language for every party", which makes comparison possible; (2) the "hide party names" toggle, which lets a reader test their own bias without any scoring; (3) an explicit "Are you telling me how to vote?" answer. Its weak points are also instructive: it paraphrases rather than quotes, so a party can dispute the wording; the source link is a click away rather than on the face of the claim; it depends on a paid 13-person team and re-fundraising every cycle; and the "favourites" heart is a small matching feature that its own About page has to apologise for. We quote verbatim with the source on the face of every claim, which is the harder but more defensible choice. What we should copy is the plain-language one-line headline above each quote so that a reader can scan.

#### 2b. Vote Compass NZ (TVNZ / Vox Pop Labs / Victoria University of Wellington)

OBSERVED. Ran in 2014, 2017, 2020 and 2023 with 1News as media partner; academic team led by Associate Professor Jennifer Lees-Marshment (University of Auckland) with Dr Danny Osborne and Victoria University colleagues. "Vote Compass has proved highly popular, attracting over a million users combined in the 2014, 2017 and 2020 elections." Lees-Marshment describes it as "a gigantic poll" of over 30 policy positions. Source: University of Auckland, "Online tool aligns voters' views with party policies", https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2023/09/05/online-tool-aligns-voters-views-with-party-policies.html (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The 2026 landing page names 1News, Vox Pop Labs and Victoria University of Wellington. Source: https://votecompass.tvnz.co.nz/nz2023 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Bias complaint: see 1a.

#### 2c. On the Fence (Massey University Design & Democracy Project)

OBSERVED. Built by Massey students Karl Kane, Kieran Stowers and Josh Barr for the 2014 election; a game-like questionnaire with slider questions that ranks parties 1st, 2nd, 3rd for the user. Its stated aim: "It doesn't tell you how to vote or who to vote for, but it puts 'trainer wheels' on the future for many young people". Source: McGuinness Institute, "On the Fence – Voter Engagement", https://www.mcguinnessinstitute.org/civicsnz/on-the-fence-voter-engagement/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Party positions were self-reported: parties with sitting MPs or polling over 0.5% "have signed off on the questions within the tool and provided their own responses". Before the 2017 election it had "over 237,000 unique users within four weeks"; 78% of first-time voters said it "improved their understanding of what Government does". Relaunched 2020 with a te reo Māori option, supported by the Electoral Commission and the McGuinness Institute. Source: Massey University, "Helping young people to be informed, confident voters", https://creative.massey.ac.nz/stories/research/helping-young-people-to-be-informed-confident-voters (accessed 25 Sep 2026). No 2023 edition found (unverified).

JUDGED. On the Fence shows that a design-school aesthetic (sliders, plain words, "balancing act" framing) reaches young voters, and that parties will sign off questions when asked. It also shows the shelf-life problem: student-built tools stop when the students leave.

### 3. India

#### 3a. MyNeta (Association for Democratic Reforms and National Election Watch) — closest relative on the "data with no advice" side

OBSERVED. Who and legal basis. ADR describes itself as "a citizen-led, non-political and non-governmental organization". MyNeta is "an open access repository platform" of candidate affidavit data. The data exists because Supreme Court judgments in 2002 and 2003 require every candidate to "disclose criminal, financial and educational background prior to the polls" in a sworn affidavit filed with the Election Commission of India (ECI). Two datasets: Election Watch (criminal, financial and educational information from affidavits since 2002) and Political Party Watch (party income, expenditure and donations above Rs 20,000). Source: ADR, "FAQs", https://adrindia.org/content/faqs (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. What it shows and how. Coverage: Lok Sabha 2004–2024, every state assembly, Rajya Sabha, and some municipal and panchayat elections; recent additions include West Bengal, Kerala, Assam, Puducherry and Tamil Nadu 2026. Per candidate: criminal cases (with sections of the penal code), movable and immovable assets, liabilities, education, income-tax returns for self and spouse, PAN status, profession, and government contracts. Extra views: sortable lists of candidates with declared criminal cases; "re-contesting candidate comparisons" showing asset growth between elections; winner expense analysis; electoral-bond data. The site disclaimer reads: "All information on this website has been taken by ADR from the website of the Election Commission of India" and "ADR does not add or subtract any information, unless the EC changes the data"; "in case of discrepancy ... the information available on the ECI website should be treated as correct." Source: MyNeta, home page, https://www.myneta.info/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). A candidate page (example: Imthiyas, Independent, Coimbatore South, Tamil Nadu 2026) is organised as: criminal cases (here "No criminal cases", with a "Data Readability Report of Criminal Cases"), assets summary, detailed movable and immovable assets, liabilities (a Bajaj Finance loan of Rs 55,844), education ("8th Pass"), PAN and income-tax returns, profession (Tourist Guide), government contracts. The page labels everything "self-declared affidavit" and says: "This information is an archive of the candidate's self-declared affidavit that was filed during the elections." No score or judgement appears. Source: https://www.myneta.info/TamilNadu2026/candidate.php?candidate_id=2062 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The criminal-case list for Lok Sabha 2024 runs to 83 pages, sortable by party and case count; the top entry is K Surendran (BJP, Wayanad) with 243 declared cases. The list carries the line: "Such information is only aimed at highlighting the growing criminality in politics ... to enable voters to form an informed choice." Source: https://www.myneta.info/LokSabha2024/index.php?action=summary&subAction=crime&sort=criminal (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. Failures acknowledged. ADR says the "Sheer volume of data ... often poorly scanned" means a lag of "5–7 days before polling date", and "the original affidavit should be considered accurate" where they differ. Source: ADR FAQs (as above). Aggregate headline figures for 2024 winners (the widely reported "46% of winners with criminal cases") could not be fetched from ADR's report page (server error); unverified here.

OBSERVED. The official equivalent. In March 2024 the ECI launched a "Know Your Candidate" (KYC) app; Chief Election Commissioner Rajiv Kumar said voters "can also now check for themselves the assets and liabilities that candidates with criminal antecedents hold", and that "Candidates with a criminal record are required to publish or make public this information in newspapers on television thrice", while parties "have to explain why they chose them". Source: The Week, "'Know Your Candidate': ECI's new app", https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2024/03/17/know-your-candidate-ecis-new-app-to-check-whether-your-candidate-has-a-criminal-history.html (accessed 25 Sep 2026). In April 2026 the ECI folded KYC into its ECINet app (per search results from Organiser and newsonair.gov.in; pages not fetched, so details unverified).

JUDGED. MyNeta is the purest "publish the record, add nothing" site in the world, and it has survived 20+ years and lawsuits because of two design rules we already share: every fact is traceable to a filed document, and the site says in writing that the original beats the copy. Three things to copy. First, the phrase "self-declared" on every field: it tells the reader who is responsible for the claim. Second, the "Data Readability Report", an honest flag that the source scan was hard to read. Third, the between-elections comparison for repeat candidates (what did they say last time?). One thing to think hard about: MyNeta's sortable "most criminal cases" league table is, functionally, a ranking, and the line "highlighting the growing criminality in politics" is an editorial stance. We should not build sortable league tables of candidates on any measure.

#### 3b. Neta App

OBSERVED. A Jalandhar start-up founded by Pratham Mittal (2018) letting users "rate" their MP and MLA with one changeable vote each; claimed 2.5 million votes during the May 2018 Karnataka election from 100,000 app users; bootstrapped; verified users via "voter rolls, Aadhaar and apps such as Truecaller". The main concern reported was manipulation ("political parties can use their men to jack up votes"). Source: Forbes India, "Neta app: Finger on democracy's pulse", https://www.forbesindia.com/article/startups/neta-app-finger-on-democracys-pulse/50635/1 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). HuffPost India reported Election Commission officials questioning whether the app was publishing banned opinion polls during elections (headline only; page blocked by robots.txt, so details unverified). Source: https://www.huffpost.com/archive/in/entry/neta-app-elections-privacy-democracy-voter-data_in_5cd8ebdae4b0705e47de8a0b. Current status: no evidence of activity after 2019 found (unverified).

JUDGED. A crowd-rating of politicians is a popularity contest with a privacy problem. Nothing to borrow; a useful example of what regulators treat as an opinion poll.

### 4. Japan

OBSERVED. The official baseline. Japan's Public Offices Election Act (article 167) requires an official election gazette, the 選挙公報 (senkyo kōhō), "containing candidates' names, backgrounds, and policy positions" written by the candidates themselves, for Diet and gubernatorial elections; since December 2012 prefectural election commissions post it online until polling day, and the National Diet Library archives it. Source: National Diet Library, "日本-選挙公報", https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/rnavi/politics/post_1136 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. Vote-match tools (ボートマッチ). Mainichi's えらぼーと (since 2007): 25 policy questions, "compared to candidate answers"; "The Mainichi Shimbun uses results from policy questionnaires administered to candidates beforehand as foundational data"; about 4.7 million cumulative users over 14 runs. Source: PR Times, "毎日新聞ボートマッチ・えらぼーと 2025年参院選", https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000000623.000032749.html (accessed 25 Sep 2026). 選挙ドットコム (senkyo.com, run by Ichini Inc., CEO Takao Takahata) 投票マッチング: 20 questions, five-point answers; positions come from a questionnaire "to eligible national parties and political groups that submitted candidate lists for proportional representation"; "surpassed 1 million users within 7 days" in July 2025 and 7 million cumulative uses. Source: PR Times, "投票マッチングの利用者が100万人を突破", https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000000210.000019431.html (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Senkyo.com lists candidates from three sources: the candidate's own application, "primary information from party-announced endorsed candidates", and verified press reports; corrections are by form and "will not charge any fees". Source: Ichini Inc., https://ichi-ni.jp/info/20230329/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). NHK runs its own candidate questionnaire and vote match ("By answering the same questionnaire, you can see numerical alignment with candidates' and parties' views"), gated behind NHK's terms-of-use screen. Source: https://news.web.nhk/senkyo/database/sangiin/2025/votematch/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Nikkei's 2025 beta uses a "new method" with VETA Inc. (Waseda researchers) giving a 0–100 score. Source: https://vdata.nikkei.com/election/2025/vote-match/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Response rates for the Mainichi and NHK candidate surveys were not obtainable (Mainichi site blocked; NHK gated); unverified.

OBSERVED. Criticisms. A media round-up notes "The format and questions differ greatly between sites", "The impression changes significantly when the phrasing differs", users "report different results depending on the site", and advises "Using multiple vote-matches". Source: allatanys.jp, "迫る参議院選 ボートマッチのすゝめ", http://allatanys.jp/blogs/27244/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). A statistician's critique: five-point Likert answers are ordinal, "段階評価は距離を意味しない" (a scale rating does not mean distance), so treating them as distances is a measurement error; parties that answer "neutral" to everything come out with artificially high agreement ("中間の回答ばかりする政党の一致度が過剰に高くなりやすい"); how individual candidate answers are aggregated into a "party position" is unspecified; only Nikkei's conjoint method escapes these problems. Source: ill-identified diary, "選挙公約マッチングサイトの問題と統計学", https://ill-identified.hatenablog.com/entry/2026/02/02/000944 (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

JUDGED. Japan is the most mature vote-match market in the world (five national outlets, seven million cumulative uses on one site alone) and also the best evidence that matching is fragile: the same voter gets different "closest parties" from different newspapers because of wording and arithmetic. The Japanese lesson for us is the official gazette: a state-guaranteed, candidate-written, equal-space statement for every candidate, posted online. The UK has no equivalent, which is exactly the gap Democracy Club statements and our verbatim quotes fill.

### 5. South Korea

OBSERVED. Candidates registering with the National Election Commission must submit "documents on property, military service records, criminal records, certificates of final academic backgrounds, tax payment history over the last 5 years, and default history". Source: NEC (English), "Candidate Registration", https://www.nec.go.kr/site/eng/03/10301070000002020070601.jsp (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Election commissions "send [candidate campaign booklets] to absentees and each household", affix candidate posters, and the National Election Broadcasting Debate Commission broadcasts debates. No Korean matching app ("Ppoppi" or similar) could be found; unverified, treat as not established.

JUDGED. Korea's model is the strongest "disclosure by law" regime: five years of tax payments, arrears, military service and criminal record, mailed to every household. It is a reminder that in the UK almost none of this is available for candidates, so our candidate pages must be honest about what is *not* knowable (we already have the /about/accuracy page; a per-candidate "what we could not find" box would extend it).

### 6. Taiwan

OBSERVED. Official bulletin. Every household receives a government-printed election bulletin that contains "the candidates' resumes and campaign manifesto"; the CEC records audio versions for blind voters. Source: ANFREL Asian Electoral Resource Center, "Taiwan – Election Management", https://aerc.anfrel.org/country/taiwan/election-management/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). In 2018 the platform column was for the first time allowed to include images; The Reporter compiled all 22 mayoral candidates' platforms from the CEC bulletin and "released raw data as an open spreadsheet". Some candidates gamed it (a mock banknote; a ten-character slogan). Source: The Reporter, "選舉公報首度解禁可放圖片", https://www.twreporter.org/a/2018-local-election-electoral-bulletin-policy (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. g0v voter guides. The 立委投票指南 (Legislator Voter Guide, vote.ly.g0v.tw; 388 commits) and 縣市長／議員投票指南 (councils.g0v.tw; 1,513 commits, 105 stars) share three written principles: "不做家長式的媒體，不做議題上的價值高下判斷、排名" ("No paternalistic media; no value judgements or rankings on issues"); "預設皆開源" ("open source by default"); and "如要引入非政府公開資訊，必需是全民可參與編輯協作的或是候選人、民代、政黨自行編輯的" ("non-governmental data must be either citizen-collaboratively edited or self-edited by candidates, representatives or parties"). Data comes from the CEC (candidates), the Legislative Yuan and county councils (votes, minutes, attendance), and includes bills, committees, donations and standpoints modules. Sources: https://github.com/g0v/twly-voter-guide and https://github.com/g0v/councilor-voter-guide (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

JUDGED. The g0v "no paternalism, no ranking" rule is the closest written charter to ours anywhere, and it comes with a data-provenance rule we should adopt in our ledger: anything not from an official record must be either openly co-editable or written by the candidate. Their weakness is that volunteer projects go stale between elections (the councillor guide has 39 open issues).

### 8. Philippines

OBSERVED. #### 10b. Kenya: Mzalendo, Uchaguzi, IEBC

OBSERVED. Mzalendo ("patriot") began in 2005, relaunched in 2012 on mySociety's Pombola with Omidyar Network funding, became an independent trust in 2014, and since 2019 has NIMD as core funder. Its annual Parliamentary Scorecard (from 2013) rates MPs on "lawmaking, oversight, and representation" from Hansard, using "volunteers and students to comb the Hansard". Effects: MPs "increased requests for speaking time with the Speaker to improve scorecard rankings"; President Ruto in 2022: "I don't want to hear that you're not speaking in parliament!"; Lang'ata constituents petitioned to recall their MP over a low score. Data problems: "voting by acclamation is not recorded" and committee records "remain inaccessible". The NIMD case study warns that "bold naming and shaming of the best and worst performers in parliament, have been tough to replicate in other contexts where this is mostly seen as sensationalism", and that "PMOs have to be careful not to burn bridges." It also notes that "most citizens do not understand the role of their MPs" and approach them for "handouts or personal help". Source: NIMD, "Legislative Accountability in Kenya" (Case 3), https://nimd.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Case-3_Kenya.pdf (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Mzalendo's data page says "Profile facts are drawn from retained primary-authority records and published only after independent review", figures are "separated by House, so figures are never blended or misleading", and "Machine-derived results are withheld; this is not a measure of public opinion." Source: https://mzalendo.com/about-data-analysis/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Home page: 418 members, 954 Hansard sittings, 558 bills tracked, a Promise Tracker, and "Bonga na Mzalendo" WhatsApp channel. Source: https://mzalendo.com/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The site "frequently inspires front-page stories" on MP attendance. Source: mySociety, https://www.mysociety.org/democracy/pombola/case-study-mzalendo/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Uchaguzi (Ushahidi) is an election-day incident-reporting platform, not a candidate tool; its 2013 evaluation found the platform "ran for the whole deployment without going down" but "the inability to track information sent to response partners ... greatly weakened" it. Source: Ushahidi, https://www.ushahidi.com/about/blog/uchaguzi-monitoring-and-evaluation-final-report-released (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The IEBC home page showed no candidate-information resource on fetch; unverified.

#### 10c. Nigeria: Shine Your Eye (EiE Nigeria)

OBSERVED. Shine Your Eye, run by Enough is Enough Nigeria (project owner Yemi Adamolekun), was "built by mySociety" and profiles elected officials' "educational background, experience, social media accounts, phone numbers, and email addresses"; it partners with Tracka (BudgIT) on constituency projects; listed as "Active" with last update 8 March 2022. Source: Civic Tech Innovation Network, https://civictech.africa/initiative/shine-your-eye/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Stears election coverage: not fetched; unverified.

JUDGED. Africa's tools are mostly *parliament monitors* rather than *pre-election candidate guides*, because the official candidate data is thin. Mzalendo's scorecard is the best-documented example of scoring changing politicians' behaviour, and also of scoring being read as sensational. Its two quiet rules, "published only after independent review" and "figures never blended", belong in our accuracy page.

### 11. Latin America

#### 11a. Brazil: TSE DivulgaCandContas, Ranking dos Políticos, Politize!

OBSERVED. DivulgaCandContas (the Superior Electoral Court's disclosure system) shows for every candidate: full name, ballot name and number, party or coalition, office sought, registration status (approved, rejected, under review), the asset declaration, and, for executive offices (president, governor, mayor), the "proposta de governo", "documento com planos e compromissos de gestão"; plus campaign finance (donors, suppliers, spending limits). Sources: TSE, "Sistema DivulgaCandContas do TSE é atualizado para as Eleições 2024", https://www.tse.jus.br/comunicacao/noticias/2024/Julho/sistema-divulgacandcontas-do-tse-e-atualizado-para-as-eleicoes-2024 ; Band, "Como consultar candidatos e contas de campanha no TSE", https://www.band.com.br/politica/eleicoes/2026/como-consultar-candidatos-e-contas-de-campanha-no-tse (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Bulk data is on the TSE open-data portal (https://dadosabertos.tse.jus.br/dataset/candidatos-2024).

OBSERVED. Ranking dos Políticos scores federal legislators out of 10: votes 7.5, expenses 1, attendance 1, privileges 0.5, plus bonuses for "legislative production" and penalties of "0.5 point per conviction". A Legislative Council decides which votes count and "For a proposal to be classified as favorable, it must reach a 70% consensus." It states: "We are not neutral. We believe that a better country depends on strengthening the rule of law", with priorities of "public-sector efficiency, improvement of the business environment, and the fight against corruption". Source: Ranking dos Políticos, "Criteria and Methodology", https://ranking.org.br/en/criteria-and-methodology (accessed 25 Sep 2026). The Intercept found it is owned by the Multilaser founders Alexandre Ostrowiecki and Renato Feder, that its council is drawn from pro-market economists, that votes against impeachment or the spending cap lost points while a deputy "condemned for administrative misconduct" ranked 5th nationally and a deputy with no cases ranked 505th, and that TV networks promoted it "as neutral technology". Source: The Intercept Brasil, "Atenção, eleitor: não caia no engodo chamado 'Ranking dos Políticos'", https://www.intercept.com.br/2018/08/05/atencao-eleitor-nao-caia-no-engodo-chamado-ranking-dos-politicos/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

OBSERVED. Politize! is "uma organização da sociedade civil, sem fins lucrativos" describing itself as "plural, didática, gratuita e suprapartidária"; its 2022 presidential guide organises proposals by five themes and sends readers to "the official government proposals of all presidential candidates" on the TSE site, with no scoring. Funding: small individual donations (R$5–R$120), foundations, and paid services to schools and government. Sources: https://www.politize.com.br/propostas-dos-presidenciaveis/ and https://www.politize.com.br/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

JUDGED. Brazil offers the best and worst. Best: a legal duty for executive candidates to file a written programme with the electoral court, which any site can quote and link. Worst: a privately funded score that admits "We are not neutral" in the small print while broadcasters present it as neutral technology. Any score, however "transparent", becomes a campaign asset for whoever it favours.

#### 11b. Mexico: INE "Candidatas y Candidatos, Conóceles"

OBSERVED. By 20 March 2024, 2 of 3 presidential candidates and 2,373 congressional candidates had filed. The INE disclaims content: "La información contenida en el portal ... es responsabilidad de los actores políticos" (the political actors are responsible for the content). Source: INE Central Electoral, https://centralelectoral.ine.mx/2024/03/20/invita-ine-a-ciudadania-a-consultar-sistema-candidatas-y-candidatos-conoceles/ (accessed 25 Sep 2026). Portal: https://candidaturas.ine.mx/.

JUDGED. When the electoral authority hosts the questionnaire, participation approaches 94%; when a charity does, it is 74% at best (smartvote ACT) and usually unpublished. That gap is the strongest argument in this file for lobbying the Electoral Commission or Democracy Club to make candidate statements part of the nomination workflow.

### 12. Global standards

OBSERVED. ACE Electoral Knowledge Network distinguishes voter information ("the date, time, and place of voting; the type of election; identification ... registration requirements; and mechanisms for voting"), which is "the responsibility of the election authority", from voter education, which "can easily be viewed as the responsibility of both of the election authority and civil society", and civic education. Its principles for providers are neutrality, accessibility, timeliness and accuracy; it does not treat candidate information as a distinct category. Sources: ACE, "Voter Education" topic pages, https://aceproject.org/ace-en/topics/ve/vea and .../vea03 (accessed 25 Sep 2026). International IDEA and NDI guidance pages on voter information and on VAAs returned 404s; the Lausanne Declaration on VAAs (vaa-research.net) could not be fetched. Their specific guidance is therefore unverified in this file; only the generic principles above are confirmed. Wikipedia's VAA article summarises the research: two sourcing approaches ("candidate self-completion" versus positions "taken from party programmes and debates" by journalists or experts); reported effects of "More than half of the VAA users declared to have been motivated to do further research", turnout effects of 8–22% in early studies, declared vote switching of 3–10% but only "1% actual change" in Belgium; and the criticism that VAAs "can never give neutral voting advice". It lists almost no VAAs outside Europe (only Israel's 2009 compass, 600,000 visitors). Source: Wikipedia, "Voting advice application", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_advice_application (accessed 25 Sep 2026).

JUDGED. The standards literature has a blind spot: it covers "how to vote" (information) and "why vote" (education) but has little to say about "who is on my ballot and what have they said", which is our product. That is an opportunity for our /about page to state our own principles, borrowing from Elections ACT (equal treatment) and g0v (no ranking, provenance rule).

## Part C. The 15 most useful lessons for What's It To Me?

1. A no-quiz, document-sourced comparison can reach one in six voters. Policy.nz did it in 2020 and 2023 with plain-language summaries and links to sources, and explicitly "not using a quiz-based format or algorithms". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy.nz and https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PO2609/S00223/policynz-returns-for-the-2026-election.htm

2. Put a one-line plain-English headline above every verbatim quote. Policy.nz's whole usability rests on "simplified policy statements ... that use consistent language" (for example "Ban synthetic nitrogen fertilisers over time"). We keep the quote as the claim and add the headline as a label, clearly marked as ours. https://policy.nz/2023/party-vote/policies/economy/policy/POL53765

3. Offer a "hide candidate names" toggle. Policy.nz lets readers browse "with party names hidden or visible". It lets a reader test their assumptions without any scoring, and it is cheap to build on our /ballot/[id]/compare page. https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PO2609/S00223/policynz-returns-for-the-2026-election.htm

4. Answer "Are you telling me how to vote?" in those words, on the About page. Policy.nz: "Your vote is yours, use it how you like. Policy.nz exists to make it easier to understand what the candidates want to do with your vote, but there's a lot more to voting than this." https://policy.nz/about

5. Label every self-reported fact "self-declared" and say the original beats the copy. MyNeta: "This information is an archive of the candidate's self-declared affidavit" and "in case of discrepancy ... the ECI website should be treated as correct." Our ledger already links sources; add the responsibility label on the face of each quote (candidate statement, party manifesto, leaflet, Hansard). https://www.myneta.info/

6. Publish a readability or confidence flag when the source was hard to read. MyNeta's "Data Readability Report of Criminal Cases" is an honest per-record warning. Our equivalent: a flag when a leaflet scan or PDF was partly illegible. https://www.myneta.info/TamilNadu2026/candidate.php?candidate_id=2062

7. Never write "voted against X"; show the division and the question put. They Vote For You's Jane Hume case (votes against crossbench amendments summarised as "consistently voted against creating a federal Anti-Corruption Commission") led to legal threats and a public admission that the site records "formal votes only". https://patleslie.net/blogs/blog_tvfy/tvfy_post

8. Adopt g0v's two written rules: "no value judgements or rankings on issues", and any non-official data must be "citizen-collaboratively edited or self-edited by candidates". The second rule is a provenance test we can apply to every ledger entry. https://github.com/g0v/twly-voter-guide

9. Cite Elections ACT as the official precedent for equal treatment: same font, same size, order by lot, every candidate listed "regardless of whether that candidate has submitted a candidate information statement". https://www.elections.act.gov.au/elections/education/fact-sheets/fact-sheet-candidate-statements

10. Let candidates review what is attributed to them before polling day. Vote Compass: "All the parties were consulted and invited to review the findings and provide feedback." Vote Pilipinas sought "the candidates' feedback on their profile". Build a Ballot publishes a dated timetable (cut-off, questionnaire out, assessments sent, deadline, go-live). We can do this without matching, via /candidates/submit and a published timetable per election. https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/newfoundland-labrador/vote-compass-explainer-1.5891260 and https://www.buildaballot.org.au/candidate-information

11. Expect 74% candidate participation at best for a charity questionnaire, and near 94% only when the electoral authority hosts it. smartvote ACT: 101 of 137; INE Conóceles: 93.61%. Design every page to be complete and fair when a candidate has said nothing, and say plainly why the section is empty. https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/6957405/ and https://mvsnoticias.com/nacional/2025/3/31/conoceles-ine-reporta-registro-de-informacion-de-mas-del-93-de-candidaturas-judiciales-678945.html

12. Keep figures unblended and reviewed. Mzalendo: "Profile facts are drawn from retained primary-authority records and published only after independent review" and "separated by House, so figures are never blended or misleading." Apply to our PolicyEngine grid: never mix two elections' or two offices' effects on one page. https://mzalendo.com/about-data-analysis/

13. Compare a returning candidate with their own past statements. MyNeta's "re-contesting candidate comparisons" show change between elections. We hold dated quotes; for the May 2027 locals we can show what a re-standing councillor said in 2023 next to 2027. https://www.myneta.info/

14. Plan the after-election page now. Del Dicho al Hecho turns programmes into a tracked record (Boric 44% compliance, 2025). Our append-only ledger makes a "what they said, then what happened" page for the Holborn and St Pancras winner straightforward. https://www.deldichoalhecho.cl/

15. Keep the domain and archive the pages. yoquierosaber.org now serves gambling spam; votepilipinas.com carries hotel listings. Register whatsittome.org long-term, snapshot every ballot page to the Internet Archive on polling day, and keep /ledger reachable after each election. https://yoquierosaber.org/ and https://votepilipinas.com/

## Part D. Mistakes to avoid (each observed abroad)

Percentages and colours become verdicts. Build a Ballot's green/yellow/red bands and Vote Compass's match percentages are read as advice despite disclaimers. Japanese users get different "closest parties" from different newspapers because of wording and arithmetic (allatanys.jp; ill-identified diary). Our rule against scoring is correct; also avoid anything that looks like a score, including sortable league tables (MyNeta's "most cases" list).

An editorial title on top of raw data. They Vote For You's policy titles turned votes on amendments into "voted against an anti-corruption commission". If we ever label a quote by topic, the label must be neutral ("Housing", not "Against new homes").

Paraphrase without the source on the face of the claim. Policy.nz summaries are contestable because the reader sees the summary first and the source later. Keep the quote first, the source visible, and any headline of ours visibly ours.

A cause-driven owner running a "neutral" tool. Build a Ballot (climate charity), Bijak Memilih (youth policy NGO), Ranking dos Políticos (business owners with a stated agenda: "We are not neutral"). Our /who-we-are and funding disclosures must stay boringly complete.

Naming and shaming. Mzalendo's scorecards changed MP behaviour but are "mostly seen as sensationalism" elsewhere and one triggered a recall petition. We inform; we do not rank.

Blending sources or elections. Mzalendo's warning ("figures are never blended") and They Vote For You's inability to distinguish absence from abstention both show what happens when unlike data are averaged.

Compound questions and forced scales. The Vote Compass 2011 critique ("Questions with more than one question in them") applies to any household-profile question we ask on /profile: one fact per question, bands not points.

Running the tool as a poll. Vote Compass publishes weighted "findings" from its users. If we ever publish aggregate usage, say plainly it is not a poll (Mzalendo: "this is not a measure of public opinion").

Going dark between elections. On the Fence, smartvote Australia, g0v guides, Shine Your Eye and vote4.hk all stalled. Our daily GitHub Actions refresh and the 26 listed elections help; the risk is the human editorial load, which Policy.nz solves by re-fundraising every cycle.

Letting the domain lapse. See lesson 15.

## Part E. Ideas no UK site does yet (all consistent with "never rank, score or recommend")

Hide-the-names reading mode. A toggle on /ballot/[id]/compare that blanks candidate and party names so readers compare quoted positions on their merits, then reveals them (Policy.nz 2026).

"Self-declared" and "who is responsible" labels on every quote. Each quote carries a short label of who wrote it and who is answerable for its truth (candidate statement, party document, official record), following MyNeta and the INE's "the political actors are responsible for the content".

Per-candidate "what we could not find" box. Korea and India show voters five years of tax, assets, military service and criminal record; the UK shows almost nothing. A plain list of what is legally unavailable in the UK, per candidate, is more honest than a blank.

Candidate pre-publication review with a public timetable. Publish per election: quote cut-off date, date candidates were sent their page, deadline for corrections, go-live. Log every correction in /ledger (Build a Ballot timetable; Vote Compass party review).

Practise ballot with "check my ballot paper". For May 2027 multi-member wards, an AEC-style dummy ballot (fictional names) that tells the reader whether their marks would count.

Returning-candidate comparison. For anyone who stood before, show their earlier dated quotes next to the current ones (MyNeta re-contest comparison), without comment.

After-election "what happened" page. Keep the winner's quotes and append dated records of relevant votes, council decisions or Hansard entries as they occur (Del Dicho al Hecho, Mzalendo promise tracker), again without a compliance percentage.

Data-provenance rule in the ledger. Adopt g0v's test: anything not from an official record must be either candidate-written or openly co-editable, and the ledger says which.

Audio version of each ballot page. Taiwan's CEC records audio bulletins for blind voters; a generated audio reading of each candidate's quotes, in ballot order, is cheap and no UK site offers it.

Open spreadsheet of every quote per election. The Reporter released all 2018 mayoral platforms "as an open spreadsheet for researchers". Our /data page can export each ballot's quotes with sources on nomination close.

Bulk archive on polling day. Automatic Internet Archive snapshots of every /ballot page on the eve of the poll, linked from /ledger, so the record cannot be disputed later.
