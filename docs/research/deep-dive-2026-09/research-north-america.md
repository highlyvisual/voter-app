# North American voter-information tools: what they do well and badly

Deep-dive for What's It To Me? (whatsittome.org). Written 25 September 2026. All URLs were accessed on 25 September 2026 unless stated. Where a page could not be fetched today, the entry says so and the claim is marked **unverified**.

Conventions used below: **OBSERVED** = taken from a named source, quoted or closely paraphrased. **JUDGED** = my opinion about what it means for What's It To Me? (WITM).

A note on method. Pages were fetched with the WebFetch tool or, where a site blocks automated readers, with a plain browser-style request and text extraction. Ballotpedia, lwv.org, the Internet Archive, leginfo.legislature.ca.gov, the New York Times and the Washington Post all blocked at least one method. Elections Canada returned server errors all afternoon. My search budget ran out about two-thirds of the way through, so the last third of the research relied on fetching URLs I already knew; gaps are flagged.

## Part A. Tool-by-tool findings

### 1. Ballotpedia (Lucy Burns Institute) — the encyclopaedia with a sample-ballot lookup and a candidate survey

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** Ballotpedia is published by the Lucy Burns Institute, a 501(c)(3) in Middleton, Wisconsin, founded by Leslie Graves in 2006; Ballotpedia launched 30 May 2007. Revenue was $5.37m in 2019 and $8.93m in 2024. Staff was "over 50" in 2021. (Wikipedia, "Ballotpedia", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballotpedia.) Its About page says it has had "more than 1.2 billion page views since our founding in 2007" and that the Sample Ballot Lookup Tool "has helped tens of millions of voters" (Ballotpedia, "Ballotpedia:About", https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:About). Ballotpedia does not publish a donor list. A critical piece by Kyle Tharp (Chaotic Era, 22 Oct 2025, https://www.chaoticera.news/p/this-popular-election-resource-is-funded-by-far-right-donors-does-it-matter) says DonorsTrust and Donors Capital Fund have given over $30m since 2013 (at least $3.9m in 2023), with 2023 gifts from the Sarah Scaife Foundation ($350k), Bradley Foundation ($200k), Bradley Impact Fund ($200k), Ed Uihlein Family Foundation ($100k) and Searle Freedom Trust ($75k); Tharp estimates conservative donors supply "approximately 65–70% of annual revenue". I could not check those figures against the Form 990 today, so treat the percentages as **unverified**. InfluenceWatch (itself a conservative Capital Research Center site) confirms Donors Capital Fund, Searle and Bradley as donors and notes board members "have affiliations with the Republican Party" (https://www.influencewatch.org/non-profit/lucy-burns-institute/). Ballotpedia also sells data ("Do you want a spreadsheet of this type of data? Contact our sales team" appears under every results table) and its survey terms say it "reserves the right to license access to all or a portion of this database" (Ballotpedia, "Survey: Frequently Asked Questions", https://ballotpedia.org/Survey:_Frequently_Asked_Questions).

**What it shows for an address (OBSERVED).** The upgraded Sample Ballot Lookup (Feb 2024) shows, for an address, "candidates and ballot measures specific to their location, including candidate biographies, survey responses, past election results, and ballot measure details with arguments for and against each measure", plus "candidate comparisons", "time to use estimates", "user-controlled information detail levels", policy explainers linked to survey keywords and English/Spanish (Ballotpedia News, 6 Feb 2024, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2024/02/06/announcing-ballotpedias-upgraded-sample-ballot-lookup-tool/).

**How positions are sourced and attributed (OBSERVED).** Two channels. (a) The Candidate Connection survey, written by the candidate. (b) Staff-written "key messages" for races Ballotpedia judges competitive: staff "identify key messages in races we expect to be competitive", covering "all major party candidates" in selected general elections and minor-party candidates only "when there are three or fewer total candidates running". Crucially: "Ballotpedia's policy is to publish key messages provided by the candidate in their survey response over those compiled by Ballotpedia staff", and if a candidate submits a survey after staff research, "our policy is to take down the staff-researched messages in favor of the candidate's own messages". If one candidate in a race gets a quote instead of full key-message coverage, "every candidate with staff-researched key messages should also receive a quote instead of full coverage" — a parity rule. (Ballotpedia, "Editorial approach to writing about key campaign messages", https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:Editorial_approach_to_writing_about_key_campaign_messages.) Endorsements come in four tiers (candidate-reported, "Recognized Endorsement Contributors", staff-researched, editorially-selected) and "Ballotpedia does not verify endorsements listed in the Candidate Connection survey" (https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:Our_approach_to_covering_endorsements).

**Ranking / matching (OBSERVED).** None. Candidate order is fixed by rule: incumbents first, then major-party candidates alphabetically by party, then minor parties, then independents, then write-ins, and the page warns "the candidate list order on Ballotpedia may not match the order of candidates on your ballot when you vote" (https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:How_we_order_candidate_lists). **JUDGED:** WITM's choice of ballot-paper order is better for a voter standing in a polling booth; Ballotpedia's warning line is worth copying in reverse ("This is the order on your ballot paper").

**Candidate participation (OBSERVED).** Survey is free, open to every candidate at every level, takes "around 30 minutes", can be saved and resumed, is identity-verified via a photo-ID upload through the Truepic app (ID kept six months after the November general election, then deleted). Key questions: "Who are you? Tell us about yourself in 200 words or less", "Please list below 3 key messages of your campaign", "What areas of public policy are you personally passionate about?", plus lighter questions ("What was the last song that got stuck in your head?"). Not every question is required. Response rates (excluding the 2024 ultra-local pilot): 2018 1,957 respondents (6.9%; the denominator is not stated in the source and works out at about 28,400); 2020 4,593 in contested races (19.3% of contested); 2022 6,087 of 31,452 (19.1%); 2024 5,414 of 28,246 (19.2%, "the highest since the initiative began in 2018"). In 2024 a further 1,127 of 68,921 ultra-local candidates responded (about 1.6%). State-legislative candidates were 41% of 2024 respondents; congressional 18%. (Ballotpedia News, 16 Jan 2025, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/01/16/6541-candidates-responded-to-ballotpedias-candidate-connection-survey-in-2024/; Ballotpedia News, 6 Feb 2023, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2023/02/06/6087-candidates-responded-to-ballotpedias-candidate-connection-survey-last-year/; Ballotpedia, "Ballotpedia's 2024 Candidate Connection report", https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia%27s_2024_Candidate_Connection_report.)

**How non-responders are shown (OBSERVED).** Quietly. On election pages a small icon marks each candidate who completed the survey, with the legend "= candidate completed the Ballotpedia Candidate Connection survey" and a line "If you are a candidate and would like to tell readers and voters more about why they should vote for you, complete the Ballotpedia Candidate Connection Survey." A non-responder's profile simply lacks the "Campaign themes" section; there is no "declined" label (observed on https://ballotpedia.org/John_Avlon and https://ballotpedia.org/George_Santos).

**Editing and disputes (OBSERVED).** "Ballotpedia does not fact-check responses" but does verify identity. Typos are fixed on request only. Substantive changes are not made: Ballotpedia "will offer to add a timestamped custom note from the candidate above the original response"; removal requests are refused, with the same offer; a second survey is published above the first, both timestamped. Any Ballotpedia edit is "clearly marked with [brackets]". Ballotpedia "will remove sexually explicit content, calls to violence, and hate speech from people who are using it to target others". Third parties disputing a response are asked for "a credible source". (https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:Our_approach_to_Candidate_Connection_survey_edit_and_removal_requests.) Errors go to editor@ballotpedia.org or a form; "All errors are expected to be corrected within 24 hours"; Ballotpedia "does not publish a list of corrections to each article, but we do keep an internal, comprehensive log" (https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:How_we_approach_error_corrections). The neutral-point-of-view page is two sentences plus an email address (https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:Neutral_point_of_view_policy). The editorial-independence page: donors get "no right of review or influence of editorial content" (https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia%27s_editorial_independence_policy).

**Data licence / API (OBSERVED).** No open licence found; data is sold. A copyright-policy page linked from every policy box returned "page does not exist" today.

**Design features worth borrowing.** The append-only, timestamped, candidate-note-above-original approach to disputes (URL above); the parity rule for staff-written summaries; the "[brackets]" convention for editor changes; the "time to use" estimate on the ballot tool.

**Criticisms and failures (OBSERVED then JUDGED).** Funding concentration and opacity (Tharp; InfluenceWatch). Tharp's own conclusion is that the "explicit content appears largely nonpartisan" but the "financial architecture" is not transparent. **JUDGED:** the lesson is that "our content is neutral" does not settle the question if the money is hidden; WITM's public ledger and published funding page are the right instinct. Second failure: the corrections log is private, so readers cannot see the error history. Third: 19% is the ceiling a very large, well-funded outfit reaches after seven years of asking.

### 2. VOTE411 (League of Women Voters Education Fund) — the volunteer-run questionnaire guide

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** Launched October 2006 by the League of Women Voters Education Fund (LWVEF); "strictly nonpartisan; it neither supports nor opposes candidates" (https://www.vote411.org/about). LWV's combined c3/c4 revenue for FY2024-25 was $24,100,900 (LWV Annual Report 2024–25, https://www.lwv.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/LWV%20Annual%20Report,%202024-25.pdf).

**Usage (OBSERVED).** 9.19 million users in 2024, "nearly 56,000 candidates" and "nearly 29,000 races"; a new campaign-finance tool for federal candidates; traffic nearly doubled after Taylor Swift linked to it (LWV, "2024 Election Impact Report", https://www.lwv.org/2024-election-impact). 7.6 million users in FY25; over 55,000 candidates in nearly 30,000 elections for November 2024 (Annual Report, above).

**What it shows for an address (OBSERVED).** Enter address (and party, for primaries); see only the races on your ballot; each candidate has a photo, profile and answers to the same questions; non-responders show "a placeholder photo"; at the end a "Finish" button offers to "print your selections" to take to the polls. "Your address will never be saved, sold or used for marketing." The Alachua League notes the interface "lacks a side-by-side comparison feature" (LWV Alachua, "How to Use the VOTE411 Voter Guide", https://my.lwv.org/florida/alachua/article/how-use-vote411-voter-guide).

**Candidate participation method (OBSERVED).** Local and state Leagues write the questions, email each candidate a personal link, and chase by post, email, text, Facebook and phone. The Utah League's instructions: "Candidate responses are limited to 750 characters (including spaces and punctuation)"; responses appear verbatim; no editing except for layout; no libel, no copyrighted uploads; if a candidate does not answer, "their name and campaign profile still appear online, but marked: 'The candidate has not yet responded'"; candidates may add or revise answers at any time up to and after publication (LWV Utah, "VOTE411 Instructions", https://www.lwvutah.org/vote411-instructions). New Mexico's written policy: "Strict word limits are enforced, and all responses are printed in paragraph form"; the League does "not edit for meaning, grammar, punctuation, spelling, or accuracy" except where content appears defamatory or false, in which case the candidate is asked to reword or the answer is not printed; non-respondents get "No reply received"; candidates get "at least two weeks" from mailing (LWVNM, "Voter Guide Policy", https://www.lwvnm.org/BDocs/Policy/VoterGuidePolicy.php). Houston: "Candidate responses are published exactly as submitted"; the League "reserves the right to exclude responses containing obscenities, slurs, or egregious personal attacks"; late candidates are listed with "No response received"; "inclusion does not imply League endorsement" (LWV Houston, https://www.lwvhouston.org/voters-guide). The Mid-Hudson League's wording is "Candidate Has Not Responded" (https://jessica50f.substack.com/p/league-of-women-voters-mid-hudson).

**Response rates (OBSERVED).** LWV does not publish a national rate. Two state Leagues do: North Carolina 2022 primary, "66% of the invited candidates participated" (524 of about 800); "64% of candidates who won their primary race (199) had responded" (LWV North Carolina, "VOTE411 and Voter Services", https://my.lwv.org/north-carolina-state/committee/vote411-and-voter-services). Nebraska 2024 primary: 189 candidates in 63 races contacted, 115 responded, "61% overall response rate"; every candidate was posted a card and then chased by email, text, Facebook or phone, "sometimes repeatedly" (LWV Nebraska, https://lwvnebraska.org/vote-411-can-be-used-for-general-election/). **JUDGED:** the difference between Ballotpedia's 19% and the League's 61–66% is the chasing. A short questionnaire, a personal link, and repeated human follow-up from a local, known organisation roughly triples the rate.

**How it stops candidates attacking each other (OBSERVED).** Not by a rule against naming opponents (I found none in League policies) but by four devices: identical questions for all; a hard character limit; verbatim publication with no editing; and a reserved right to withhold answers that are defamatory, obscene or "egregious personal attacks". For live events, the League's debate FAQ sets the standard: criteria for inviting candidates must be "nonpartisan, fair and applied objectively" and fixed "well before any candidates are invited"; speaking order is "determined by random draw"; for federal races "A debate must be canceled if only one candidate remains" because an empty-chair debate would be an in-kind contribution (LWV, "FAQ's: League Candidate Forums and Debates", https://my.lwv.org/sites/default/files/candidate_forum_faqs_2019.pdf).

**Impartiality complaints (OBSERVED).** Handled locally. Example: LWV Amarillo explained why some candidates were missing from a 2026 primary guide — wrong email addresses supplied at filing, missed deadlines, and candidates forwarding their personal links to each other so answers were overwritten (AOL/Amarillo Globe-News, https://www.aol.com/articles/league-explains-why-candidate-didnt-144328100.html). **JUDGED:** the forwarded-link failure is a real risk for any small site using magic links; WITM's candidate-submit flow should bind a link to one candidate and one device, or use a code the candidate confirms.

**Data licence / API.** None found.

**Design worth borrowing.** "The candidate has not yet responded" (present tense, leaves the door open) rather than "declined"; placeholder photo as the visible signal; the "help get candidates to answer" page with sample social posts that voters can send to their own candidates (https://www.vote411.org/candidate-contact).

**Criticisms / failures (JUDGED).** No central response-rate reporting; quality depends on the local League; no side-by-side comparison; the "print your selections" feature edges toward a voting plan (fine) but WITM should not add a "pick" feature because its whole promise is not to steer.

### 3. BallotReady — the for-profit, API-first ballot

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** Founded 2015 in Chicago by Aviva Rosman and Alex Niemczewski; early money from a $100,000 University of Chicago Social New Venture Challenge prize, the National Science Foundation and the Knight Foundation (InfluenceWatch, https://www.influencewatch.org/for-profit/ballotready/ — a conservative source that labels BallotReady "Left of Center" without giving specific criticisms). It is a for-profit; in 2016 Niemczewski said "The data will be sold to the public sector as a way to generate revenue to ensure that the website's information remains free for voters" (GovTech, https://www.govtech.com/gov-experience/BallotReady-Website-A-Valuable-Data-Source-for-Government.html). Its homepage lists NSF, Snapchat, Spotify, Miami Heat, Knight Foundation and University of Chicago as customers, and says "millions of Americans are voting informed this election" (https://www.ballotready.org/). About page: "helps tens of millions of people each year" (https://www.ballotready.org/about).

**What it shows for an address (OBSERVED).** Full ballot down to school board and judges; candidate biography, issue stances, endorsements; polling place; registration. Optional email capture on the address form.

**How positions are sourced (OBSERVED).** Its research page is unusually explicit: candidate lists come from election administrators; biographies from five source types (campaign website, campaign social media, personal social media, LinkedIn, officeholder websites); an issue stance must be a "succinct, specific, and actionable statement" of what the candidate will do; stances are taken "exclusively" from campaign websites "using direct quotes"; social media is excluded because posts are "incredibly extensive and variable"; "issue stances are not fact checked" but the forward-looking rule limits false claims; endorsements come from advocacy, business, labour, political and media organisations, sorted by issue, and "Private citizen endorsements are excluded"; candidates can ask for changes and get a reply "within 24 hours" (BallotReady, "Research process", https://www.ballotready.org/research-process). In 2016 "Each data point includes source attribution" (GovTech, above).

**Ranking / matching (OBSERVED).** Its API describes ballot data "for voter matching" and endorsements filterable by organisation (https://organizations.ballotready.org/ballotready-api). **JUDGED:** filtering by "organisations you trust" is a soft recommendation engine. WITM should not copy it.

**Candidate participation.** No survey; candidates can request corrections. No response-rate concept.

**Data licence / API (OBSERVED).** GraphQL API, "over 1,100 peak API requests per second", data on "over 200,000 officeholders"; pricing by demo request; commercial terms (API page above).

**Criticisms / failures (JUDGED).** Business model means the same data feeds partisan clients' apps; the consumer site's neutrality claim rests on the research rules, not on the ownership. The "direct quotes from campaign websites only" rule is a strength (it is close to WITM's word-for-word rule) but it means candidates without websites have empty pages.

### 4. Voter's Edge California (MapLight + LWV California Education Fund) — the best personalised guide, now closed

**Who ran it and funding (OBSERVED).** MapLight (Berkeley nonprofit founded 2005 by Daniel Newman; funders included Sunlight Foundation, Open Society, MacArthur, Ford, Tides and Democracy Alliance donors — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MapLight) with the LWV California Education Fund. Candidates were invited by email "soon after the close of the candidate filing period" to fill in "priorities, a biography, achievements, endorsements, a political vision, and goals" plus photo, video and social links, "at no charge" (Mono County candidate letter, Nov 2018, https://monocounty.ca.gov/sites/default/files/fileattachments/elections/page/29411/vec_candidate_letter_of_introduction_nov_2018.pdf; Santa Clara County copy, https://files.santaclaracounty.gov/exjcpb1296/migrated/Voter's%20Edge%20Election%20Guide.pdf).

**What it showed (OBSERVED).** For an address: "candidate priorities, biographies, endorsements, top donors, ballot measure explanations, campaign funding details, and news articles" (MapLight, 5 Oct 2020, https://maplightarchive.org/story/voters-edge-california-launches-to-guide-voters-and-fight-misinformation/). Usage: "approximately two million California voters used Voter's Edge" in 2018, "roughly 1 in 8 state voters"; "Over 75% of users reported voting for more offices and leaving fewer choices blank"; "more than 90%" said it increased confidence (same source; self-reported survey, so treat as indicative). MapLight's 2022 Senate testimony repeats "two million voters in California each election season" (https://selc.senate.ca.gov/sites/selc.senate.ca.gov/files/MapLight%20-%20Testimony%20for%20Nov%2029%202022.pdf).

**What happened to it (OBSERVED).** "Voter's Edge, a successful decade-long partnership between MapLight and the League of Women Voters of California Education Fund (LWV), has ended"; users are sent to VOTE411 (MapLight, https://www.maplight.org/votersedge; votersedge.org now redirects there). "VOTE411 replaced Voter's Edge for all California local League of Women Voters starting January 2024" (LWV Marin, https://www.marinlwv.org/vote411-team). The LWVC biennial report says only that it "made a major switch" and that voting-process content "had been available on Voter's Edge, but didn't have a place on Vote411", so cavotes.org was rebuilt to hold it (https://lwvc.org/biennial-report/). No public reason was given. **JUDGED:** the most-loved feature set in the field (candidate's own priorities + funding + endorsements + measures on one page) died because it depended on a bespoke platform run by a small nonprofit whose own finances were shrinking, not because voters stopped using it. Lesson for WITM: keep the data model exportable and the running cost tiny.

### 5. Vote Smart (Project Vote Smart) — the Political Courage Test and its collapse

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** Founded 1992 by Richard Kimball; refuses money from corporations, unions, parties and lobbying groups; major donors have included Ford, Carnegie and Knight; "As of 2025, the organization reported $941,346 in revenue against $1.48 million in expenses"; laid off six staff in 2014; now at Drake University, Iowa (Wikipedia, "Vote Smart", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_Smart).

**Method (OBSERVED).** The Political Courage Test asks candidates to state positions on a fixed list of issues drawn from "national and local polls, the majority, minority and third party platforms, State of the Union and Response speeches" and so on. "Those candidates who provide their answers will pass no matter what those answers are." Those who refuse "fail", and Vote Smart then compiles positions from "public statements, voting record, and special interest group ratings and endorsements", with the limit: "When a candidate's public record lacks clarity on that issue, Vote Smart will not infer an answer" (Vote Smart, "Political Courage Test", https://justfacts.votesmart.org/about/political-courage-test). Candidates may leave up to 30% blank (Wikipedia).

**Response-rate collapse (OBSERVED).** 72% in 1996; 48% by April 2008 (NPR, 10 Apr 2008, https://www.npr.org/2008/04/10/89521656/mccain-booted-by-watchdogs-he-once-aided); 38% in 2010 (WEKU/Kentucky Public Radio, 29 Aug 2011, https://www.weku.org/politics/2011-08-29/fewer-candidates-taking-courage-test); 20% in 2016 (Wikipedia). Why: Kimball in 2008 — "Both major political parties now train their candidates not to provide that information, to stay in control of their message, to stay on their pitch" and "The more money they have, the more ability they have to control message" (NPR). A Florida House Democratic leader: "We tell our candidates not to do it. It sets them up for a hit piece" (Wikipedia). Vote Smart spokeswoman in 2011: candidates "really want to be able to control their message" (WEKU). In 2008 Vote Smart removed John McCain, a founding board member, from its board for refusing to take the test (NPR).

**VoteEasy (OBSERVED).** Launched 2010; lets voters compare their positions with candidates' (Wikipedia). **JUDGED:** VoteEasy is a matching tool built on inferred positions for the 60–80% who refused; the inference step is exactly the thing WITM has ruled out.

**Impartiality (OBSERVED).** "Vote Smart does not permit the use of its name or programs in any partisan activity, including advertising" (PCT page). **Data/API:** a research API existed (pvsR package, PLOS ONE 2015 — Wikipedia); current terms not checked.

**Criticisms / failures (JUDGED).** The "courage" framing — labelling non-responders as failing a courage test — gave parties a reason to boycott and turned a data problem into a fight. Fixed-list yes/no issue questions are easy to weaponise in attack ads, which is the stated reason candidates stopped. WITM's model (quote what the candidate has already published; ask for nothing that is not already public) avoids the trap.

### 6. OpenSecrets and FollowTheMoney — money data

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** OpenSecrets is the 2021 merger of the Center for Responsive Politics (founded 1983) and the National Institute on Money in Politics (Helena, Montana; FollowTheMoney.org). Donors since 2020 include Carnegie, Democracy Fund, Hewlett, Omidyar and Open Society; 2023 revenue $2.5m against $4.3m expenses, with layoffs in 2024 (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenSecrets). Income also from "custom research and licensing data for commercial use" (https://www.opensecrets.org/about/).

**How money is presented and caveated (OBSERVED).** The industry methodology page: "Corporations and other organizations are prohibited from making campaign contributions from their treasuries", so contributions are tracked from individuals and PACs affiliated with them, classified by the "employer/occupation" field the donor reports; "not every contribution is made with the donor's economic or professional interests in mind"; "Tens of millions of dollars of contributions in each election cycle are not classified by industry at all"; sub-$200 gifts are not itemised; only about 70% of contributions are categorised; current-cycle totals are "typically updated every three to four weeks"; all figures should be "assumed to be conservative" (https://www.opensecrets.org/industries/methodology.php). The expenditures methodology: itemisation only above $200 per vendor; contributions and expenditures "are often reported at different times and processed at different frequencies", so totals differ across pages (https://www.opensecrets.org/campaign-expenditures/methodology). The well-known per-candidate line "The organizations themselves did not donate, rather the money came from the organizations' PACs, their individual members or employees or owners, and those individuals' immediate families" could not be re-read today because candidate pages returned 403; **unverified wording**.

**Licence / API (OBSERVED).** "As of April 15, 2025, our API offerings have been discontinued" after 17 years; content under Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 3.0; commercial reuse by fee (https://www.opensecrets.org/open-data/api; https://www.opensecrets.org/open-data). FollowTheMoney.org still shows state data "through 2024" but is "no longer actively maintained as integration continues" (https://www.followthemoney.org/).

**JUDGED.** The most useful thing OpenSecrets does is state, on every page, what the number is not. WITM's Electoral Commission party-funding data should carry a similar standing caveat (donations to a party are not donations to a candidate; local candidate spending returns are separate). The API shutdown is a warning against building on someone else's free API without a fallback.

### 7. Sunlight Foundation — the legacy

**OBSERVED.** Founded April 2006 by Ellen Miller and Michael Klein with $3.5m from Klein; funders included Open Society, Pew, Knight, Rockefeller, Ford, Hewlett, Omidyar, Bloomberg and Arnold; ran OpenCongress (retired 2016, users sent to GovTrack), Influence Explorer, Politwoops (now ProPublica), Open States and Sunlight Labs (closed September 2016); 2017 revenue $495,097 against $1,451,359 expenses; closed September 2020, with Klein saying "Virtually all of the activities and staff of Sunlight have been transferred to other engaged institutions, or closed" (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight_Foundation). **JUDGED:** the tools that survived (GovTrack, Open States, Politwoops) were the ones with a simple data model and a new home. Design for hand-over.

### 8. Democracy Works: TurboVote, Voting Information Project, Ballot Scout

**OBSERVED.** Democracy Works is a 501(c)(3). TurboVote: "60M+ election notifications sent through TurboVote in 2024"; "79% of TurboVote users voted in the 2024 General Election, 15 percentage points higher than the national average" (self-reported); it published data for 3,462 elections in 2025; partners include TikTok, Meta, Google, Anthropic and Perplexity (https://www.democracy.works/about). The Voting Information Project "was created in 2008 as a joint effort of state and local election officials, The Pew Charitable Trusts, and Google", run by Democracy Works since 2018, covering "more than 40 states and the District of Columbia", publishing polling places, early-vote sites, drop boxes and "ballot details" through the Google Civic Information API; it does "NOT rely on artificial intelligence to gather, review, or publish information"; funders include Google, Pew and MacKenzie Scott's Yield Giving (https://www.votinginfoproject.org/about). Ballot Scout is postal-ballot tracking, "licensed and operated by Enhanced Voting", with notifications in 11 languages (https://ballotscout.org/). **JUDGED:** VIP shows that the boring layer (where and when to vote, what is on the ballot) is what search engines and platforms will actually integrate; WITM's Democracy Club feed is the UK equivalent, and the "no AI in the pipeline" pledge is a trust signal worth stating explicitly if it is true of WITM's own pipeline.

### 9. Google Civic Information API

**OBSERVED.** Free; up to 25,000 queries a day; US residential addresses only; data from VIP; voter information "intended to be accurate only for that particular election" (https://developers.google.com/civic-information). The current v2 reference lists only `elections`, `voterinfo` and `divisions`; the old representative-lookup endpoints no longer appear and the representatives page returns 404 (https://developers.google.com/civic-information/docs/v2). I could not confirm the announced turn-down date for the Representatives API today; **unverified** (my recollection is spring 2025). **JUDGED:** together with the OpenSecrets API closure, this is two major free civic APIs withdrawn within a year. WITM already caches upstream data in its own Postgres; keep doing that.

### 10. ISideWith

**OBSERVED.** Founded March 2012 by Nick Boutelier and Taylor Peck; "no affiliations with any investors, shareholders, political party or interest group"; donations via PayPal (https://www.isidewith.com/about). Matches users to candidates through a long yes/no quiz with weighted importance (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISideWith). Candidate positions are partly self-entered through a candidate login (the login page could not be fetched; **unverified**). Criticisms: a 2019 test that ran the quiz about 3,000 times with random answers found all candidates scoring above 50% on average, one candidate (Williamson) significantly disadvantaged (p = 0.0001) and score drift for the same candidate between the 2016 and 2020 quizzes "despite identical questions", which the author read as hidden "balancing and counter-biasing mechanisms" (Christopher Cinq-Mars Jarvis, 19 Dec 2019, https://blog.cinqmarsmedia.com/impartiality-in-politics-an-analysis-of-isidewith-coms-popular-quiz-bb6c9f4a8d3a). A 2020 review found "minimal bias in favor of the left" but rated several question descriptions poorly for one-sided framing, e.g. a wealth-tax description giving only the case for, and a question phrased as "giving terrorists constitutional rights" (The Bipartisan Press, 2 Feb 2020, https://www.thebipartisanpress.com/politics/what-is-isidewiths-bias/). No usage figures verified. **JUDGED:** every criticism of ISideWith is a criticism of the matching step — question framing, weighting, and opaque scoring. There is no version of a match that is not an editorial act.

### 11. Guides.vote

**OBSERVED.** Small nonprofit, "fiscally sponsored by Youth Service America"; guides began in 2012 for campuses; distributed with partners such as Ben & Jerry's, NAACP and Vote.org (https://guides.vote/about/). A guide (2026 Maine US Senate) is organised as 14 policy areas, each with a question and each candidate's answer built from three parts: a bold one-word assessment ("Mixed", "Mostly yes", "Increase", "Tighten"), a one-line summary, then sourced claims with inline hyperlinks to Congress.gov, news or the candidate's site. It covers "candidates polling 15% or more" and states "We do not support or oppose any political party or candidate" (https://guides.vote/guide/2026-maine-us-senate-voters-guide/). **JUDGED:** the sourcing discipline is good; the one-word assessment is the site itself characterising a candidate, which WITM does not do. Also note the 15% polling threshold — a UK site listing all 15 Holborn and St Pancras candidates is doing something Guides.vote deliberately does not.

### 12. Vote Compass (Vox Pop Labs / CBC) and The Political Compass — Canada and the matching model

**Who runs it (OBSERVED).** Vox Pop Labs, a social enterprise founded by Clifton van der Linden (McMaster University); "33.6 million surveys completed to date" in 7 countries; media partners CBC/Radio-Canada, ABC, TVNZ, WSJ, Vox Media, Globo, RTL, France 24 (https://voxpoplabs.com/vote-compass/). "more than 50 elections worldwide" (CBC Q&A, below).

**Method (OBSERVED).** Analysts "review all the policy statements that the various parties have made since the last election" to find "issues where the parties diverge", draft propositions with local academics, and "run a series of pilot studies using representative samples" to calibrate; "Don't know" is allowed; results page links to explainers; van der Linden: "Vote Compass is neither telling you who you should vote for nor predicting your vote" (CBC, "Q&A: What you need to know about CBC's Ontario Vote Compass", 23 May 2022, https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/cbc-vote-compass-ontario-election-q-and-a-1.6447708). In 2011 the team "consulted with all parties on positioning and disagreed only when warranted by public statements" (Maclean's, below). Aggregate results are published with the disclaimer: "respondents to Vote Compass are not pre-selected... the data are a non-random sample from the population and have been weighted... by gender, age, education, region, language spoken in the home, and partisanship" (CBC, 23 Apr 2025, https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/vote-compass-election-issues-1.7516039).

**Usage (OBSERVED).** Over one million completions in "slightly more than one week" in 2011 (Maclean's); 101,295 responses logged in a few days after the 2025 debates (CBC, above).

**Criticisms (OBSERVED).** 2011: Sun Media, Conservative campaign chair Guy Giorno and the National Post's Kelly McParland complained that neutral or middling answers produced a Liberal result; political scientists Peter Loewen and van der Linden replied that "Across those 30 issue statements, the Liberals are the most likely to occupy middling positions" (Maclean's, 7 Apr 2011, https://macleans.ca/news/canada/confused-you-may-be-liberal/). Tim Abray (Queen's) argued it oversimplifies vote choice, can override a voter's intuition, and that CBC's brand "magnifies" its influence (CBC Radio "The 180", https://amp.cbc.ca/radio/the180/canadian-election-through-u-s-eyes-canada-s-role-in-the-migrant-crisis-vote-compass-broken-1.3213373/vote-compass-boon-or-bane-to-democracy-1.3213435). Wikipedia records the general worry that such tools "have the potential to become push polling, if the survey questions posed are poorly designed" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_Compass). An academic paper titled "The Origins and Direction of Measurement Error in the 'Vote Compass' on the CBC" exists but could not be read today (**unverified** content).

**The Political Compass (OBSERVED).** Run since 2000 by journalist Wayne Brittenden; 62 propositions, four-option scale with no neutral; criticised for phrasing "impossible to answer properly" (Tom Utley), for dimensionality-reduction methods "roundly criticized" by scholars (van der Linden), for "nonsensical" placements of real politicians (Daniel Mitchell) and for "allegedly propagating libertarian ideas" (Britannica) (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Political_Compass). **JUDGED:** both tools show that once you place parties on a scale you own the placement, and every party will argue about it. WITM's position — quote, cite, do not place — sidesteps the whole dispute.

### 14. Official state voter guides — California and Oregon

**California (OBSERVED).** Statewide candidates "who accept the voluntary expenditure limits" of Proposition 34 "may purchase the space to place a statement in the state voter information guide that does not exceed 250 words. The statement may not make any reference to any opponent of the candidate"; the Secretary of State "may not include" a statement from a candidate who has not accepted the limits; the same applies to State Senate and Assembly candidates in county guides (Government Code §85601, https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=85601). Spending limits for 2025–26 run from $784,000 to $19,611,000 depending on office; deadline for statewide candidates 15 July 2026 (California SoS, "Candidate Statements", https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/candidate-statements). US House candidates "may purchase the space" for 250 words in the county guide; "The statement shall not refer to any opponent" (Elections Code §13307.5). Local nonpartisan candidates: 200 words (up to 400 if the local body allows), "shall not include the party affiliation of the candidate, nor membership or activity in partisan political organizations"; statements "shall be printed in type of uniform size and darkness, and with uniform spacing"; the local agency "may require each candidate filing a statement to pay in advance... the candidate's estimated pro rata share"; and the section does not exempt anyone from liability for "false, slanderous, or libelous statements" (Elections Code §13307). Section 13308: a statement "shall be limited to a recitation of the candidate's own personal background and qualifications, and shall not in any way make reference to other candidates for that office or to another candidate's qualifications, character, or activities", and the elections official "shall not cause to be printed" any statement that breaks this (all three sections fetched from leginfo.legislature.ca.gov, 25 Sep 2026). The printed guide carries, on every measure-argument page: "Arguments printed on this page are the opinions of the authors and have not been checked for accuracy by any official agency", and points readers to the FPPC for each committee's "top 10 contributors" (2024 General Election Official Voter Information Guide, https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2024/general/pdf/complete-vig.pdf). The equivalent candidate-statement disclaimer was not in the pages read today; **unverified**. The actual price per statement was not found today; **unverified**.

**Oregon (OBSERVED).** Any federal or state candidate may file a statement in the State Voters' Pamphlet on payment of a fee or with petition signatures: President/Vice-President $3,500; US Senator and statewide offices $3,000; US Representative $2,500; State Senator/Representative $750; judges and local offices $600; signature alternative 500 (300 for US Representative, 200 for state and county offices); maximum 325 words in one column. Each statement must begin with four required fields — occupation, occupational background, educational background, prior governmental experience — which "is certified as true by the candidate"; false claims in those fields "may be investigated by the Elections Division under ORS 260.715(1)", a Class C felony, with a civil remedy under ORS 260.532. Statements "appear in ballot order"; party order in primaries "rotates every two years". After the deadline the Elections Division checks only that the required fields are present, the photo complies (front-facing, head and shoulders, under four years old, no hands, backgrounds or professional dress) and that the text does not contain "obscene, profane or defamatory language; incite or advocate hatred, abuse or violence toward any person or group; or... language which may not legally be sent through the mail", and that any named endorser has signed a SEL 400 endorsement form or is quoted with source and date. Measure arguments: $1,200 or 500 signatures, 325 words, "Any person or organization may file". Every statement is printed with the line "(This information furnished by [name].)" (Oregon Secretary of State, "State Voters' Pamphlet Manual", https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/Documents/VPManual.pdf; filing page https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/get-involved/Pages/file-voters-pamphlet.aspx; 2026 general pamphlet online 29 Sep 2026, https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/Pages/voters-pamphlet.aspx). The pamphlet's page-level line "The above information has not been verified for accuracy by the State of Oregon" is from memory; **unverified** today.

## Part C. The 15 most useful lessons for What's It To Me?

1. **Publish the response rate and expect about 20% without chasing, 60% with it.** Ballotpedia's 19.2% (2024) versus the League's 61% (Nebraska 2024) and 66% (North Carolina 2022) is the clearest number in this whole field. https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/01/16/6541-candidates-responded-to-ballotpedias-candidate-connection-survey-in-2024/ ; https://lwvnebraska.org/vote-411-can-be-used-for-general-election/

2. **Never delete, never overwrite — timestamp and stack.** Ballotpedia refuses removal, adds a dated note above the original, and marks its own edits in [brackets]. This is the same instinct as WITM's ledger; apply it to candidate statements too. https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:Our_approach_to_Candidate_Connection_survey_edit_and_removal_requests

3. **Use "has not yet responded", not "declined" or "failed".** The League's present-tense label keeps the door open; Vote Smart's "failed the courage test" gave parties a reason to boycott. https://www.lwvutah.org/vote411-instructions ; https://justfacts.votesmart.org/about/political-courage-test

4. **Do not infer positions for candidates who have said nothing.** Vote Smart infers from votes and interest-group ratings; BallotReady leaves the section empty. Empty is honest. https://www.ballotready.org/research-process

5. **Take positions only from what the candidate has published, quoted directly.** BallotReady's "exclusively from campaign websites using direct quotes" rule is close to WITM's and has run at scale since 2015. https://www.ballotready.org/research-process

6. **Apply a parity rule to any editor-written text.** Ballotpedia: if one candidate in a race gets only a quote, every staff-covered candidate in that race gets only a quote. WITM's identical-page rule is the same idea; write it down as policy. https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:Editorial_approach_to_writing_about_key_campaign_messages

7. **Print the caveat next to the money.** OpenSecrets states on its methodology page what is unclassified, unitemised, and conservative; WITM's party-funding panel needs a one-line "this is money to the party, not to this candidate". https://www.opensecrets.org/industries/methodology.php

8. **Say what the calculator is not for, and date it.** KFF: "not definitive for what you will pay"; Tax Foundation: "for educational use only... should not be used for tax preparation"; both date the data. Put an equivalent line on every /stakes page. https://www.kff.org/interactive/subsidy-calculator/ ; https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/federal/tax-calculator-obbba/

9. **State the list of things you did not model.** Tax Foundation's "Modeling Notes" and "Many tax policies remain unspecified" lines are the honest way to show a manifesto's gaps. https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/kamala-harris-tax-plan-2024/

10. **Cache everything upstream; free APIs die.** OpenSecrets' API closed 15 April 2025; Google's representative lookups have gone; Voter's Edge vanished when its platform partner ended the deal. https://www.opensecrets.org/open-data/api ; https://www.maplight.org/votersedge

11. **Offer a "time to use" estimate and a detail dial.** Ballotpedia's 2024 rebuild added both; for a 15-candidate ballot they matter. https://news.ballotpedia.org/2024/02/06/announcing-ballotpedias-upgraded-sample-ballot-lookup-tool/

12. **Give voters a way to nudge candidates.** VOTE411's "help get candidates to answer" page hands voters a ready-made public request. A UK version ("Ask your candidate to publish their positions") costs nothing. https://www.vote411.org/candidate-contact

13. **Verify identity, not truth — and say so.** Ballotpedia verifies the sender (photo ID via app, deleted after six months) and states "Ballotpedia does not fact-check responses". WITM's candidate-submit flow should have a visible equivalent sentence. https://ballotpedia.org/Survey:_Frequently_Asked_Questions

14. **Publish an error-handling promise with a clock.** Ballotpedia commits to 24-hour correction but keeps its log private; WITM's public ledger can do better by showing the log. https://ballotpedia.org/Ballotpedia:How_we_approach_error_corrections

15. **Borrow the pamphlet's required fields and attribution line.** Oregon requires occupation, occupational background, education and prior governmental experience at the top of every statement, in ballot order, each ending "(This information furnished by [name].)". It is the closest thing to a neutral template for a candidate statement. https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/Documents/VPManual.pdf

## Part D. Mistakes to avoid

**Building a match, however soft (JUDGED from ISideWith, Vote Compass, VoteEasy, BallotReady endorsement filters).** Every fairness complaint about these tools attacks the placement or weighting step: neutral answers "make you Liberal" (Maclean's 2011), one-sided question blurbs (Bipartisan Press 2020), score drift between years (Cinq-Mars 2019). None of those complaints can be made against a site that only quotes.

**Framing non-response as a moral failing.** Vote Smart's "courage" language coincided with a fall from 72% to 20% participation and the parties telling candidates "It sets them up for a hit piece". Ask for what is already public, and label absence neutrally.

**Fixed yes/no issue lists.** They are what attack ads are made from (NPR 2008), and they push candidates to say nothing. WITM's method (quote the manifesto, leaflet or Hansard) does not create new hostages.

**Editorial one-word verdicts.** Guides.vote's "Mixed" / "Mostly yes" labels are a characterisation by the site. Do not add them, even as a summary aid.

**Undisclosed funding while claiming neutrality.** Ballotpedia's content passes most bias reviews; its donor list does not exist in public, and that is the story critics tell. WITM should keep its funding page current to the pound.

**Magic links that can be forwarded.** LWV Amarillo lost candidate answers because candidates forwarded personalised links and overwrote each other. Bind links to a confirmed email or code.

**A "print your picks" feature.** VOTE411's selection-and-print is popular but is a step toward the site holding a voting plan. WITM's /share and /quick pages should stay descriptive.

**Depending on a partner's platform.** Voter's Edge, Sunlight's tools and the OpenSecrets API all ended when the host's priorities changed. Keep the data model simple, exportable, and documented so someone else could run it.

**A private corrections log.** Ballotpedia's is internal. WITM's ledger is public; keep it that way and make it searchable per candidate.

## Part E. Candidate participation: what the data says and what a small UK site can copy

**What the response-rate data says (OBSERVED).**
- A large, national, free, identity-verified, thirty-minute survey with no personal follow-up gets about one candidate in five: Ballotpedia 6.9% (2018), 19.3% of contested-race candidates (2020), 19.1% (2022), 19.2% (2024). Ultra-local candidates without a pre-existing profile responded at about 1.6% in the 2024 pilot (1,127 of 68,921).
- A short questionnaire with a personal link, sent by a known local civic body and chased by post, email, text and phone gets about three in five: 66% (North Carolina, 2022 primary), 61% (Nebraska, 2024 primary).
- A long fixed-position test that labels refusers publicly and that parties fear as ad fodder falls over time: 72% → 48% → 38% → 20% (Vote Smart, 1996–2016).
- Response correlates with winning: 64% of North Carolina primary winners had responded; Ballotpedia respondents won at 15.7% (2020), 24.4% (2018), 18.1% (2022) and 1,068 of 6,541 in 2024 (16.3%; Ballotpedia's own news piece says "20%" of tracked respondents) — a mix of contested and uncontested races, so not comparable to the NC figure.

**The rules the mature guides use (OBSERVED, by rule).**
- *Same questions for every candidate in a race*: LWV (New Mexico policy; Utah instructions); Ballotpedia (standard question set with optional extras); Oregon (four required fields for everyone).
- *Hard length limit*: VOTE411 750 characters (Utah); Ballotpedia 200 words for "Who are you?"; California 250 words (statewide, House, Senate, Assembly), 200 for local nonpartisan (400 if allowed); Oregon 325 words.
- *Verbatim, no editing for meaning*: LWV New Mexico ("do not edit for meaning, grammar, punctuation, spelling, or accuracy"); Houston ("published exactly as submitted"); Ballotpedia (typos only on request; editor changes in [brackets]).
- *No attacks / no reference to opponents*: California by statute (§85601, §13307.5, §13308: "shall not in any way make reference to other candidates"); LWV by reserved right to withhold "egregious personal attacks", obscenity, slurs or apparent defamation, with a chance to reword; Oregon by review for "obscene, profane or defamatory language" and incitement; Ballotpedia by removing sexual content, calls to violence and targeted hate speech only.
- *Deadlines*: LWV New Mexico "at least two weeks" after mailing; VOTE411 accepts late answers online; California 88 days before the election for local statements, 15 July 2026 for statewide; Oregon fixed filing deadline with a later window only for photos and endorsement forms.
- *Non-response label*: "The candidate has not yet responded" (VOTE411), "No reply received" (New Mexico), "No response received" (Houston), placeholder photo (VOTE411); icon-for-responders-only (Ballotpedia); nothing printed (official pamphlets).
- *Verification*: identity via photo ID (Ballotpedia); email link pre-populated with name and party, not editable by the candidate (VOTE411); candidate signature/attestation with felony exposure for the required fields (Oregon); no fact-check of free text anywhere.
- *Disputes*: third parties must supply "a credible source" (Ballotpedia); candidates cannot replace an answer but can add a dated note (Ballotpedia); candidates can revise at any time (VOTE411).
- *Endorsements*: unverified if candidate-reported (Ballotpedia); require a signed endorsement form or a dated public quote (Oregon).

**What a small, unfunded UK site can realistically copy (JUDGED).**
1. One short form, identical for every candidate in a ballot, with a character limit of about 750 and a plain instruction that it will be shown word for word, unedited, next to the same form from every other candidate. That is VOTE411's format and it is cheap.
2. Personal, non-forwardable links tied to the candidate's published contact (Democracy Club's email or the agent's address), plus one paper letter to the address on the nomination paper. Nebraska's postcard-then-chase routine is the single best-evidenced way to raise response; two students can do it for 15 candidates in one constituency and for a few hundred in May 2027 if council candidates are batched by ward.
3. A neutral label, "Has not yet published a statement here", with the date last asked, and a public count on the ballot page ("6 of 15 candidates have responded"). Also show, per candidate, what WITM *has* found elsewhere (leaflets, Hansard, party manifesto) so an empty form is not an empty page.
4. Oregon's four required fields (occupation, occupational background, education, prior public office) as the first lines of every statement, labelled as supplied by the candidate.
5. Ballotpedia's dispute rules verbatim: no removal, no overwriting; a dated note above the original; site edits in [brackets]; third-party challenges need a source; and a written promise to correct factual errors in the site's own text within 24 hours, logged in /ledger.
6. California's content rule as the only content rule: the statement is about the candidate's own background and intentions and may not refer to other candidates. It is simple, it is law in the largest US state, and it removes the need to judge what counts as an attack.
7. A "help us ask" page like VOTE411's, giving voters a one-line message to send their candidate.

What the site should *not* copy: identity verification by photo ID (too heavy, and a data-protection burden for two people); a thirty-minute survey; any question that asks the candidate to take a new position rather than to describe the ones already published.

## Part F. How official state pamphlets handle fairness — the nearest thing to a legal standard

**Equal format, not equal access (OBSERVED).** Both California and Oregon guarantee uniformity of presentation — California: "type of uniform size and darkness, and with uniform spacing" (§13307(b)); Oregon: statements "appear in ballot order", one column each, with party order in primaries rotating every two years, and even the photograph is standardised (front-facing, head and shoulders, no props, under four years old). Neither guarantees that every candidate appears: in California a statewide candidate must both pay and accept voluntary spending limits (Gov. Code §85601), local candidates may be charged their "pro rata share" of printing, and in Oregon the fee runs from $600 to $3,500 unless the candidate gathers 200–500 signatures instead. **JUDGED:** the official pamphlets are fair in format and unfair in access; WITM, which shows every candidate free of charge, is already ahead on the second point and should copy the first — identical layout, identical field order, identical length cap.

**Content rules are narrow and mechanical (OBSERVED).** California forbids party labels in local statements, forbids any reference to opponents in statewide, House, Senate and Assembly statements (§85601, §13307.5) and confines local statements to the candidate's "own personal background and qualifications" (§13308), with the elections official instructed not to print a non-compliant statement. Oregon checks only that the four required fields are present and true, that the photo complies, and that the text is not obscene, profane, defamatory, inciting or unmailable; endorsements need a signed form or a dated public quote. Nobody fact-checks the free text.

**Disclaimers put responsibility on the author (OBSERVED).** California prints with every argument: "Arguments printed on this page are the opinions of the authors and have not been checked for accuracy by any official agency" and keeps authors liable for "false, slanderous, or libelous statements" (§13307(e)). Oregon prints "(This information furnished by [name].)" at the foot of each statement and attaches a felony to false answers in the required fields (ORS 260.715(1)) and a civil remedy for other false statements (ORS 260.532). The Oregon page-level "has not been verified for accuracy by the State of Oregon" line is remembered but unverified today.

**Money is disclosed alongside speech (OBSERVED).** California's guide links each measure to the FPPC's "top 10 contributors" list. Candidate pages do not carry the equivalent; Voter's Edge added it and was the better for it.

**JUDGED — what to adopt as WITM's "pamphlet standard".** (a) Same fields, same order, same cap, same layout for every candidate, in ballot-paper order. (b) A one-line attribution under every candidate-supplied block: "Supplied by the candidate on [date]. Not checked for accuracy by What's It To Me?" (c) A one-line attribution under every quoted position: "Quoted from [source], [date]." (d) Content rule: about yourself, not about others. (e) No fee, no signatures, no polling threshold — every nominated candidate gets the page. (f) Funding data on the same page as the statement, with the OpenSecrets-style caveat about what the money is and is not.
