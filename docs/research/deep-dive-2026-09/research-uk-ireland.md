# UK and Irish equivalents of What's It To Me? — what they do well and badly

Research date: 25 September 2026. All URLs accessed on that date unless stated. Every figure below was read from the cited page; where a page could not be fetched or a figure could not be checked, it is marked **unverified**.

Method: about 85 page fetches and 40 web searches across Democracy Club, mySociety, Full Fact, IFS, Vote for Policies, Who Should You Vote For, iSideWith, Electoral Calculus, the Electoral Commissions of the UK and Ireland, EONI, WhichCandidate.ie, Smartvote.ie, Open Council Data, LGiU, ALDC, local-elections.uk, electionhustings.uk, ElectionLeaflets.org, Open Elections, PolicyEngine and academic sources. The BBC website was blocked from this environment, so BBC tools are described only through Democracy Club's published partner data. The search budget ran out before the Guardian, FT and Telegraph 2024 calculators could be located; those are marked unverified.

Labels: **OBSERVED** = read on the cited page. **JUDGE** = my opinion.

---

## 1. Democracy Club — WhoCanIVoteFor (WCIVF), WhereDoIVote, candidates database, ElectionLeaflets

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** Democracy Club is a Community Interest Company, no. 09461226, founded 2010, with a small paid team and "hundreds of online volunteers". It has been "a data supplier to the Electoral Commission since 2017" and says it has "delivered personalised election information to over 10 million people since 2018". (Democracy Club, "About us", https://democracyclub.org.uk/about/.) The Electoral Commission's election-information API is "run by Democracy Club on behalf of the Electoral Commission" (Electoral Commission, "Documentation | Election information API", https://api.electoralcommission.org.uk/docs/). The ElectionLeaflets relaunch was funded by the Joseph Rowntree Reform Trust (Democracy Club, "The return of ElectionLeaflets.org", 3 Mar 2025, https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2025/03/03/the-return-of-electionleafletsorg/). Its dedicated funding page returned 404, so a full funder list is **unverified**.

**What a voter sees (OBSERVED).** For a postcode, WCIVF lists each election, the candidates in a plain vertical list (name, party, photo), the voting instruction ("Mark an X in the box for your preferred candidate. You can mark up to one candidate") and a link to the Electoral Commission. The by-election page for Holborn and St Pancras carries no statement about sourcing or impartiality (https://whocanivotefor.co.uk/elections/parl.holborn-and-st-pancras.by.2026-10-08/). Each candidate page shows a statement to voters, social and email links, a table of every election the person has stood in since 2016, links to TheyWorkForYou, Wikipedia and ElectionLeaflets, an "Add or edit details" button and a "favourite biscuit" field. The provenance line on every statement reads: "This statement was added by [name], their team, or by a Democracy Club volunteer, based on information published by the candidate elsewhere", with a last-updated date (e.g. https://whocanivotefor.co.uk/person/84249/sagal-abdi-wali, updated 10 Sep 2026; https://whocanivotefor.co.uk/person/7505/zack-polanski).

**Sourcing and attribution (OBSERVED).** "Nomination data is verified by at least two people and sources are required for all edits" (Democracy Club, "Data and APIs", https://democracyclub.org.uk/projects/data/). Candidates are invited to "create an account" and add "photos, statements to voters and social media links" (WCIVF, "Running in a UK election?", https://whocanivotefor.co.uk/standing/). The candidate-site help and volunteer pages are blocked to crawlers, so the written moderation rules for statements are **unverified**. Democracy Club itself admitted in 2019: "we never really know how people make decisions and what information might help" and asked "how do we decide what goes on Who? Who should decide?" (Democracy Club, "What Goes On Who?", 19 Sep 2019, https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2019/09/19/what-goes-who/).

**Ranking or matching (OBSERVED).** None. Candidates are listed; nothing is scored.

**Candidate participation — the "statement to voters" take-up (OBSERVED).**
- General election 2024: 95% of candidates had photos, 82.4% email, 88.5% a weblink, **53.6% a statement to voters**, 15.6% Wikipedia; 7,250 new users registered to edit. (Democracy Club, "2024 local and general elections", https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2024/.)
- Local elections 2025 (8,600+ candidates): photos 49%, links 41%, email 26%, **statements 25%**. (Democracy Club, "2025 local elections", https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2025/.)
- May 2021: the report gives "18%" of candidates with a statement overall but also quotes a range from 97.73% (mayoral) to 34.27% (local council), which cannot both be right; treat the 18% as **unverified**. Candidate self-edits rose from 578 (Jan–May 2019) to 1,378 (Jan–May 2021). (Democracy Club, "Towards better elections — 2021", https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2021/.)
- 2023: "increasing difficulties with our archive of candidate contact details and especially election statements", because there is no mechanism to spot when a stored statement is out of date. (Democracy Club, "Local elections 4 and 18 May 2023", https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2023/.)
- 2026: 25,000 candidates in English locals, 4.9 per seat; the team "struggled to collect details from party websites, moderate the thousands of photos submitted, and evaluate requests to merge candidate profiles"; 24 people made 500+ edits each. (Democracy Club, "2026 elections report", https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2026/.) 148 local manifestos were collected by 29 April 2026, only 2 in Easy Read. (Democracy Club, "Candidates and manifestos in the 2026 local and devolved elections", 30 Apr 2026, https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2026/04/30/candidates-and-manifestos-in-the-2026-local-and-devolved-elections/.)

**Impartiality complaints (OBSERVED).** The 2024 feedback review reports no bias complaints; written feedback was about which parties were standing, hustings (105 requests), candidates' professional backgrounds and past results. (Democracy Club, "2024 general election feedback", 7 Aug 2024, https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2024/08/07/2024-general-election-feedback/.) ElectionLeaflets publishes a takedown rule: Democracy Club "reserves the right to remove any images" that are malicious or illegal, with complaints to hello@democracyclub.org.uk (https://electionleaflets.org/about/).

**Licences and APIs (OBSERVED).** Candidate data is CC BY-SA: "you are free to reuse the data for any reason, as long as you give attribution to 'Democracy Club' and any reuse is shared under the same licence"; party logos and candidate photos are excluded (Democracy Club candidates API help, https://candidates.democracyclub.org.uk/help/api). The Electoral Commission API returns only candidate names and parties (https://api.electoralcommission.org.uk/docs/).

**Usage (OBSERVED).** 20 million postcode searches 1 Apr–4 Jul 2024; 14,467,228 in the general-election period; 5,515,210 in election week, of which WhereDoIVote 22.3%, Electoral Commission 20.4%, WCIVF 17.9%, BBC News 12.1%, other API users 27.1%, widgets 2.2% (https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2024/; https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2024/07/04/2024-general-election-postcode-search-summary/). 2026: 10,564,221 searches since 1 April; election week 4,635,003; WCIVF 25.8%, WDIV 22.8%, Electoral Commission 21.0%, BBC 11.4%; Northern Ireland only 0.2% of searches (https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2026/05/08/2026-postcode-search-summary/). Satisfaction: WCIVF 86% in the 2024 election week, 74% in May 2025, 76.63% in May 2026 (England 75.74%, Scotland 83.79%); 14,241 feedback responses in 2026; 30% of WCIVF users said it made them more likely to vote (https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2026/06/03/2026-elections-user-feedback/; https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2026/).

**What users praise and complain about (OBSERVED).** Praise: "Helpful to have all my local candidate information in one place." Complaints: "There's almost no information about individual candidates"; "If candidates can't be bothered to advertise themselves then I can't be bothered to vote." Lack of polling-station maps drew repeat criticism in 2025. (Same feedback posts.)

**Design worth borrowing (OBSERVED).** The one-line provenance sentence under every statement (person page URLs above). The "every election this person has stood in" table. The pairing of a candidate with their leaflets on ElectionLeaflets. The Electoral Commission API's honesty that it is an "Alpha service".

**JUDGE.** Democracy Club has solved distribution (half its traffic arrives through the Electoral Commission, BBC and other partners) but not depth. Its own feedback shows the dominant complaint is empty candidate pages. Statement coverage collapses from about half at a general election to a quarter at local elections. The 2023 admission that stored statements go stale with no way to detect it is exactly the problem a dated, linked ledger addresses.

## 2. mySociety — TheyWorkForYou (TWFY) and TWFY Votes

**Who runs it and funding (OBSERVED).** mySociety, a registered UK charity founded 2004. TWFY started in 2004 as a volunteer project using Public Whip software and was adopted by mySociety in 2006; the Department for Constitutional Affairs funded its API in 2006 (Wikipedia, "TheyWorkForYou", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TheyWorkForYou). mySociety says "one in three UK adults has heard of TheyWorkForYou and one in five has used the site" (mySociety, "Democracy", https://www.mysociety.org/democracy/). 2.2 million email alerts were sent in 2026 to date; the September 2026 post is a donation appeal (mySociety, "They work for you (and we do too)", 9 Sep 2026, https://www.mysociety.org/2026/09/09/they-work-for-you-and-we-do-too/).

**What a voter sees (OBSERVED).** An MP page has: overview, speeches and questions, committees/APPGs, signatures, voting summary, recent votes, register of interests, 2024 election donations and constituency information. The voting page words each line as a pattern plus a party comparison, e.g. "Consistently voted for more rights for tenants and regulation on landlords" with "Comparable Liberal Democrat MPs consistently voted for", and "[1 vote for, in 2025 …]". It carries a Covid absence caveat and the line "Votes are not opinions but they matter". (TWFY MP votes page reached via https://www.theyworkforyou.com/mp/26325/keir_starmer/holborn_and_st_pancras/votes — note the fetch resolved to the page of Liz Jarvis MP, so the ID in that URL appears not to be Keir Starmer's.)

**How summaries are computed and caveated (OBSERVED).** Each policy has a 0–100 score turned into a phrase ("0 to 5 – consistently voted for" … "85 to 95 – almost always voted against"). Votes are "scoring" or "informative". Because whips are not published, "we use the average vote of a party's MPs to work out what the instructions probably were". Absences are not counted because their meaning is ambiguous. Inclusion criteria: uses Parliament's powers, cohesion, uniqueness, noteworthiness — "In practice, these principles have to be flexible to the reality of the parliamentary process." (TWFY, "Voting and decisions on TheyWorkForYou", https://www.theyworkforyou.com/voting-information/.) Since 2025 summaries are calculated for the current Parliament and "all time" separately, because mixing old and recent votes made long-serving MPs' "positions in the present moment harder to understand" (mySociety, "Updating TheyWorkForYou's voting summaries", 13 Mar 2025, https://www.mysociety.org/2025/03/13/updating-theyworkforyous-voting-summaries/). TWFY Votes adds MP self-explanations ("more MPs are now publicly explaining how they voted. We're beginning to log these statements"), a "Report a problem" link on every decision page, and "agreements" passed without a division (TWFY Votes, "Help & Overview", https://votes.theyworkforyou.com/help/about; mySociety, "Introducing TheyWorkForYou Votes", 19 May 2025, https://www.mysociety.org/2025/05/19/theyworkforyou-votes/). Policy additions are published in dated update posts (Jul 2025, Oct 2025, Jul 2026) with reasons for each inclusion, e.g. the Palestine Action proscription vote qualified as "the first vote to be held on proscription of a group under the Terrorism Act" (https://www.mysociety.org/2025/10/22/voting-summaries-update-october-2025/; https://www.mysociety.org/2026/07/01/theyworkforyou-voting-summaries-update-july-2026/).

**Criticisms (OBSERVED).** UCL Constitution Unit: 30 Conservative MPs protested in 2019 that climate summaries misrepresented them; 50 complained in 2021 that data "misrepresented" their stances; Caroline Lucas appeared to oppose fracking regulation because she voted against a bill she thought too weak; Opposition Day votes are shown without context; screenshots shared on social media "miss out vital pieces of context". The authors still defend publication, noting proxy voting for parental leave arose from a TWFY controversy. (Worthy and Morgan, "Should we be allowed to see MPs' voting records?", UCL Constitution Unit blog, 20 Dec 2021, https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/constitution-unit/2021/12/20/should-we-be-allowed-to-see-mps-voting-records/.) In 2006 The Times reported MPs asking pointless written questions to raise their TWFY activity rank; TWFY removed absolute rankings and added explanatory text (Wikipedia, "TheyWorkForYou"). A parallel critique of the Australian sister site They Vote For You: a senator was shown as "consistently voted against" an anti-corruption commission she supported, because votes on amendments were counted as votes against the bill; 31 of 185 recorded votes were silently excluded; party-whipped votes were presented as personal choices. (Pat Leslie, "How They Vote For You can mislead the voting public", https://patleslie.net/blogs/blog_tvfy/tvfy_post.)

**Licence and API (OBSERVED).** TWFY has an API and WriteToThem/MapIt are separate products; MapIt "starts at £23/mth and is free for low-volume non-profit use" (https://mapit.mysociety.org/).

**Design worth borrowing (OBSERVED).** The "comparable [party] MPs" sentence attached to every line; the dated update log of methodology changes; the "Report a problem" link on every decision; logging MPs' own explanations beside the record.

**JUDGE.** TWFY is the clearest UK case of a well-meant summary being weaponised out of context. Twenty years in, it is still adding caveats. The lesson for a candidate site is not to produce any single-sentence summary of a person's position without the underlying quote and date beside it.

## 3. mySociety — WriteToThem and MapIt

**OBSERVED.** WriteToThem lets people contact MPs, councillors and others; it was first funded by the ODPM e-innovations fund and is now supported by "the Welsh Government's Democratic Engagement Grant" (2025–27); the councillor database comes from GovEval (WriteToThem, "About us", https://www.writetothem.com/about-us). Its last published responsiveness league table covers 2015–16: 90,175 messages to MPs, 50,852 survey answers; the measure is "surveys saying 'MP replied' out of total survey answers" (https://www.writetothem.com/stats). The 2016 post reported response rates falling from 63% in 2005 to 50% in 2016 and a data gap 2008–13 (mySociety, "They're back, and they're bad", 13 Jun 2016, https://www.mysociety.org/2016/06/13/theyre-back-and-theyre-bad-writetothem-responsiveness-stats/). No later table was found; why it stopped is **unverified**.

**JUDGE.** The responsiveness table is a ranking by proxy and appears to have been quietly abandoned. WriteToThem's real value is the one-click route from "who represents me" to "write to them", which What's It To Me? does not offer.

## 4. Vote for Policies (closed)

**Who ran it and funding (OBSERVED).** "An independent not-for-profit organisation registered in the United Kingdom (no. 8198015)", led by Matt Chocqueel-Mangan and Leon Prescod; "a few small grants in the past, and currently any income comes from small donations" (Vote for Policies, "About", https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/about/). A 2014 crowdfunder raised £23,551 from 882 supporters (Crowdfunder, https://www.crowdfunder.co.uk/p/voteforpolicies).

**How it worked (OBSERVED).** Policies taken "direct from the manifestos", shown "without showing which party they belong to", in random order; "no algorithmic matching" — results were "purely on your preference of the policies presented to you". Bias control: "We send our policy summaries to the political parties for verification in advance of publishing, and discuss any changes." Known limits: devolved issues gave a "sub-optimal comparison"; Northern Ireland was excluded because NI parties' manifestos did not fit the categories. (Vote for Policies, "Survey FAQ", https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/faq/.)

**Usage (OBSERVED).** "Nearly 4 million" uses over five general elections; 1.1 million in 2019. A TNS poll found users "more likely to vote" (no percentage shown). (About page; news archive https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/blog/category/news/.)

**What happened (OBSERVED).** Closed May 2025: "we couldn't match – let alone double (as was the target) – the 1 million users who used the site in 2019"; "we still don't have the technology needed to automate policy tracking so it can be run sustainably"; "Current funding of non-partisan services is a fraction of what's needed"; funders see democracy tools as too risky or not partisan enough, and funding "often focuses on short-term, transactional outcomes". (Vote for Policies, "Farewell to Vote for Policies – and thank you", 12 May 2025, https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/blog/farewell-to-vote-for-policies/.) In 2024 it had "only six weeks to launch", built on the Embeddables platform pro bono, and concluded "A lack of non-partisan voter information isn't the only reason for voter apathy" (Vote for Policies, "2024 General Election roundup", 10 Jul 2024, https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/blog/2024-general-election-roundup/).

**JUDGE.** The party-verification step is the best impartiality mechanism in this whole survey and is cheap to copy. The failure was economic: hand-summarising manifestos every election without automation, and a funding model that needed a big audience every five years to justify itself.

## 5. Who Should You Vote For? (whoshouldyouvotefor.com)

**OBSERVED.** Run since 2005 by "Paul and Andrew" of Thoughtplay as volunteers; "more than five million people have taken it"; "with the hosting charges this costs us money to do". The 2024 quiz has 22 statements on a five-point scale, plus leadership and demographic questions. Positions are set by the authors, who choose issues "that can be reasonably easily understood in a single sentence, and where there is appreciable difference between the parties". On bias: "if a person answers 'agree' to every question and one party is more strongly recommended than the others then we get accused of bias", so questions are inverted; and to "Can you rig the results?": "You don't, but we aren't." No candidates or constituencies. (https://www.whoshouldyouvotefor.com/; https://whoshouldyouvotefor.com/faq.php; https://whoshouldyouvotefor.com/q/vl.)

**JUDGE.** Honest hobby project. Its impartiality rests entirely on two unnamed authors' judgement of what each party thinks; nothing is cited.

## 6. iSideWith UK

**OBSERVED.** Founded March 2012 by Taylor Peck and Nick Boutelier in the US; "not affiliated with any investors, shareholders, political party or interest group"; runs on donations (iSideWith, "About", https://www.isidewith.com/about/). The UK 2026 quiz groups questions by theme with "Learn more", "Stats" and "Discuss" links and importance sliders (https://uk.isidewith.com/political-quiz). The 2024 constituency guide says "Take the political quiz to see which candidates match your political beliefs" and links a methodology page (https://uk.isidewith.com/elections/2024/constituencies/members-of-parliament). The methodology page and the UK about page returned redirect loops, so how candidate positions are assigned is **unverified**.

**JUDGE.** It ranks candidates against a voter but does not, on the pages reached, show where each candidate's position came from. That is the opposite of what the CONTEXT rules require.

## 7. Electoral Calculus

**OBSERVED.** Founded by Martin Baxter; "a member of the British Polling Council and abides by its rules"; a Market Research Society company partner; sells "result prediction, estimation of likely results under client-chosen scenarios" and market research (Electoral Calculus, "About Us", https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/aboutus.html). MRP since 2019; correctly called the largest party in 11 of 12 elections since 1992 (Wikipedia, "Electoral Calculus", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_Calculus). The July 2026 MRP (5,545 adults, 23–30 June 2026, fieldwork by Find Out Now) publishes per-seat predictions, a map, a list of ministers at risk and a section modelling anti-Reform tactical voting (https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20260708.html).

**JUDGE.** Useful for "who might win here" but it is a prediction and tactical-voting engine, which a strictly neutral site should link to, not copy.

## 8. Full Fact — election fact checks and Government Tracker

**Funding and impartiality (OBSERVED).** Registered charity; 2025 income £3,054,478; largest funders Meta £342,712, European Fact Checking Standards Network £259,726, Mohn Westlake Foundation £250,000, Peter Samuel Charitable Trust £210,000, Google.org £205,978, LSE Enterprise £189,854 (Journalism AI), Google Ireland £154,131, Health Foundation £131,821; individual donors £426,692; "Our funders have no input into our editorial content"; no political-party money; cross-party board (Full Fact, "Funding", https://fullfact.org/about/funding/). Safeguards: cross-party board, conflict-of-interest policy, limits on staff political activity, external reviews; "We provide links to all sources so that you can check what we've said for yourself"; "Ultimately, it is for our audience to judge for themselves whether we succeed" (Full Fact, "Impartiality", https://fullfact.org/about/impartiality/).

**Corrections log (OBSERVED).** A public page: "This is a record of corrections we've made in the past 3 years", listing date, linked article and the error, e.g. Sept 2026: "An earlier version of this fact check incorrectly suggested that the plane was not visible due to the angle of the footage." (Full Fact, "Corrections", https://fullfact.org/about/corrections/.) This is the only UK corrections ledger found that resembles What's It To Me?'s /ledger, though it is edited rather than append-only.

**Election 2024 (OBSERVED).** 217 verdicts, 35 fact checks, 78 live-blog updates, 3,754 media mentions, 20 daily PA round-ups to about 170 outlets, 14 interventions with parties; the Green Party corrected its manifesto after Full Fact contacted it (Full Fact, "General election 2024, fact checked", https://fullfact.org/blog/2024/jul/general-election-2024-fact-checked/). Manifesto checks quote the claim, weave the verdict into prose, link primary sources and say "We've asked Reform UK about this and will update this article if we hear back"; "Full Fact's AI tools helped identify this claim" (https://fullfact.org/election-2024/reformuk-manifesto/).

**Government Tracker (OBSERVED).** 94 pledges from Labour's 2024 manifesto and later announcements; seven statuses (Achieved, Appears on track, In progress, Appears off track, Not kept, Unclear or disputed, Wait and see) with icons; filters by policy area and status; each row dated (https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/). Ratings need "discussion with at least two other senior members of our fact checking team" (FAQ, https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/frequently-asked-questions/). A pledge page quotes the pledge with page number ("page 36 of the Labour manifesto"), gives the status reasoning with dated statistics, shows published and last-updated dates (published 18 Nov 2024, updated 14 Sep 2026) and ends "Did you spot something that needs updating? Contact us." (https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/1-5-million-homes/). Full Fact's own limits: only 80 of about 300 pledges tracked at Sept 2025; the first set skewed to Labour's own priorities; "Pledge tracking is an art, not a science"; "for too many pledges there's simply insufficient information available" (https://fullfact.org/politics/tracker-blog-september-2025/). AI: Gemini drafts questions, ChatGPT monitors sources, about 80% accuracy, every output human-reviewed; funded via the Google News Initiative's JournalismAI challenge (https://www.journalismai.info/programmes/innovation/innovation-challenge-2024/full-fact).

**JUDGE.** Full Fact is the model for governance: named funders with amounts, a written impartiality method, a public corrections record and "we asked X, will update" wording. Its tracker shows the cost of verdict labels — even a seven-way scale still needs an "unclear or disputed" bucket and a blog admitting selection bias.

## 9. Institute for Fiscal Studies

**OBSERVED.** The 2024 calculator "What are the parties' plans for benefits and taxes?" shows effects by income decile, family type, age, region and work status, in cash (2024 prices) or as a share of income, over five years; it models Conservative, Labour and Lib Dem packages; it states what is included ("three more years of tax threshold freezes") and what is not ("only analyses the distributional effects of changes to taxes and benefits on household income"; no take-up, no behavioural response; fuel duty not modelled); funded by UKRI ESRC and the abrdn Financial Fairness Trust (https://ifs.org.uk/calculators/what-are-parties-plans-benefits-and-taxes). The election microsite lists "Be the Chancellor" and names funders abrdn Financial Fairness Trust and Nuffield Foundation (https://ifs.org.uk/microsite/election-2024). At the manifesto launch Paul Johnson called NHS promises "essentially unfunded commitments" and described a "conspiracy of silence" (https://ifs.org.uk/events/general-election-2024-ifs-manifesto-analysis).

**JUDGE.** The IFS calculator is the closest UK precedent to the PolicyEngine household grid. Its assumptions box is the template: say what is in, what is out, the price year, and the funder.

## 10. Other "what it means for your money" tools

**OBSERVED.** UKTaxCalculators.co.uk (Ray Arman) took gross income and self-employment status and compared six parties on "headline taxation figures in each party's manifesto"; unmentioned policies "retain the current taxation policies"; liability disclaimer (https://www.uktaxcalculators.co.uk/election/). Hargreaves Lansdown's "Labour manifesto 2024 – what it means for your money" is a generic seven-section explainer with no citations and product links (https://www.hl.co.uk/news/labour-manifesto-2024-what-it-means-for-your-money). Policy in Practice compared manifestos qualitatively; it "did not model household impacts" (https://policyinpractice.co.uk/blog/what-do-the-general-election-2024-manifestos-tell-us-about-work-and-welfare/). TaxWatch analysed administration and compliance with no household examples (https://www.taxwatchuk.org/2024-election-manifestos-analysed/). PolicyEngine UK is "Free, open-source tools to understand tax and benefit policies", with a household calculator, API and Python package, and supporters ranging from NIESR to the Green Party of England and Wales and the Adam Smith Institute (https://policyengine.org/uk). Guardian, FT, Telegraph and BBC manifesto calculators for 2024: **unverified** (not located before the search budget ran out; BBC site blocked).

**JUDGE.** No UK site found combines a per-candidate ballot with household modelling. Commercial "what it means for your money" pages are unsourced and sell products.

## 11. Electoral Commission (UK) voter pages

**OBSERVED.** "Enter your postcode to find out who the candidates in your area are"; the tool also gives elections, polling station, photo-ID rules and the local electoral services contact; England, NI, Scotland, Wales and overseas versions; no Democracy Club credit visible on the page and no explicit impartiality statement (https://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/i-am-a/voter/your-election-information; https://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/i-am-a/voter). The Commission is the single largest referrer of Democracy Club postcode searches after Democracy Club's own sites (20.4% in July 2024; 24.6% in May 2025; 21.0% in May 2026 — sources in section 1).

**JUDGE.** The regulator provides names and parties only. Everything beyond that is left to others.

## 12. BBC postcode pages

**OBSERVED (via Democracy Club only).** The BBC embedded Democracy Club's lookup in election stories from 2023: "over 400,000 postcode searches during the two election weeks of 2023"; a BBC staffer called it "a good two/three-way relationship with Democracy Club and the Electoral Commission" (Democracy Club, "Case Study: the BBC", 6 Mar 2024, https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2024/03/06/case-study-the-bbc/). BBC News accounted for 425,498 searches in May 2023, 12.1% of election-week searches in July 2024, 26.7% in May 2025 and 11.4% in May 2026 (sections 1 and 11). The BBC's own pages were blocked to this environment; any "what the manifestos mean for you" calculator is **unverified**.

**JUDGE.** The BBC does not build candidate data; it borrows it. Its lesson is placement: put the postcode box inside the story people are already reading.

## 13. UK Parliament "Find your MP", Politics.co.uk, Open Council Data, LGiU, MyCouncillor, local-elections.uk, electionhustings.uk, Open Elections

**OBSERVED.**
- Parliament's Find Your MP accepts a postcode, place, name or job title and returns contact details; the Members API has an OpenAPI spec; licence terms were not on the page fetched (https://members.parliament.uk/FindYourMP; https://members-api.parliament.uk/index.html).

**JUDGE.** There is no UK Ballotpedia. The nearest things are Democracy Club (names, statements) and Wikipedia. Local-elections.uk has the right slogan but is anonymous, which a voter cannot trust.

## 14. Ireland — WhichCandidate.ie, Smartvote.ie, RTÉ, An Coimisiún Toghcháin

**WhichCandidate.ie (OBSERVED).** Run by researchers in the Department of Politics and Public Administration, University of Limerick (Dr Rory Costello), "supported by funding from the Irish Research Council", also the Department of Foreign Affairs' Communicating Europe Initiative and UL Engage; covers Dáil, mayoral and European elections but not local. Method: researchers pick issues, candidates fill in the questionnaire, voters answer the same questions; score 1 for identical, 0.5 for adjacent, 0 for opposite, averaged to a percentage; users pick their important issues first; "no opinion" drops a question. Non-respondents: "Some candidates have declined to share their policy views with us and are not featured on the website"; voters are told to contact them directly. Issues on which all candidates agree are excluded. Users can build a "mock ballot paper". About 200,000 unique visitors in the 2020 general election. A UL study of 48,000+ users at the 2024 European elections found 36% changed their initial preference after using it, most where their favourite was outside the tool's top five. (https://whichcandidate.ie/; https://whichcandidate.ie/faq; Irish Times, 19 Nov 2024, https://www.irishtimes.com/politics/2024/11/19/election-2024-how-do-your-views-match-with-election-candidates-in-your-area-use-this-tool-to-find-out/; Irish Examiner, https://www.irishexaminer.com/news/politics/arid-41725374.html; University of Limerick, https://www.ul.ie/news/policy-over-posters-ul-lecturers-election-website-helps-voters-make-informed-decision; Limerick Voice, http://limerickvoice.com/education/research-highlights-how-online-election-tools-influence-voter-choices/.) Candidate response rates are not published — **unverified**.

**Smartvote.ie (OBSERVED).** Founded by Keith Moore and Gordon Rose out of UCD's Innovation Academy; piloted at UCD student elections (March 2015) and the Carlow–Kilkenny by-election (12 of 13 candidates took part); 30 issues; in 2016 "every party has answered the survey, and about 60% of independent candidates"; won a Social Entrepreneurs Ireland award (https://irishtechnews.ie/is-smartvote-ie-the-new-tinder-of-politics/; https://www.thejournal.ie/smartvote-election-2545584-Jan2016/). The Swiss original has candidates answer 30–75 questions and matches by Euclidean distance (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartvote). RTÉ involvement in Smartvote.ie: **unverified** — RTÉ's 2024 guide offers a constituency map and candidate lists and links the Commission's "Find My Constituency", but no matching tool (https://www.rte.ie/news/election-24/2024/1116/1479187-simple-guide-general-election-2024/).

**An Coimisiún Toghcháin (OBSERVED).** Provides checktheregister.ie, a constituency finder, ballot-marking guides and accessible templates; no candidate information (https://www.electoralcommission.ie/general-elections/). Its June 2024 framework on online electoral information, political advertising and deceptive AI is voluntary and aimed at platforms, parties and candidates (https://www.electoralcommission.ie/media-release/an-coimisiun-toghchain-publishes-framework-on-online-electoral-process-information-political-advertising-and-deceptive-ai-content-in-advance-of-june-elections/).

**JUDGE.** Ireland's tools are candidate-level because STV forces it. WhichCandidate's honesty about non-respondents and its mock-ballot feature are worth copying; its ranking is not.

## 15. Northern Ireland

**OBSERVED.** EONI offers a polling station finder, "Find my Elected Representatives", results and voting guidance, but no candidate lists on the page fetched (https://www.eoni.org.uk/elections/). Democracy Club has partnered with EONI on a polling-station finder and published a map of proposed polling places for the 6 May 2027 combined council and Assembly elections, "the first time an entire UK region's polling place scheme has been publicly mapped out in this way" (https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2026/07/31/mapping-northern-irelands-proposed-polling-places/). WCIVF covered "all except one candidate (99.5%)" at the 2022 Assembly election with 91% satisfaction among NI users and 51,232 NI searches (https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2022/). No NI-specific candidate-information tool was found.

**JUDGE.** NI is an open gap. The 2027 Assembly and council elections are a natural target, but STV and NI's party system mean the PolicyEngine grid needs NI-specific tax and benefit rules.

## 16. Academic evidence on voting-advice tools

**OBSERVED.** Reviews note that careful "selection of VAA statements and a proper process of benchmarking based on survey data" is essential, that some scholars argue VAAs "inherently cannot deliver neutral guidance", and that measured effects on declared preference range from 3% (Finland) to 10% (Netherlands), with behavioural change of about 1% in Belgium (Wikipedia, "Voting advice application", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_advice_application). A Danish study using a 30-question candidate VAA found elected politicians "stay true to the positions they took in the VAA" but does not report response rates (European Political Science Review, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/candidates-voters-and-voting-advice-applications/B4BBA53D4C023855DCA885A5B6F9E135).

## (2) Fifteen lessons for What's It To Me?

1. **Print a provenance sentence under every quote.** Democracy Club's "This statement was added by X, their team, or by a Democracy Club volunteer, based on information published by the candidate elsewhere" is short and honest (https://whocanivotefor.co.uk/person/84249/sagal-abdi-wali). Ours already names source and date; add who entered it.
2. **Expect a quarter to a half of candidates to give you nothing.** 53.6% statements at GE2024, 25% at 2025 locals (https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2025/). Design the empty state for the majority case at the May 2027 locals.
3. **Empty pages are the top complaint, and they suppress turnout intent.** "If candidates can't be bothered … I can't be bothered to vote" (https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2026/06/03/2026-elections-user-feedback/). Fill absent candidates' pages with what is public: leaflets, party manifesto, past results, council votes.
4. **Send policy summaries to the parties before publishing.** Vote for Policies' verification step (https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/faq/) is the cheapest bias defence found. Offer each candidate a pre-publication check of their quotes with a deadline, and record in the ledger whether they replied.
5. **Say "we asked, and will update".** Full Fact's wording (https://fullfact.org/election-2024/reformuk-manifesto/) turns non-response into a visible fact rather than a gap.
6. **Keep a public corrections record, and keep it dated.** Full Fact's log (https://fullfact.org/about/corrections/) is the only UK precedent; ours is append-only, which is stronger. Show a "last updated" date on every candidate page as Full Fact does on each pledge (https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/1-5-million-homes/).
7. **Stale statements are a known failure.** Democracy Club could not detect out-of-date statements in 2023 (https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2023/). Our refresh jobs should flag any quote whose source URL changes or dies.
8. **Publish the household-model assumptions box the way the IFS does.** What is in, what is out, price year, no take-up or behaviour modelled, who funded it (https://ifs.org.uk/calculators/what-are-parties-plans-benefits-and-taxes).
9. **Show the counterfactual explicitly.** UKTaxCalculators states that unmentioned policies "retain the current taxation policies" (https://www.uktaxcalculators.co.uk/election/). Every grid cell should say what "no change" means.
10. **Put the postcode box where readers already are.** BBC embedding produced 400,000 searches in two weeks in 2023 (https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2024/03/06/case-study-the-bbc/). Our /embed page is the right idea; pitch it to local papers and councils (78 councils used Democracy Club's widget in 2026).
11. **Give each candidate a "how they voted" line only with the votes beside it.** TWFY's format — a phrase, a count, a date and "Comparable [party] MPs …" — is the minimum context (TWFY votes page, section 2). Never a bare phrase.
12. **Log the candidate's own explanation next to the record.** TWFY Votes now records MPs' public explanations of votes (https://votes.theyworkforyou.com/help/about). For councillors standing again, quote their stated reasons from meeting minutes.
13. **Publish a dated method-change log.** mySociety's quarterly "voting summaries update" posts explain each inclusion (https://www.mysociety.org/2025/10/22/voting-summaries-update-october-2025/). Do the same for topic taxonomy and grid changes.
14. **Offer a "mock ballot" print-out.** WhichCandidate's mock ballot paper (https://whichcandidate.ie/) lets voters rehearse without the site choosing for them; it fits a no-ranking rule if the voter fills it in.
15. **List hustings and leaflets.** 105 users asked WCIVF for hustings in 2024; 63% of voters get candidate information from leaflets (https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2024/08/07/2024-general-election-feedback/; https://democracyclub.org.uk/blog/2025/03/03/the-return-of-electionleafletsorg/). electionhustings.uk shows a volunteer can cover a borough (https://www.electionhustings.uk/).

## (3) Mistakes to avoid

- **One-line summaries without context (TheyWorkForYou).** 30 MPs in 2019 and 50 in 2021 complained of misrepresentation; screenshots travel without caveats (https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/constitution-unit/2021/12/20/should-we-be-allowed-to-see-mps-voting-records/). Amendment votes counted as votes against a bill (They Vote For You, https://patleslie.net/blogs/blog_tvfy/tvfy_post).
- **Silent exclusion (They Vote For You).** 31 of 185 votes dropped with no explanation. Our ledger must record what we chose not to include and why.
- **Activity rankings that change behaviour (TheyWorkForYou 2006).** MPs gamed written-question counts; TWFY removed absolute rankings (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TheyWorkForYou). Do not publish "most active candidate" style counts.
- **Hand-built content with no automation (Vote for Policies).** "We still don't have the technology needed to automate policy tracking so it can be run sustainably" (https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/blog/farewell-to-vote-for-policies/). Our GitHub Actions refreshes are the right instinct; keep the manual share small.
- **Depending on a big-bang election for funding (Vote for Policies).** Five-yearly spikes did not sustain it. By-elections and locals are the steady work.
- **Unsourced party positions decided by two people (Who Should You Vote For?, iSideWith).** "You don't, but we aren't" is not a method (https://whoshouldyouvotefor.com/faq.php).
- **Anonymous operation (local-elections.uk).** A voter tool with no named operator or funder cannot be trusted (https://local-elections.uk/about). Our /who-we-are and funding disclosure must stay prominent.
- **Excluding non-respondents entirely (WhichCandidate).** Voters lose the candidates who did not answer (https://whichcandidate.ie/faq). Keep every ballot name on the page, with an honest "no published positions found" state.
- **Excluding Northern Ireland and devolved nuance (Vote for Policies).** Its NI exclusion and "sub-optimal" devolved comparisons (https://voteforpolicies.org.uk/faq/) are a warning for the 2027 NI and Scottish/Welsh elections.
- **Selection bias in what you track (Full Fact Tracker).** The first 43 pledges followed the government's own priorities (https://fullfact.org/politics/tracker-blog-september-2025/). Our topic list should be drawn from an external, stated source such as ONS household spending categories, not from the loudest campaign.
- **Moderation you cannot staff (Democracy Club 2026).** "Thousands of photos" and merge requests overwhelmed the team (https://democracyclub.org.uk/report_2026/). Limit what candidates can submit to text with a source URL.
- **Commercial "what it means for your money" pages (Hargreaves Lansdown).** No citations, product links throughout (https://www.hl.co.uk/news/labour-manifesto-2024-what-it-means-for-your-money). Never mix advice or products into the grid.

## (4) What we do that none of them does, and what they all do that we do not

**Genuine differentiators (OBSERVED against the tools above).**
- Nobody else shows every ballot candidate on an identical page with every position quoted word for word from a named, dated, linked source. Democracy Club shows statements written by candidates; TWFY shows votes for sitting MPs only; the VAAs show positions the candidate or an editor asserted.
- Nobody else links a per-candidate ballot to a household-level tax and benefit model. The IFS calculator works at party level with three parties; PolicyEngine is a general calculator with no ballot.
- Nobody else keeps an append-only public ledger of claims. Full Fact's corrections log is edited and covers only corrections.
- Nobody else combines council meeting agendas, planning applications, school and consultation data with the ballot. LGiU and Open Council Data stop at councillors and compositions.
- Nobody found refuses on principle to rank, score, match or predict. Democracy Club does not rank but does not say why; every quiz tool ranks; Electoral Calculus predicts.

**What they all do that we do not (OBSERVED).**
- Polling station lookup with the Electoral Commission or Democracy Club data. WhereDoIVote's satisfaction (88%) beats WCIVF's every year; voters want the practical answer first.
- A direct "write to this person" route (WriteToThem) and candidate contact details on the page (WCIVF).
- Candidate self-service editing with an audit trail (Democracy Club) — ours is /candidates/submit; it needs the same "added by X on date" trail.
- Published user-satisfaction and traffic figures after every election (Democracy Club, Full Fact). Our /impact page should publish comparable numbers.
- Named funders with amounts (Full Fact). If we have none, say so in the same place.
- A partner widget adopted by councils and newspapers (Democracy Club: 78 councils, The Times, "Future Surrey"). Our /embed exists; take-up is what is missing.
- Leaflet archive links per candidate (WCIVF → ElectionLeaflets). We ingest ElectionLeaflets; check that every candidate page links back to their leaflets.

**JUDGE.** The field splits into three: list-makers (Democracy Club, the Commissions, BBC) that are trusted but shallow; matchers (VAAs) that are engaging but opaque and mostly dead or hobbyist in the UK; and analysts (Full Fact, IFS, TWFY) that are rigorous but party- or MP-level. What's It To Me? sits in the empty space between them. Its risks are the ones that killed Vote for Policies (unsustainable hand curation) and that dog TWFY (context stripped by sharers). The defences are already in the design: automation, quotes with sources, the ledger, and the refusal to rank.
