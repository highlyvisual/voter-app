# Research paper 10: Promises against records — every method

**Date:** 24 September 2026
**For:** Romily Johnson (Founder and Product Lead) and Barny Trevelyan-Johnson (Technical Lead), What's It To Me? (whatsittome.org)
**Status:** research only. This paper informs; it does not pick an option.

**The question in one line:** What are all the ways a site could show a party's or candidate's promise next to what actually happened, what does each one need, and where does each one break?

## Three-line summary

1. Six families of method exist: pledge-to-vote pairing (paper 02), outcome trackers run by people (Full Fact and academics), computed measures of an MP's record (votes, attendance, questions, EDMs, speeches), legislative outcome tracking through Parliament's Bills API, money tracking through HM Treasury and OBR documents, and machine-assisted pairing with a person confirming. All were tested against live sources on 24 September 2026.
2. The only methods that stay pure fact are the Bills API test (a named Bill either has a Royal Assent date or it does not; 18 of 25 Labour 2024 commitments I checked have one, 3 are Bills still going through, 4 have no Bill in the API) and money pledges with a stated number (rate, date, threshold) checked against a Treasury or OBR document. Everything else needs a human judgement at some point, and every published tracker says so in its own words.
3. All these methods work only for the governing party. For opposition parties, independents and new candidates there is no record to show, and the safest published practice (Full Fact, TheyWorkForYou, Public Whip) is to say plainly why the data is absent rather than leave a gap or show a zero.

## Findings

### 1. Pledge-to-division pairing (summary of paper 02; not redone)

Paper 02 took 50 pledges from the 2024 Labour, Conservative, Liberal Democrat, Green and Reform UK manifestos and tried to pair each with a Commons division. Result: 5 clean pairs, 7 ambiguous, 38 never voted on. Every clean pair came from a vote that the pledging party itself set up while in government. Romily's decision on 24 September 2026 was: no pairing for now; promises and votes shown separately.

Looser variants (which paper 02 did not test) would carry a label rather than a match:

- **"Same subject" label** — a division and a pledge share a topic (e.g. both about rented housing) but the division is not the pledge. Needs a person to decide what "same subject" means. Risk: readers will read it as a match anyway.
- **"Party set this vote up" label** — a division on a government Bill that carries a manifesto measure. This is close to method 4 below and is factual, but says nothing about how the individual MP's vote relates to the promise beyond party line.
- **"Voted on an amendment to it" label** — most divisions on a Bill are on amendments, not the Bill. Paper 02's 7 ambiguous cases were mostly this. Any label here is a judgement.

The reason pairing fails is structural, not a data problem: most manifesto promises are delivered by government action, spending or regulation, not by a Commons division, and an opposition party cannot set up a vote on its own pledge. The academic literature reaches the same conclusion from the other side (see 2b).

### 2. Outcome-based pledge trackers run by people

#### 2a. Full Fact's Government Tracker

- **What it is.** Launched November 2024. As read on 24 September 2026, it covers 94 pledges "from Labour's 2024 election manifesto and from further announcements made since", with counts: Achieved 23, Appears on track 18, In progress 22, Appears off track 8, Not kept 6, Unclear or disputed 5, Wait and see 12. Source: [Full Fact, Government Tracker](https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/) (read 24 Sep 2026).
- **How pledges were chosen.** Full Fact's fact checkers "went through it line-by-line to identify everything that appeared to be a measurable pledge, and found nearly 300", then started with Labour's "five national missions" and "six first steps". Source: [Full Fact, Government Tracker FAQs](https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/frequently-asked-questions/).
- **Who decides.** "After checking the evidence, and discussion with at least two other senior members of our fact checking team, we'll apply a 'status' to the pledge." Same source.
- **Definitions, in their own words** (same source):
  - Achieved: "For when a pledge has been met."
  - Appears on track: "work on a pledge is under way and appears likely to be met based on current progress, though this is a judgement call".
  - In progress: "work on a pledge is under way but we're not able to confidently assess whether it appears to be on or off track".
  - Appears off track: "appears unlikely to be met based on current progress, though this is a judgement call".
  - Not kept: "For when a pledge has been broken. This includes pledges which may have been abandoned" and modified pledges, where "we'll rate the old pledge as 'not kept' and start tracking the new one".
  - Unclear or disputed: "we are unable to offer a verdict, usually because either key data is unavailable or the original meaning of the pledge was unclear or disputed".
  - Wait and see: "delivery has not yet started, or it is too early to assess".
- **How often updated.** "Each pledge is clearly marked with the date it was last updated on. That doesn't mean that was the last time we looked at it." Same source. Individual pages read for this paper carry update dates from 12 May to 24 September 2026.
- **Other parties.** "Not currently — this is a Government Tracker ... if we continue to run the Government Tracker and there's a change of government, we would look at another party's record in exactly the same way." Same source.
- **What Full Fact itself says the tracker does not tell us** (26 September 2025 blog): "Pledge tracking is an art, not a science"; the sample "is skewed towards the things Labour was always likely to act on first"; and "for too many pledges there's simply insufficient information available to provide a meaningful rating". Source: [Full Fact, "Broken promises? What Full Fact's Government Tracker does (and doesn't) tell us about Labour's first year", 26 Sep 2025](https://fullfact.org/politics/tracker-blog-september-2025/).
- **Worked examples of judgement** (each from the pledge page, read 24 Sep 2026):
  - "Labour will not increase taxes on working people" — **Not kept**, updated 28 May 2026, on the ground that the 2025 Budget's threshold freeze means "from April 2028 people will pay more in these taxes than they otherwise would have done". Source: [Full Fact, taxes on working people](https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/taxes-on-working-people/). The narrower pledge on income tax *rates* is separately rated "Unclear or disputed" on the tracker's front page. The same manifesto sentence yields two different verdicts depending on which words are treated as the promise. This is the kind of judgement a tracker has to make and a site would have to own.
  - "13,000 additional police officers, PCSOs and special constables into neighbourhood policing roles" — **Appears on track** (23 Jun 2026), with the caveat that overall officer and PCSO numbers fell over the same period, so the rise came from reallocation. Source: [Full Fact, 13,000 neighbourhood police](https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/13000-neighbourhood-police/). The number is a fact; "on track" is a forecast.
  - "Labour will cap corporation tax at ... 25%" — **Appears on track** (6 Mar 2026), citing the Corporate Tax Roadmap and Finance Act 2025. Source: [Full Fact, corporation tax cap](https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/corporation-tax-cap/).
- **Note on context in the pages.** Full Fact's pages read on 24 September 2026 state that Sir Keir Starmer announced on 22 June 2026 that he would stand down and that Andy Burnham is now Prime Minister and has said he is "going to work to the 2024 manifesto". Sources: [Full Fact, first steps verdict, 26 Jun 2026 (updated 27 Jul 2026)](https://fullfact.org/politics/keir-starmer-resignation-manifesto-pledges-government-tracker/) and the [tracker front page](https://fullfact.org/government-tracker/). I report this as what Full Fact's pages say; I did not check it against a second source.
- **Criticism.** I found no published attack on Full Fact's tracker from a party or campaign in the sources I could reach (see could-not-verify list). The generic criticisms of promise trackers are in 2d.

#### 2b. Academic pledge-fulfilment studies (Comparative Party Pledges Project)

- **The definition.** "We define a pledge as a statement committing a party to one specific action or outcome that can be clearly determined to have occurred or not." Statements such as "we will ensure that our government shows respect for families" are "rhetoric, not pledges". Source: Thomson, Royed, Naurin, Artés, Costello, Ennser-Jedenastik, Ferguson, Kostadinova, Moury, Pétry and Praprotnik, "The Fulfillment of Parties' Election Pledges: A Comparative Study on the Impact of Power Sharing", *American Journal of Political Science* 61(3), 2017, pp. 527–542, [author manuscript at Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/59403/1/Thomson_etal_AJPS_2016_The_fulfillment_of_parties_election_pledges.pdf).
- **The categories.** Three: fully fulfilled, partially fulfilled, not fulfilled. Two countries (Italy, Spain) used only "not fulfilled" and "at least partially fulfilled", so the pooled analysis uses that two-way split. Same source.
- **Who decides and how reliable.** Trained researchers code each pledge. On *identifying* pledges, nine coders working on one Canadian manifesto reached "an average agreement rate of 93 percent". Same source. I did not find a published agreement figure for the *fulfilment* judgement in this paper.
- **Scale and the UK figure.** 18,743 pledges, 54 elections, 12 countries. "The UK governing parties fulfilled 86 percent (494 of 575 pledges) at least partially" — but the five UK governments in the dataset are 1974–79 Labour and the 1979–97 Conservative governments. Nothing after 1997 is in this dataset. Same source.
- **The book.** Naurin, Royed and Thomson (eds), *Party Mandates and Democracy: Making, Breaking, and Keeping Election Pledges in Twelve Countries*, University of Michigan Press, 2019, open access (CC BY-NC 4.0). Source: [Fulcrum record](https://www.fulcrum.org/concern/monographs/vx021g52t).
- **How often updated.** Never, in the tracker sense: these are one-off retrospective codings done after a government ends. They are a method to borrow, not a feed to plug in.
- **What this means.** The academic method is the strictest published standard for what counts as a pledge and it still ends in a coder's judgement of "partially". Its value to the site is the definition (testable action or outcome), which would cut roughly two-thirds of manifesto sentences before any matching starts.

#### 2c. House of Commons Library and Institute for Government

- **Commons Library.** The Library publishes "progress of legislation" briefings after each King's Speech. The 2024 one tracks the 35 Bills announced on 17 July 2024: "30 received Royal Assent, four were carried over to the next session and one was not introduced"; across the whole session the government introduced 63 Bills, of which 54 received Royal Assent. It is a table of introduction date, House and Royal Assent date. It "does not link bills to manifesto commitments" and is now "no longer being updated" (last update 15 July 2026). Source: [House of Commons Library, "2024 King's Speech: progress of legislation", CBP-10314](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10314/). The 2026 equivalent, published 11 June 2026 for the King's Speech of 13 May 2026, uses the same format and also does not reference manifesto commitments. Source: [CBP-10893](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10893/). I did not find any Library briefing that rates manifesto pledges as delivered or not; the Library's main search page blocked automated reading (robots.txt), so this is "not found", not "does not exist".
- **Institute for Government.** IfG's *Whitehall Monitor 2026* (13 January 2026) "is not designed to assess manifesto delivery"; it measures the civil service. Source: [IfG, Whitehall Monitor 2026 overview](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/whitehall-monitor-2026/overview). A search for an IfG manifesto tracker returned a 403 from the IfG site and no such page elsewhere. Unverified whether IfG runs one.
- **Others found.** [Pledge Progress](https://pledgeprogress.co.uk/) describes itself as "an independent hobby website unaffiliated with any political party", tracks 193 Labour 2024 pledges with statuses Kept, Compromised, Broken, In Progress, No Evidence, and shows no methodology or funding statement on its front page. Baker McKenzie (a law firm) publishes a commercial "UK Government Policy Implementation Tracker" ([August 2025](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2025/08/uk-government-policy-implementation-tracker)); I did not read it in full.

#### 2d. What critics say about promise trackers in general

- A February 2026 study of promise trackers (RMIT ABC in Australia, with reference to PolitiFact's Obameter, Canada's Polimeter and Full Fact) reports the tracker's own staff saying "there's nothing scientific" about which promises are selected, that selection covered 66 of 338 identified promises, and that political scientists have criticised journalist-led trackers as "less transparent regarding their definitions and methodology". It also records a government contacting the tracker behind the scenes to ask for status changes. Source: Waller, Morieson and Thomas, "Understanding election promise tracking as a form of fact-checking", *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, 2 Feb 2026, [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-06603-7).
- Trackers run by governments about themselves are attacked as self-grading. The Canadian government's mandate tracker was called "a self-focused, self-issued, self-completed report card" using phrases like "underway, with challenges" instead of "broken". Source: Robyn Urback, CBC News, 15 Nov 2017, [link](https://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.4401842).
- The recurring attack lines, then, are: which promises were chosen; who decided the status; whether "in progress" hides "not done"; and whether the tracker treats a changed promise as broken. Full Fact's FAQ answers each of these in advance in writing, which is the defence that has held so far.

### 3. Computed measures of a member's record (no pledge needed)

What Parliament's own APIs return, opened on 24 September 2026:

| Measure | Where it comes from | Fact or score? |
|---|---|---|
| How the member voted in each division | [Commons Votes API](https://commonsvotes-api.parliament.uk/) `division/{id}.json` returns `Ayes`, `Noes`, `NoVoteRecorded` lists with each member's `Party`; `divisions.json/membervoting?queryParameters.memberId=` returns per-member `MemberVotedAye`/`MemberVotedNo`/`MemberWasTeller`; [Members API](https://members-api.parliament.uk/) `/api/Members/{id}/Voting?house=1` returns `inAffirmativeLobby`/`inNegativeLobby`/`actedAsTeller` (896 items for member 4514 today). | Fact per division. |
| Party split on each division | Commons Votes API `divisions.json/groupedbyparty` returns `Ayes` and `Noes` as `{PartyName, VoteCount}` lists. | Fact per division. This is what the site's Real-O-Mat rules already use. |
| "Did not vote" | The `NoVoteRecorded` list (94 members on division 1899, for example). The API records no reason. The `ProxyName` field shows when a proxy voted. | Fact, with the reason unknown. The site's wording "did not vote (reason not recorded)" matches what the API knows. |
| Agreement with own party | Not a field. It must be computed: for each division, compare the member's lobby with the lobby holding the majority of their party's votes. | A score the moment it is summarised. Public Whip: "there is no true way of identifying a 'rebellion'" without whip data, which parties do not publish; free votes cannot be told apart from whipped ones. Source: [Public Whip FAQ](https://www.publicwhip.org.uk/faq.php). |
| Rebellions | Parliament's own data has no rebellion field (checked: Commons Votes swagger lists only `division`, `groupedbyparty`, `membervoting`, `search`, `searchTotalResults`; Members API has no whip endpoint). "Rebellion" is always a third party's computation against an inferred whip. | Score. |
| Attendance at divisions | Not a field. Computed as divisions voted ÷ divisions held while a member. | Score. Public Whip notes low figures can reflect abstention, ministerial duty, the Speaker's role or deliberate selective voting (same FAQ). Sinn Féin MPs "cannot sit in the chamber or vote in divisions" because they do not take the oath, so any attendance figure for them is 0% by rule, not choice. Source: [Commons Library, "What is the Parliamentary Oath?", 21 Sep 2026](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/what-is-the-parliamentary-oath/). |
| Written questions asked | Members API `/api/Members/{id}/WrittenQuestions` (73 for member 4514). | Count is a fact; as a comparison it is a score, and ministers do not ask written questions of their own government, so the count measures role as much as effort. |
| EDMs signed | Members API `/api/Members/{id}/Edms` (31 for member 4514). | Count is a fact. "Ministers, Whips and Parliamentary Private Secretaries do not normally sign EDMs" and "Neither the Speaker nor Deputy Speakers will sign EDMs". Source: [UK Parliament, Early Day Motions](https://www.parliament.uk/about/how/business/edms/). So a low count marks a job, not inactivity. |
| Speeches / contributions | Members API `/api/Members/{id}/ContributionSummary` (478 debates for member 4514, with per-debate `speechCount`, `questionCount`, `interventionCount`, `answerCount`); [Hansard API](https://hansard-api.parliament.uk/) `search/contributions/Spoken.json?queryParameters.memberId=` (5,637 spoken results for member 4514). | Count is a fact; comparison is a score. |
| Bills sponsored | [Bills API](https://bills-api.parliament.uk/) `Bills/{id}` returns `sponsors` (member or organisation) and `billTypeId` (1 = Government Bill; 5, 7, 8 = Private Members' Bills by route; 2 = PMB starting in the Lords). | Fact per Bill. |

**What the practitioners did with these.** TheyWorkForYou removed its "numerology" section (speeches per year, written answers, attendance at votes, "above/below average" comparisons) in July 2020 because "disclaimers ... we don't think they are really read by users" and because MPs' roles differ. Source: Alex Parsons, mySociety, "Publishing less: our current thinking about comparative statistics", 16 Nov 2020, [link](https://www.mysociety.org/2020/11/16/publishing-less-our-current-thinking-about-comparative-statistics/). TheyWorkForYou's voting summaries still exist but "we do not count absences because we cannot be clear on how they should be interpreted", and the summaries turn a 0–100 alignment number into phrases ("consistently voted for" = 0–5, and so on). Source: [TheyWorkForYou, Voting information](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/voting-information/). Its API offers `getMP`, `getMPInfo`, `getDebates`, `getWrans`, `getWMS`, `getHansard` and constituency lookups; there is no `getDivisions` method listed. Source: [TheyWorkForYou API docs](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/api/docs).

**Where the line is.** A per-division fact (this member was in this lobby; this party split this way; this member has no vote recorded) is what Parliament publishes. The moment two or more of them are added up into one number for a person — agreement %, attendance %, rebellions, questions per year — the number depends on choices (what counts as the party line, which divisions count, whether tellers count, whether free votes are excluded) that Parliament does not make and does not publish. That is the point at which a fact becomes a score, and the site's rule against ranking or scoring bites.

**Honest counts.** 25 commitments checked. 18 have a Royal Assent date in the API (rows 1–8, 9a, 14–20, 22, 23). 3 are government Bills still in Parliament (9b, 10, 13). 4 have no Bill in the API (11, 12, 21, 25). 1 is not a legislative pledge (24). Of the 18 Acts, 9 are matches where the manifesto names the Bill, Act, company or body itself (3, 8, 14, 15, 17, 18 and, by name of the law, 1, 16 and 19 are close), and the rest depend on my judgement that the Bill is the vehicle for the promise, marked (J). Someone else could make a different match on 5, 6 and 22.

**What this method shows and what it cannot.** It shows, as fact, that a Bill exists, who sponsored it, which stage it is at and when it got Royal Assent. It cannot show: that the Act does what the promise said (row 6); that the Act is in force (row 1: Royal Assent October 2025, in force May 2026); that a promise without a Bill has been dropped (rows 11, 12, 21 — Full Fact rates two of these "In progress" on the strength of draft Bills the API does not return); or anything at all about a promise delivered by spending, regulation or administration (most of the manifesto). The Commons Library uses exactly this method for the King's Speech, and states it does not connect Bills to manifestos (2c).

**No-data case.** Opposition parties, minor parties and independents cannot introduce government Bills. The API would show, at most, Private Members' Bills sponsored by their MPs (`billTypeId` 2, 5, 7 or 8), nearly all of which stop at second reading (see the Water Bill, id 3777, a Ballot Bill sponsored by Clive Lewis, at "2nd reading" since 2024). So a Bills-API panel for a Conservative, Liberal Democrat, Green or Reform UK manifesto would be empty by construction. The wording that keeps this honest is something like: "This party was not in government in this Parliament, so none of its manifesto promises could be put into a government Bill. That is not a record of anything not done."

### 5. Money tracking: pledges with a number against what was enacted

**Method.** Where a manifesto states a rate, threshold, date or sum, compare it with the HM Treasury Budget document and the OBR's costing. The OBR states that it "certified all policy measures in the forecast as reasonable and central" and publishes a policy measures table with each measure's yield. Source: [OBR, Economic and Fiscal Outlook, October 2024](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2024/).

**2024 Labour pledges checkable this way** (manifesto wording from the PDF; enacted figures from [HM Treasury, Autumn Budget 2024, 30 Oct 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/autumn-budget-2024/autumn-budget-2024-html) unless stated):

| Pledge (verbatim) | Number in the pledge | What the Budget/OBR document says | Fact or judgement? |
|---|---|---|---|
| "we will not increase National Insurance, the basic, higher, or additional rates of Income Tax, or VAT" | rates unchanged | Employer NICs "increase the rate ... by 1.2 percentage points to 15%. The per-employee threshold ... reduced from £9,100 per year to £5,000 per year ... from 6 April 2025". OBR: "£25.7 billion by 2029-30". Employee rates, income tax rates and VAT rate not changed in that Budget. | The numbers are fact. Whether employer NICs is "National Insurance" within the pledge is a judgement; Full Fact split the sentence into separate pledges and rated the headline "Not kept" and the income-tax-rates part "Unclear or disputed" (2a). |
| "Labour will cap corporation tax at the current level of 25 per cent ... for the entire parliament" | 25% | "Corporate Tax Roadmap, which confirms that the government will cap the rate of Corporation Tax at 25% ... for the duration of the Parliament." | Fact so far; "for the entire parliament" can only be settled at the end. |

Three numbers, three matches. Whether 31 March 2030 is "the end of the next parliament" depends on the election date. |

Numbers not checkable against a Treasury document because they are outputs, not budget lines: 40,000 appointments a week, 6,500 teachers, 13,000 neighbourhood police, 1.5 million homes, 8,500 mental health staff, 3,000 nurseries, 700,000 dental appointments. Each has an official statistical series (NHS England, DfE, Home Office, MHCLG) and each needs a baseline and a counting rule that the manifesto did not give; Full Fact's 13,000-police page shows what deciding those rules looks like (2a).

**No-data case.** Opposition manifestos also carry numbers (a tax rate they would set, a sum they would spend). There is no Treasury document enacting them, so the only honest display is the pledge and its own costing, with the note that the party was not in a position to enact it. Where a party published no costings at all, that is a fact about the manifesto and can be stated as such.

**Bias risk.** Choosing which number to show. A page that shows the employer NICs rise next to the "no increase in National Insurance" sentence, and nothing else, will be read as a verdict, whichever way it is meant. Showing the whole sentence, the whole Budget paragraph and the OBR line, dated, is the defence; choosing the fragment is the attack surface.

### 6. Machine-assisted pairing: a model proposes, a person confirms

Published attempts and what they report:

- **Identifying pledges in manifestos** (the first step, before any matching). Fornaciari, Runeson, Hovy, Thomson, Naurin and Adhikari, "'We will Reduce Taxes' – Identifying Election Pledges with Language Models", *Findings of ACL 2021*: BERT-type models on 12,827 manifesto sentences (Swedish and Indian parties; 27.5% were pledges) reached F1 of 87.01 on Swedish and 76.31 on Indian text; adding surrounding context *reduced* performance by "up to 10 points in F1". Source: [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-acl.301.pdf). No UK data.
- **Tracking fulfilment events** (the closest thing to what Romily asked for). PledgeTracker (Chen, Deng, Vlachos at Cambridge; Schlichtkrull at QMUL; Corney, Asl, Salisbury, Dudfield at Full Fact), September 2025: a system that searches the web, builds a dated timeline of events for a pledge, and filters to events that "contribute to the fulfilment of the pledge", built on 50 pledges from Labour's 2024 manifesto with 1,559 annotated events. With Full Fact's fact checkers judging 513 events, it reached precision 0.764, recall 0.553, F1 0.641. The best model on the offline filtering task scored F1 0.633. Source: [arXiv 2509.11804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.11804). In plain terms: about one in four events the system offers is wrong, and it misses nearly half of the relevant ones, so it is built as a tool for a person, not a publisher.
- **Pledge-to-vote matching by machine.** I found no published UK study that matches manifesto pledges to Commons divisions automatically and reports an error rate. The academic work matches pledges to *outcomes* (with coders), not to votes.

**What the design would need.** A published pair would carry three things: the model's confidence (a number), the person's decision (confirmed / rejected / ambiguous), and the person's one-line reason. That is fact about the process, not about the MP. The bias risk moves from the pairing to the model's training and the confirmer's consistency, and the only defence is publishing both. Note that the PledgeTracker precision of 0.76 was with professional fact checkers doing the confirming and Full Fact's name on the paper; a smaller team would need its own agreement figures before publishing.

### 7. The no-data case, method by method (Romily's explicit requirement)

The rule she set: state clearly why some parties or MPs have no data, so it reads as absence of data, not absence of action. What each method should say when empty:

| Method | Who has no data | What is true, and can be said | What must not be implied |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pledge-to-vote pairing | All parties (5 clean pairs in 50 pledges); all opposition parties in practice | "No Commons vote has been held on this promise" (it is a fact about Parliament's agenda) | that the party avoided a vote or that the promise was dropped |
| Human outcome tracker | Every party not in government | "Only a governing party can deliver a manifesto, so only Labour's 2024 manifesto has a delivery record in this Parliament" (Full Fact's own position: "if ... there's a change of government, we would look at another party's record in exactly the same way") | that other parties have "kept 0 promises" |
| Computed record | New candidates; MPs who took office mid-Parliament; Sinn Féin MPs (cannot vote by rule); the Speaker and Deputies; ministers (EDMs, written questions) | "Not an MP in this Parliament — no voting record exists", "Took seat on [date] — divisions before then not applicable", "Sinn Féin MPs do not take the oath and cannot vote in divisions" | any figure at all, including 0% |
| Bills API | Every party not in government | "This party was not in government, so it could not introduce a government Bill" | that a promise with no Bill was broken (rows 11, 12, 21: Full Fact rates these "In progress"/"Wait and see", not "Not kept") |
| Money tracking | Opposition parties; any pledge without a number; any pledge whose number is an output not a budget line | "No Budget enacted this party's figures; the party's own costing was £X" | that an uncosted pledge is a broken one |
| Machine-assisted pairing | Same as pairing; plus any pledge the model scored below threshold | "No pair proposed" with the threshold published | that the model "found nothing wrong" |

The common thread in every published source read for this paper is the same sentence in different words: TheyWorkForYou "cannot be clear on how [absences] should be interpreted"; Public Whip "there is no true way of identifying a 'rebellion'"; Full Fact "unable to offer a verdict, usually because either key data is unavailable". Each of them names the gap rather than filling it.

## What this means for the site

These are observations, not a recommendation.

- The current page (manifesto quotes verbatim; divisions on Real-O-Mat rules; "did not vote (reason not recorded)") already sits at the fact end of every row above. Parliament's APIs return exactly the per-division facts the site shows and nothing that would let it show more without computing.
- Two methods can be added without any judgement at the point of display: (a) for a manifesto sentence that names a Bill, Act, company or body, the Bills API's stage list and Royal Assent date; (b) for a manifesto sentence with a rate, threshold, date or sum, the Treasury paragraph and OBR line, quoted and dated. Both are empty for every non-governing party, and both need the empty state written in words before the first page ships.
- Every other method needs a person to decide something, and every published body that does it says so in its own methodology text. If the site ever does that, the published sources suggest the defence is the same three things Full Fact publishes: which pledges were chosen and why, who decided each status, and a dated reason with sources on every one.
- Full Fact's verdicts can be quoted as a labelled third-party source ("Full Fact rated this 'Appears on track' on 23 June 2026") without the site adopting them. That is a cited fact about Full Fact.
- Romily's "look at MP's policies and see what they followed through" maps, for a governing-party MP, onto method 4 plus method 5 (the party's Bills and Budgets, which the MP's party set up) and, for the MP personally, onto Bills they sponsored (a fact from the Bills API). For any other MP it maps onto nothing yet, and the honest page says why.
- The PledgeTracker figures (0.76 precision with professional checkers) are the best available estimate of what a model-plus-person pipeline achieves on this exact manifesto. Anyone building one would be publishing a tool whose one-in-four error rate is known in advance.
